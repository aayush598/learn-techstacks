# Coding Patterns — Two Pointers Interview Questions and Answers

## Q1: What is the two-pointer pattern?
**A:** Two indices scanning the array — often from both ends or at different speeds — with a monotonic decision, achieving O(n) on sorted inputs.

## Q2: When is two pointers applicable?
**A:** Sorted arrays (pair sums, removing duplicates), palindrome checks, reversing in place, and merging sorted sequences — anywhere a monotone decision lets you skip comparisons.

## Q3: What is classic two-sum with sorted input?
**A:** l=0, r=n-1; if sum < target move l right, if > move r left — O(n), versus the hash-map O(n) that also works unsorted; state both approaches.

## Q4: What is the key invariant of the two-pointer scan?
**A:** Once you move past a position, you never need to revisit it because the ordering makes the current choice irrelevant — the proof that skipping is safe.

## Q5: When must input be sorted for this pattern?
**A:** For pair/monotone problems such as two-sum and container-with-most-water, sortedness (or a known ordering) justifies moving one pointer — without it, the pattern may discard valid answers.

## Q6: How does two pointers remove duplicates in-place?
**A:** Slow pointer writes the next unique element; fast pointer scans; when fast value != previous, advance slow and write — O(n) time, O(1) space.

## Q7: What is the three-pointer / Dutch national flag problem?
**A:** Three-way partition (0s,1s,2s) with low/mid/high pointers — scanning once to sort in place; a direct extension when one comparison isn't enough.

## Q8: What is the fast-and-slow relationship?
**A:** Fast-and-slow is a two-pointer variant: pointers move at different speeds (cycle detection, middle-of-linked-list) rather than from the ends.

## Q9: What is the complexity and when does it degrade?
**A:** O(n) time, O(1) space when sorted; degrades only if you slip into nested scans or the input isn't actually monotone — justify sortedness explicitly.

## Q10: Give a real-world analogy.
**A:** Two scouts walking from opposite ends of a sorted line and meeting in the middle, discarding the region that provably cannot contain the target — each step eliminates a range.
