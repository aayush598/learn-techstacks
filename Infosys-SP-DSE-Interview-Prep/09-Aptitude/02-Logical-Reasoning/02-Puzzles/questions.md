1. **What is the approach to logical puzzles?**
   - Convert statements into a structured grid/table (possessions, order, relationships), then apply elimination via definite facts — most puzzles fall to systematic constraint propagation.

2. **How do you solve 'who lives in which house' (Einstein-like) puzzles?**
   - Build a table dimensioned by categories, fill definite placements, and chain implications; keep 'either/or' rows until a contradiction eliminates them.

3. **There are 3 boxes: one has gold, one silver, one both. Labels are all wrong. You open one box. How do you find all?**
   - All labels wrong → open the box labelled 'both': it is silver or gold. If silver, the box labelled 'gold' must be 'both' (since it can't be gold) and 'silver' is gold — the classic wrong-label logic.

4. **A ferry can hold 2; a wolf, goat, and cabbage need safe crossing. How?**
   - Take goat over; return; take wolf; bring goat back; take cabbage; return; take goat — the state-space solution to the classic crossing problem.

5. **How do you solve a scheduling/order puzzle (A sits left of B)?**
   - Translate each clue into inequalities/adjacency marks; build the sequence by fixing the most constrained person first; backtrack on contradictions.

6. **What is the difference between deductive and inductive puzzle solving?**
   - Deductively you PROVE with facts (all clues are certain); inductively you hypothesise and test. In puzzles the clues are usually deductive constraints.

7. **How do you verify a puzzle solution?**
   - Check every clue against the final solution explicitly — each statement should be satisfied; one violated clue invalidates the answer.

8. **What is the role of 'at least/at most' phrasing in puzzles?**
   - 'At most one' permits zero; 'exactly one' fixes one; 'at least one' forbids zero — subtle quantifiers change the constraint sets entirely.
