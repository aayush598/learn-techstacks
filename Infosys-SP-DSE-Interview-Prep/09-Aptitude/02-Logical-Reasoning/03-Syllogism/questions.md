1. **What is a syllogism?**
   - A deductive argument with two premises (with a common 'middle' term) and a conclusion; classic form: All/most/some statements with a quantifier structure.

2. **What are the four basic statement types?**
   - All A are B (universal affirmative); No A are B (universal negative); Some A are B (particular affirmative); Some A are not B (particular negative) — shown in Venn/Euler diagrams.

3. **What conclusions follow from 'All A are B' and 'All B are C'?**
   - All A are C (valid); also Some A are B follows from All A are B if A is non-empty — distinguish guaranteed vs possible conclusions.

4. **What is the difference between a definite and a possible conclusion?**
   - A definite conclusion holds in every Venn diagram consistent with the premises; a 'possible' conclusion holds in at least one — syllogism tests accept only definite ones.

5. **Why do candidates over-infer?**
   - People import assumptions beyond the premises (e.g., assuming existence or distribution). In syllogisms, use ONLY the premises — the classic 'fanatic of the given' trap.

6. **What is the 'No A are B → No B are A' conversion rule?**
   - "No" statements are symmetric (conversion is valid); "All A are B" does NOT imply "All B are A"; "Some" statements convert only if the terms are existential.

7. **How do Venn diagrams help?**
   - Draw overlapping circles for the three terms; place entities from premises; the conclusion must be true in every consistent placement — diagramming beats mental juggling.

8. **Give an example where a tempting conclusion is invalid.**
   - All dogs are animals; all cats are animals => 'all dogs are cats' is invalid (the common term 'animals' is the shared class, not a subset relation) — the classic trap.
