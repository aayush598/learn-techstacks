# Vocabulary Elimination Technique & Idioms

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Vocabulary, Synonyms & Antonyms ·  _Estimated reading time: 7 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [Synonyms, antonyms & one-word substitutions](../01-concepts/02-synonyms-antonyms-one-word-substitutions.md)

## In one sentence
**A vocabulary question is solved in four moves — read the sentence, name the part of speech, eliminate on class, then test sense in context — and the whole thing fits in 40 seconds.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- You will not remember every dictionary definition on the day. But you will remember a **procedure**, and the
  procedure is enough to get 2 of 4 options out of the way instantly.
- The most common lost marks in this sub-type come from picking a **true but irrelevant** synonym — a word that
  means the same thing in the abstract but not in this sentence.
- The idiom rule is short enough to memorise and saves 1–2 marks per paper.

## Core concept

### The 4-move technique

```
MOVE 1 (15s)  READ THE SENTENCE. What is actually happening? What is the target
               word doing — describing, naming, negating, intensifying?
MOVE 2 (5s)   NAME THE PART OF SPEECH required in the blank. Adjective? Verb?
               Noun? Adverb? Two options die here instantly.
MOVE 3 (15s)  TEST SENSE IN CONTEXT. Substitute each surviving option into the
               sentence and read it aloud. Does it still make sense, in this meaning?
MOVE 4 (5s)   If two survive: pick the more common word, or the one that matches
               the register. Mark and move at 40s.
```

### Move 3 — the substitution test

This is the core skill. **Do not compare the option to the target word; compare the option to the sentence.**

> "The team worked with ______ coordination to deliver the release on time."
> A. immaculate · B. meticulous · C. expensive · D. rapid

Substitute each:
- "worked with immaculate coordination" — grammar is off (immaculate is not normally used for coordination) ❌
- "worked with meticulous coordination" — makes sense, means careful, detailed ✅
- "worked with expensive coordination" — odd ❌
- "worked with rapid coordination" — grammatical but means *fast*, which contradicts "to deliver on time" being
  the point of the sentence ❌

**Answer: B.** Note that "immaculate" means *perfectly clean* and was eliminated by meaning, not by a grammar rule.

### The 4 vocabulary traps and their counters

| Trap | Shape | Counter |
|------|-------|---------|
| **1. Wrong part of speech** | An adjective offered for a noun slot | Name the part of speech first |
| **2. Right word, wrong sense** | A polysemous word's other meaning | Substitute into the sentence and read it aloud |
| **3. Milder opposite / near-opposite** | "to lessen" offered as the opposite of "to annihilate" | Ask: is the option the **full** opposite? |
| **4. Register mismatch** | "liquidate" for "cancel the order" | Ask: would you say this to a client? |

### The polysemous word list to watch

These words carry two common meanings and appear in vocabulary items as distractors:

| Word | Sense 1 | Sense 2 |
|------|---------|---------|
| **effective** | producing the intended result | in effect / actually |
| **issue** | a problem | a topic; a printed edition |
| **present** | current, at hand | a gift; to attend |
| **sound** | healthy, solid | noise; to play; deep |
| **light** | not heavy; pale | not serious; lamp |
| **cold** | low temperature | unfriendly; not hot |
| **critical** | crucial; of a crisis | of the critical approach |
| **objective** | a goal; impartial | a lens/point in a case |
| **subject** | a topic; a citizen | exposed to; a course of study |
| **state** | a condition; a nation | to say |
| **run** | manage; flow | a streak; a production |
| **sick** | ill | of something nauseating; morbid |
| **hard** | difficult; solid | of a drug |
| **fine** | of high quality | a monetary penalty; thin |

**Rule:** if the target word is in this list, check the sentence for which sense it uses before you read the
options.

### The 4 checks for an ANTONYM question

```
1. Is the option a SYNONYM of the target offered as an antonym?   → eliminate
2. Is the option a MILDER version of the target?                 → usually eliminate
3. Is it the opposite of the OPPOSITE (i.e. the target)?          → eliminate
4. Does it hold in the SAME register and part of speech?          → must hold
```

**Worked mini-example:** target = **SCEPTICAL**.
- "cynical" — distrustful of motives, not doubting a fact. It is a *near*-opposite, and cynical can even mean
  "realistically pessimistic", which is closer to the target. ❌
- "doubtful" — a **synonym** ❌
- "uncertain" — a **milder** version ❌
- "credulous" — too ready to believe. The true opposite ✅

### The 3 checks for an IDIOM question

```
1. What is the LITERAL meaning? (the answer is always here)
2. Does the option match the literal meaning?
3. Does the option match the register of the sentence?
```

| Idiom | ❌ Figurative distractor | ✅ Literal answer |
|-------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| "to spill the beans" | to destroy the beans / to spill coffee | to reveal a secret |
| "to hit the nail on the head" | to injure the head | to say exactly the right thing |
| "to burn the midnight oil" | to waste oil | to work late into the night |
| "to beat about the bush" | to search for a plant | to avoid the main point |
| "to take to one's heels" | to wear new shoes | to run away |

### When you simply do not know the word

Do not blank. Use the fallback ladder:

```
1. Eliminate on part of speech.                          (5s)
2. Eliminate on the prefix/suffix meaning you can infer:
   -un- / in- / im- / dis- / non- = not      -re- = again      -pre- = before
   -over- = too much    -under- = too little   -anti- = against  -co- = together
3. Eliminate on length and register: a very long, very technical word is
   rarely the intended answer for a simple sentence.
4. Among the survivors, pick the one whose meaning fits the context best.
```

⚠️ Step 2 is worth 1 mark in maybe 1 item per paper. Step 3 is worth more.

## Worked example

> **Q.** Choose the word most nearly opposite in meaning to **TRANSPARENT** in the sentence: "The manager
> maintained a transparent process for approving leave requests."
>
> A. opaque
> B. ambiguous
> C. concealed
> D. unclear

**Answer: A — opaque.**

| Check | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| 1 Synonym offered as antonym? | "unclear" is not an antonym of "transparent" in the sense of visibility, but in this context "unclear" is close to a synonym of "opaque" ❌ |
| 2 Milder version? | "unclear" is milder than the true opposite ❌ · "ambiguous" is about meaning, not the opposite of transparent ❌ |
| 3 Opposite of the opposite? | "concealed" is a **verb** — wrong part of speech for the slot, and it means "hidden", which is close to opaque but not the antonym ❌ |
| 4 Register + part of speech? | "opaque" is an adjective, matches the adjective slot, and is the standard antonym of "transparent" ✅ |

**Why "concealed" is the dangerous option:** it is the most *dramatically* opposite-sounding option, and it
describes hiding. But "transparent" in this sentence means *open and easy to understand*, and its antonym is
"opaque" — hard to see through, hard to understand. The register and part-of-speech check kills "concealed".

## Quick rules

- Read the **sentence** first, always. The option is judged in context.
- Name the part of speech second. That kills two options in 5 seconds.
- Substitute and read aloud. Your ear catches what your memory misses.
- For antonyms: reject synonyms, milder versions, and opposite-of-the-opposite.
- For idioms: literal meaning only.
- Polysemous words: check which sense the sentence uses.
- Fallback ladder: word class → prefix/suffix → length/register → best fit.
- 40 seconds. Then move.

## Common traps

- **Comparing the option to the target word** instead of to the sentence.
- **Choosing the most dramatic opposite.** "Concealed" beat "opaque" in the example — and it was wrong.
- **Choosing a technical or archaic word** for a simple sentence.
- **Ignoring the part of speech.** A verb cannot replace an adjective.
- **Spending 90 seconds on a vocabulary item** because the word is unfamiliar. Two items like that cost you more
  than they are worth.

## Checklist

- [ ] I read the sentence before reading the options.
- [ ] I named the part of speech of the blank.
- [ ] I substituted each surviving option into the sentence.
- [ ] For an antonym item, I rejected synonyms, milder versions, and opposite-of-the-opposite.
- [ ] I moved on at 40 seconds.
