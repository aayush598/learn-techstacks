# Solved Passages

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Reading Comprehension & Fill Blanks ·  _Estimated reading time: 10 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [Solving a passage in 90 seconds](../02-techniques/01-solving-a-passage-in-90-seconds.md)

## In one sentence
**Three complete passages solved end to end — a workplace passage, a general-knowledge passage, and a paragraph-completion cloze — with every option judged against the passage.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Each passage below is structured like a real paper item: 100–150 words, three paragraphs, questions of
  different types.
- The **evidence line** is identified for every answer, so you can see the habit, not just the verdict.

## Core concept

Label scheme used below: **P1** context, **P2** development/contrast, **P3** conclusion.

## Worked example

### Passage 1 — Workplace

> "When a project misses its deadline, the instinct is to explain why the delay was unavoidable. Experienced
> delivery managers do something different: they separate the cause of the delay from the decision about what to
> do next. The cause is often uninteresting — a dependency was late, a requirement was unclear, a test
> environment was unavailable. The decision is where the value is. A team that reports a delay with two options
> and a recommendation has converted a problem into a decision, and a decision can be made quickly. A team that
> reports only the cause has handed its manager a problem and asked someone else to think. The habit that
> separates the two is simple: every status report ends with a question and a recommendation, never with a
> reason alone."

*Labels: P1 → "separate cause from decision" · P2 → "a recommendation converts a problem into a decision" · P3 →
"end every report with a question and a recommendation"*

> **Q1.** What is the passage mainly about?
> A. How to write clear status reports during a delay
> B. Why delivery managers are busier than development teams
> C. The most common causes of missed project deadlines
> D. How to identify a delayed dependency early

**Answer: A.** It covers P1 (separate cause from decision), P2 (a recommendation turns the problem into a
decision), and P3 (the concrete habit in status reports). ❌B is never discussed. ❌C is only P2's aside. ❌D is only
the "delay" idea, not the passage's argument.

> **Q2.** According to the passage, what does a team that reports "only the cause" do?
> A. Delays the decision even further
> B. Passes the thinking to someone else
> C. Breaks the reporting habit
> D. Loses the trust of the client

**Answer: B** — "has handed its manager a problem and asked someone else to think" ✅. ❌A is implied at most; the
passage's point is the transfer of thinking, not the delay. ❌C and ❌D are never stated.

> **Q3.** It can be inferred that the author would most likely recommend that a team report:
> A. the client's reaction before the internal cause
> B. the cause in detail and the decision to be taken
> C. only the option the team prefers
> D. the delay in hours and the people involved

**Answer: B** — one step from "separates the cause … from the decision about what to do next" and "every status
report ends with a question and a recommendation" ✅. ❌A is not supported. ❌C contradicts the "two options" point.
❌D is not mentioned.

> **Q4.** Why does the author say "a decision can be made quickly"?
> A. To show that managers enjoy making decisions
> B. To contrast the speed of a decision with the slowness of an explanation
> C. To argue that deadlines are unrealistic
> D. To warn against rushing into decisions

**Answer: B** — it sets up the contrast with the next clause ("A team that reports only the cause has handed its
manager a problem and asked someone else to think") ✅. ❌A is not the author's view. ❌C and ❌D invert the
argument.

### Passage 2 — General knowledge with an inference

> "Sleep has traditionally been explained as a passive state in which the body simply repairs itself. That
> account has been undermined by the discovery of sleep cycles, during which the brain is measurably active and
> memory is consolidated. The practical consequence for organisations is easy to miss. A company that schedules
> training at 3 p.m. and considers that the employee's choice, is making a decision about cognitive performance
> rather than about convenience. Most people do not know that their own alertness varies across the day, and
> they cannot be expected to argue for better scheduling. It falls to the organisation to set the default
> sensibly, because the default is what most people will accept."

*Labels: P1 → "sleep is active, not passive" · P2 → "organisations control the default" · P3 → "the default
decides what most people accept"*

> **Q1.** The author mentions "sleep cycles" in order to:
> A. show that the traditional account of sleep is out of date
> B. prove that sleep length is more important than sleep quality
> C. suggest that most people sleep too little
> D. explain why shift work is dangerous

**Answer: A.** "That account has been undermined by the discovery of sleep cycles" — the sentence's job is to
undermine the traditional view ✅. ❌B, ❌C, and ❌D are never raised.

> **Q2.** According to the passage, why does the author say employees "cannot be expected to argue for better
> scheduling"?
> A. Because employees are not interested in training
> B. Because most people do not know their own alertness varies across the day
> C. Because managers do not listen to employees
> D. Because scheduling is fixed by the client

**Answer: B** — stated directly in the sentence before ✅. ❌A, ❌C, and ❌D have no support in the passage.

> **Q3.** It can be inferred that the author would regard a "3 p.m. training slot" as:
> A. a decision made purely for convenience
> B. an organisational decision with a cognitive cost
> C. a practice that only affects new employees
> D. a compromise that employees usually appreciate

**Answer: B** — "is making a decision about cognitive performance rather than about convenience" ✅, one step.
❌A inverts the passage. ❌C and ❌D are not supported.

> **Q4.** The author mentions "the default is what most people will accept" to suggest that:
> A. employees will protest if the default is poor
> B. employers should set defaults without consulting staff
> C. the organisation carries the responsibility for good scheduling
> D. most employees are satisfied with their current working hours

**Answer: C** — "It falls to the organisation to set the default sensibly" ✅, one step from the final sentence.
❌A is unsupported. ❌B overstates: the passage says the organisation should set the default sensibly, not that it
should ignore staff. ❌D is not supported.

### Passage 3 — Paragraph completion (cloze)

> "For a long time, project status meetings were treated as a formality: a short agenda, a series of
> colour-coded slides, and a closing decision to schedule the next one. This format survived because it served a
> purpose nobody had bothered to examine — it distributed information to everyone at once. Distributed
> information, however, is not the same as shared understanding. Two teams can read the same dashboard and
> disagree about what it means, and the disagreement only surfaces weeks later, when it is expensive. Reports
> from delivery organisations that replaced slide decks with short written summaries of decisions and risks
> suggest a different outcome: fewer surprises, and issues raised earlier, when they are still cheap to fix.
> The change was not a new tool. It was the decision to treat a status update as a communication rather than as
> a broadcast."

> **Q.** Choose the option that best completes the passage.
>
> A. The broadcast model was popular mainly because it was inexpensive to produce
> B. Status meetings have become more frequent since dashboards were introduced
> C. The most effective status updates are short written summaries rather than slide decks
> D. Most delivery organisations have stopped holding status meetings altogether

**Answer: C.**

| Check | Finding |
|-------|---------|
| Grammar | ✅ All four fit the blank grammatically — no help here, which is normal |
| Reference | C continues the entities already in the passage ("delivery organisations", "short written summaries", "slide decks") ✅. A introduces "inexpensive to produce", which the passage never discusses. B introduces "dashboards introduced" as if it were new — the passage says the opposite. D claims meetings stopped, which the passage never says |
| Topic | C stays on status-update format ✅. D shifts to the frequency of meetings, a different subject ❌ |
| Tone | C matches the analytical register ✅. A shifts into a casual causal claim ❌ |

**Why D is the most dangerous distractor:** it sounds like a punchy conclusion, and it uses the passage's own
vocabulary ("status meetings", "delivery organisations"). But the passage says the change was in the **format**,
not in **whether you meet**. A well-designed option-jump uses the passage's own words. That is why the reference
check matters more than the topic check.

## Quick rules

- Locate, then eliminate, then confirm.
- Main idea = P1 + P2 twist.
- Inference = exactly one step.
- Paragraph completion: reference and tone beat topic.
- An option that uses the passage's own vocabulary is not thereby right — it may be a deliberate topic-jump.

## Common traps

- **Choosing D in Passage 3** because it sounds conclusive and reuses the passage's words.
- Answering Q2 in Passage 2 with an option that is *plausible in real life* but not stated.
- Treating an inference option that reads "more extreme" than the passage as correct.

- [ ] I underlined the evidence line for every answer.
- [ ] I applied the correct question-type rule each time.
- [ ] I rejected every option that added a claim.
- [ ] I checked reference and tone on the paragraph-completion item.
