# Solving a Passage in 90 Seconds

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Reading Comprehension & Fill Blanks ·  _Estimated reading time: 7 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [The passage-mapping method](../01-concepts/01-passage-mapping-method.md)

## In one sentence
**A passage with its questions is a 4-minute job, not a 10-minute job — read once, label, locate, eliminate, confirm — and 90 seconds is the cap per question.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Section 2 is 22 minutes for 15 questions. If you spend 3 minutes on a passage, you lose 6 behavioural items.
- The passages are short on purpose. A 150-word passage can be read in 40 seconds by a practised reader.
- The failure mode is not slow reading; it is **reading the options twice**, and **re-reading the passage for every
  question**.

## Core concept

### The 4-phase procedure

```
PHASE 1 — ORIENT (60s)
  Read the passage ONCE, straight through. Do not stop at the questions.
  After each paragraph, write a 3-word label.
  Do not underline yet.

PHASE 2 — LOCATE (15s per question)
  Read the question. Find the line(s) that could answer it. Underline them.
  NOW read the options. (Not before.)

PHASE 3 — ELIMINATE (15s per question)
  For each option, ask: is this supported by the underlined lines?
  Cross out anything that adds, exaggerates, or comes from outside the passage.

PHASE 4 — CONFIRM (15s per question)
  Re-read the question type and apply its rule:
    main idea → does the whole passage support it?
    inference → exactly one step?
    detail → is it stated?
    vocabulary → the sense used here?
    intent → purpose, not content?
  Circle the answer and move on.
```

### Time budget

| Element | Budget |
|---------|--------|
| Reading a 150-word passage | 60s |
| Each attached question | 90s max |
| A passage with 3 questions | 5 min 30s total |
| A passage with 1 question | 2 min 30s total |

**Reserve 2 minutes at the end of Section 2** for re-checking flagged items. That is a better trade than 2 extra
minutes on one question.

### The 90-second per-question decision tree

```
Question read (15s)
   ↓
Is it a MAIN IDEA question?
 |- Yes → the answer must cover P1 topic + P2 twist. Test coverage, neutrality, proportion. (45s)
 +- No  ↓
Is it an INFERENCE question?
 |- Yes → one step only. Reject anything with "always/never/only/proves". (45s)
 +- No  ↓
Is it a "WHY does the author" question?
 |- Yes → find the sentence, name its job in the paragraph. (45s)
 +- No  ↓
Is it a DETAIL or VOCABULARY question?
 +- Yes → find the line, match the paraphrase, check the sense used. (40s)
```

### When you cannot find the answer line

| Situation | What it means | What to do |
|-----------|---------------|------------|
| No line supports any option | It is probably an **inference** question | Apply the one-step rule |
| Two options are supported | One is **too strong** or **too narrow** | Check absolutes and scope |
| The answer is clearly outside the passage | The option is wrong | Eliminate it even if it sounds sensible |
| The question is about a word | It is a **vocabulary-in-context** item | Read the sentence around the word, ignore other senses |

### The 4 marking habits

1. **Underline the evidence, not the option.** Underlining the answer text is useless later.
2. **Cross out eliminated options visibly.** It stops you re-reading them.
3. **Read the question stem with the question type in mind.** "Mainly about" and "according to" need different
   evidence.
4. **Never read a question's options before locating its line.** The single highest-value habit here.

### Self-check before you move on

- [ ] I can point to the line(s) that support my answer.
- [ ] My answer type matches the question type.
- [ ] I did not use outside knowledge.
- [ ] I checked the option for absolutes and for added claims.

## Worked example

> **Passage:**
>
> "Automation of routine testing has become standard practice in software delivery teams. Test scripts that once
> took engineers two days to write and maintain can now be generated and updated in under an hour, which allows
> teams to increase coverage rather than reduce it. The shift has not eliminated the need for human judgement:
> deciding which scenarios actually represent the business is precisely the work that tools cannot yet perform.
> Teams that have adopted automation report shorter release cycles, but only where the critical business paths
> have been identified first."
>
> *Labels: P1 → "automated testing is standard" · P2 → "but judgement still needed" · P3 → "identify critical paths
> first"*

> **Q1.** What is the main idea of the passage?
> A. Automated testing is replacing software engineers
> B. Automation speeds up testing but does not remove the need for human judgement
> C. Most companies have not yet adopted test automation
> D. Increasing test coverage is difficult without automation

**Answer: B.** It covers P1 (automation is standard and faster) **and** P2 (human judgement is still required) —
the whole passage. ❌A contradicts the passage and is an overreach. ❌C is contradicted by P1 ("standard
practice"). ❌D is contradicted by P2 ("allows teams to increase coverage").

> **Q2.** According to the passage, what must teams identify before they can expect shorter release cycles?
> A. The number of engineers on the team
> B. The critical business paths
> C. The tools used to generate test scripts
> D. The cost of maintaining test scripts

**Answer: B** — stated in the final sentence ("only where the critical business paths have been identified
first"). ❌A, ❌C, ❌D are never mentioned.

> **Q3.** It can be inferred that the author considers the automation tools to be:
> A. unable to replace engineers entirely
> B. significantly more expensive than manual testing
> C. useful mainly for large enterprises
> D. rejected by most delivery teams

**Answer: A** — the passage says the tools "have not eliminated the need for human judgement" and that deciding
which scenarios represent the business "is precisely the work that tools cannot yet perform" — one step from
"cannot fully replace engineers" ✅. ❌B is never mentioned. ❌C is never mentioned. ❌D is contradicted by P1
("has become standard practice").

> **Q4.** Why does the author mention that scripts which took "two days to write" can now be produced "in under
> an hour"?
> A. To show that engineers are less productive than before
> B. To illustrate the scale of the time saving, which in turn explains the coverage increase
> C. To argue that test scripts should be bought rather than written
> D. To warn that engineers will lose their jobs

**Answer: B** — the comparison establishes the magnitude of the saving, and the next clause in the passage uses it
("which allows teams to increase coverage rather than reduce it"). ❌A and ❌D are unsupported and pessimistic.
❌C is not the author's point.

**Time check:** this passage + 4 questions = about 6 minutes with the procedure. Without it — reading the options
first and re-reading the passage four times — it is 10–12 minutes. That difference is 4 behavioural marks.

## Quick rules

- Read the passage once. Label. Then work the questions.
- Locate the evidence line before reading any option.
- Main idea = P1 topic + P2 twist.
- Inference = exactly one step, no absolutes.
- "Why does the author…" = the sentence's **job**, not its content.
- 90 seconds per question, 2 minutes at the end of the section for re-checking.
- Cross out eliminated options so you do not re-read them.

## Common traps

- **Options before evidence.** The most expensive habit in this sub-type.
- **Reading the passage again for every question** instead of once with labels.
- **Picking a P1 fact for a main-idea question.**
- **Accepting an inference that requires two steps.**
- **Spending 3 minutes on one passage.** Two minutes of review at the end of the section is worth more.

## Checklist

- [ ] I read the passage once and labelled the paragraphs.
- [ ] I underlined the evidence for every question.
- [ ] I checked the question type before choosing.
- [ ] I rejected options with unsupported absolutes or added claims.
- [ ] I finished the passage within its time budget.
