# The Passage-Mapping Method

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Reading Comprehension & Fill Blanks ·  _Estimated reading time: 8 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [Reading comprehension topic README](../README.md)

## In one sentence
**A 100–150 word passage is usually three paragraphs doing three different jobs — identify each job in three words, then every question points at one of them.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Passages in these papers are short and **structured**: a context paragraph, a contrast or development paragraph,
  and a conclusion. That structure is why the mapping method works so reliably.
- Mapping first means you are never hunting. Each question's answer location is already known.
- It also prevents the classic failure: answering a detail question with the main idea.

## Core concept

### The paragraph-function labels

| Label | What the paragraph does | Typical signal words | What kind of question it serves |
|-------|------------------------|---------------------|-------------------------------|
| **P1 — Context / background** | Sets the scene: what, where, since when | "In recent years", "Traditionally", "At many organisations" | Main idea, introductory detail, tone |
| **P2 — Development / contrast / problem** | Adds the twist: "however", "but", "in practice" | "However", "Yet", "Despite", "In practice", "However, this" | Inference, "why" questions, the interesting detail |
| **P3 — Conclusion / recommendation** | What it means, what to do | "Therefore", "Consequently", "The solution", "Organisations should" | Main idea, author intent, recommendation questions |

**Note:** the most testable detail is almost always in **P2**, because that is where the passage adds information
the reader did not already have. The main idea is always the **P1 topic + the P2 twist**.

### The 3-word topic label

After reading each paragraph, write **three words**. Not a summary — three words.

```
P1 → "corporate training programmes"
P2 → "short courses work better"
P3 → "curriculum should be shorter"
```

Those three labels let you answer a main-idea question without re-reading.

### The 5 question types and their evidence

| # | Question type | Signal wording | What the answer must be | Where it lives |
|---|---------------|----------------|--------------------------|----------------|
| 1 | **Main idea / title** | "What is the passage mainly about?", "The title that best fits", "What is the central idea?" | A statement that **the whole passage** supports, including the twist | P1 topic **+** P2 twist |
| 2 | **Detail / specific information** | "According to the passage, …", "What does the author say about X?", "Which of the following is stated?" | Something **explicitly stated**; close paraphrase of one line | Wherever the fact is |
| 3 | **Inference** | "It can be inferred that…", "The author implies…" | Exactly **one step** of reasoning from the passage | Anywhere, but usually P2 |
| 4 | **Vocabulary in context** | "The word 'X' most probably means…", "In paragraph 2, 'X' refers to…" | The sense **used in that sentence**, not the dictionary sense | The sentence containing the word |
| 5 | **Writer's intent / tone / title** | "Why does the author mention X?", "The author mentions X in order to…" | The **purpose** of the sentence, not its content | The sentence itself |

### Main idea — the 3-test

A main-idea option must pass all three:

1. **Coverage** — the whole passage is about it, including the P2 twist.
2. **Neutrality** — it does not add something the passage never said.
3. **Proportion** — it does not spend most of its words on a single paragraph.

| Option type | Verdict |
|-------------|---------|
| A statement covering P1 and P2 | ✅ |
| A fact from P1 only | ❌ (too narrow — it's a detail) |
| A fact from P2 only | ❌ (too narrow) |
| A statement about a topic the passage never mentions | ❌ (outside knowledge) |
| A statement with "always/never/only/most" that the passage does not support | ❌ (too strong) |

### Inference — the 1-step rule

An inference is legitimate only if it follows **without adding new facts**.

| Legitimate inference | Illegitimate inference |
|----------------------|-----------------------|
| Passage: "organisations that train in short bursts see faster skill gains" → Inference: "continuous long training is less effective" ✅ (one step) | Passage: "organisations that train in short bursts see faster skill gains" → Inference: "long training programmes are ineffective" ❌ (two steps + too strong) |

**Test:** could a careful reader of this passage only agree? If a reasonable person could argue the opposite, it is
not supported.

### Writer's intent — the 3-question method

For "Why does the author mention X?":

1. **What does the sentence literally say?** (the content)
2. **What job is it doing in the paragraph?** (context, contrast, example, illustration)
3. **What is the writer's purpose?** (to show that X is possible, to contrast with Y, to warn, to illustrate)

| Option shape | Verdict |
|--------------|---------|
| Restates the content of the sentence | ❌ (the question asks "why", not "what") |
| Says the sentence is an example/illustration of the paragraph's point | ✅ |
| Says the author is criticising the passage's topic without basis | ❌ |
| Says the author is providing contrast with an opposite view | ✅ |

## Worked example

> **Passage (P1 / P2 / P3):**
>
> "Many organisations now run structured learning programmes for new joiners, covering process, tools, and
> domain knowledge. The programmes, however, are often long and delivered in a single week. Teams that break the
> same material into short, spaced sessions report noticeably faster skill gains, because each session has time
> to be applied before the next one begins. Organisations should therefore measure skill retention over
> several months, rather than attendance at the end of a single week."
>
> *P1 → "structured learning programmes"* · *P2 → "short spaced sessions work better"* · *P3 → "measure retention
> over months"*

> **Q1. What is the passage mainly about?**
> A. The problems with new-joiner training in large organisations
> B. How the length and delivery of learning programmes affect skill retention
> C. Why attendance sheets are unreliable in measuring learning
> D. The tools new joiners must learn to work effectively

**Answer: B.** It covers P1 (programmes exist) + P2 (length and spacing matter) + P3 (measure retention over
months). A is too narrow and negative ❌. C is only P3 and adds "unreliable" ❌. D is only P1 and narrows to
tools ❌.

> **Q2. According to the passage, why do short, spaced sessions produce faster skill gains?**
> A. Because trainers can cover more material in less time
> B. Because each session has time to be applied before the next one begins
> C. Because employees attend fewer sessions
> D. Because retention is measured over several months

**Answer: B** — stated almost verbatim in P2 ❌A (not mentioned), ❌C (not mentioned), ❌D (P3, and it explains
measurement, not speed).

> **Q3. It can be inferred that the author would most likely agree that…**
> A. A single week of training is sufficient if the material is well designed
> B. Training content matters less than how it is delivered
> C. New joiners prefer short sessions
> D. Retention cannot be measured reliably

**Answer: B.** The author's argument is that delivery format (length + spacing) drives skill gain more than
content ❌A (contradicted), ❌C (preference is never discussed), ❌D (the author recommends measuring retention,
so they do not consider it impossible).

> **Q4. Why does the author mention "attendance at the end of a single week"?**
> A. To show that attendance sheets are commonly used
> B. To contrast a convenient measure with a more meaningful one
> C. To criticise the training material
> D. To explain why new joiners attend sessions

**Answer: B** — P3 contrasts the convenient measure ("attendance") with the meaningful one ("skill retention over
several months") ❌A (restates content), ❌C (no criticism of material), ❌D (nothing about attendance reasons).

## Quick rules

- Read the passage, label the paragraphs in 3 words each, then read the questions.
- Locate the evidence **before** reading the options.
- Main idea = P1 topic + P2 twist.
- Inference = exactly one step.
- "Always/never/only/no one/most" in an option is a red flag.
- "Why does the author…" asks for purpose, not content.
- If you cannot find the line, it is probably an inference question — check the one-step rule.

## Common traps

- **Reading the options first.** You then hunt for a justification and pick the first option that sounds familiar.
- **Answering a main-idea question with a P1 fact.**
- **Over-reading an inference.** Two steps plus a generalisation is not an inference.
- **Using outside knowledge.** If the passage says it, use the passage; if the passage does not, it is not the
  answer.
- **Ignoring the P2 twist.** The twist is what separates the main idea from the obvious summary.

## Checklist

- [ ] I labelled each paragraph in three words before reading the questions.
- [ ] I found the evidence line before reading the options.
- [ ] I checked the question type (main idea / detail / inference / vocabulary / intent).
- [ ] I rejected any option with an unsupported absolute.
- [ ] I moved on at 90 seconds per question.
