# The Almost-Right Option

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Behavioural & Scenario-Based ·  _Estimated reading time: 7 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [The four-option answer scheme](../01-concepts/01-the-four-option-answer-scheme.md)

## In one sentence
**The option that costs you marks is the one that looks professional but is missing one of the three things the exam requires — a named recipient, a concrete action, or a closure.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Exam writers do not use four random distractors. They use **one clean wrong, two clearly wrong, and one
  almost-right** to catch candidates who stopped one step early.
- In practice, the "almost-right" option is a **passive option dressed in professional vocabulary**, or an
  aggressive option with a soft ending.
- If you know the shape of the almost-right option, you can spot it in 5 seconds.

## Core concept

### The 5 shapes of the almost-right option

| # | Shape | What it looks like | The missing piece |
|---|-------|--------------------|------------------|
| 1 | **Right emotion, no mechanism** | "I would remain calm, empathetic, and professional, and take the necessary steps." | No recipient, no action. All filler. |
| 2 | **Right action, vague recipient** | "I would inform the concerned person and take appropriate action." | "Concerned person" is not a role. Nobody is accountable. |
| 3 | **Right recipient, no plan** | "I would inform my manager about the delay." | Reports the problem and stops. The manager now has your problem, not a solution. |
| 4 | **Right plan, wrong level** | "I would inform the client and propose a revised timeline." | Bypasses your manager and the delivery manager. Escalation dressed as initiative. |
| 5 | **Right everything, wrong moment** | "I would immediately inform my manager and escalate it to the top management, and also inform the client." | Too many levels at once, and no severity judgement. |

### The three-question test that kills all five

Ask these in order about your chosen option:

1. **Recipient test** — Does it name a *role* (team lead, delivery manager, CRM, module owner)? "Concerned
   party", "the concerned person", "the authorities" → fail.
2. **Action test** — Is there a verb of *work* (analyse, propose, document, schedule, correct, track)? "Take
   appropriate steps", "handle it sensitively" → fail.
3. **Closure test** — Is there anything after the communication (track, confirm, follow up, verify)? If the option
   ends at "I informed my manager", the option is incomplete → fail.

✅ An option that passes all three is your answer. ✅

### The "sounds too good" heuristic

Some distractors are built from **generic best-practice phrases**: "learn from the mistake", "take it as a
learning opportunity", "be a team player", "handle it maturely".

These phrases are used in distractors because they contain **no information**. They could describe any behaviour,
including doing nothing. A correct option must be **situation-specific**: it must not be true in a different
scenario without change.

| Generic (distractor) | Specific (usually correct) |
|----------------------|---------------------------|
| "I would handle it professionally." | "I would inform the delivery manager with the impact analysis and propose a revised release date." |
| "I would take it as a learning opportunity." | "I would document the incident, inform my lead, and add a checklist step before the next release." |
| "I would ensure the team is informed." | "I would inform the team lead and the module owner, and record it in the defect tracker." |

⚠️ "Take it as a learning opportunity" is **not** always wrong — it is wrong as the *only* content of an option.
If an option says "I would inform my manager, document the root cause, and treat it as a learning to update
our checklist", it is fine.

## Worked example

> **Q.** You discover that a requirement you agreed with the client was missed in the design document. The
> development team is starting coding tomorrow.
>
> A. Inform the business analyst in writing that the requirement is missing from the design document, attach the
>    client's requirement note, and propose adding it to the next design review.
> B. Inform the business analyst about the missing requirement and suggest that we should always be more careful
>    in future.
> C. Speak to the business analyst informally and mention that the requirement was missed, and ask them to
>    update the document before the team starts coding.
> D. Add the missing requirement to the design document yourself so the team has everything they need.

**Answer: A**

| Option | Shape | Analysis |
|--------|-------|----------|
| A | ✅ Correct | Named recipient (BA) + evidence (client's requirement note) + proposed action (add to next design review). |
| B | Shape 3 | Reports the problem, then adds a platitude instead of a plan. "Be more careful" is not an action anyone can perform. |
| C | Shape 3 + no evidence | An informal verbal mention to one person, with no documentation, no proposal, and no timeline. It also relies on the BA remembering. It has the *gist* of A but none of A's mechanism. This is the classic almost-right: same instinct, no evidence, no closure. |
| D | Shape 4 | Takes unilateral ownership of a client requirement without validation — the BA confirms the requirement's meaning and priority. Editing the document yourself risks an unauthorised change. Also passive about the real risk: the team starts coding tomorrow and nobody has agreed the requirement is in scope. |

**Why C is the trap:** you already knew the right move (tell the BA) — you just did it badly. The distractor
punishes the *quality* of the response, not the *direction*. Notice the tell-tale: C has no written record, no
attached evidence, and no proposal.

## Quick rules

- Filler phrases ("handle it professionally", "take it as a learning opportunity") = distractor markers.
- The correct option must **break** if you move it to a different scenario. If it still works, it is generic.
- Every correct option has: **role + action + closure**.
- "I would ensure…" without saying who does what = almost-right.
- "Inform the client" alone = over-escalation, even if the intent is transparency.
- Length helps only as a tie-breaker: the correct option usually has 2 clauses; the almost-right has 1.

## Common traps

- **Sympathy with the passive option.** "I would not want anyone to be blamed, so I would keep it to myself"
  sounds noble. It is silence.
- **Mistaking "being positive" for being professional.** "I would be positive and motivate the team" says
  nothing about the defect.
- **Choosing the option with the fewest words.** The shortest option is usually passive or generic.
- **Not checking for closure.** The most common almost-right failure is stopping after "I informed X".
- **Trusting "would like to" / "would prefer to" framing.** A wish is not a process.

## Checklist

- [ ] My chosen option names a **role**, not a vague party.
- [ ] My chosen option contains a **verb of work**, not only an attitude.
- [ ] My chosen option has something after the communication (track, confirm, follow up).
- [ ] I checked that the option would **not** be true in a completely different scenario.
- [ ] I did not pick an option containing "handle it professionally" style filler.
