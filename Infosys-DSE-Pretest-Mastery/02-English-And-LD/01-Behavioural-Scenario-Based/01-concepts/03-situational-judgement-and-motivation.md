# Situational Judgement & Motivation Questions

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Behavioural & Scenario-Based ·  _Estimated reading time: 6 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [The four-option answer scheme](01-the-four-option-answer-scheme.md)

## In one sentence
**Situational-judgement items ask you to score the same four behaviours by direction — "best" or "least appropriate" — and motivational items ask you to show that you want the *work*, not just the *offer*.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Two stems dominate the rest: **"Which of the following is the most/least appropriate response?"** and
  **"Why do you want to work at Infosys?" / "Why did you choose this stream?"**
- The "least appropriate" version is the nastiest item type in Section 2. Your elimination logic has to run
  **backwards**: the passive option stops being the worst and the aggressive/over-escalating option becomes it.
- Motivation questions are scored on **evidence of fit**, not on enthusiasm. "I am passionate" is empty.
  "I want to work on data pipelines for a global bank, which is what I want to specialise in" is evidence.

## Core concept

### A. Best vs least appropriate — how the scoring flips

| Question asks for | The answer is the option that is… | Usual archetype |
|-------------------|----------------------------------|-----------------|
| **Best / most appropriate / ideal** | Calmest, most specific, most process-shaped | Professional |
| **Least appropriate / worst / not recommended** | Emotionally reactive, or process-jumping, or blaming | Aggressive or Over-escalating |

**Practical consequence for a "least appropriate" question:**

1. Immediately eliminate the **professional** option — it cannot be the answer, however tempting.
2. The **passive** option is usually *not* the least appropriate. Doing nothing is lazy, but it does not damage the
   team, the client, or the process. It is weak, not worst.
3. The **worst** option is the one that creates a *new* problem: a public confrontation, a formal complaint, a
   bypassed hierarchy, a broken client relationship, or a threat.
4. If both an aggressive and an over-escalating option exist, pick the one that **causes irreversible damage**
   (a threat to quit, an accusation of misconduct, informing the client that the company is at fault).

⚠️ Trap: many candidates treat "least appropriate" as "least professional". The passive option *is* the least
professional, but it is rarely the *least appropriate*. "Least appropriate" means "worst outcome", not "laziest".

### B. The stem patterns and what they want

| Stem pattern | What it is really testing | The expected answer |
|--------------|---------------------------|--------------------|
| "What would you do if…?" | Your default process under pressure | Option with ownership + communication + plan |
| "Which is the best response?" | Judgement | The professional archetype |
| "Which is the least appropriate?" | Damage control | The aggressive / over-escalating archetype |
| "Your team lead is not in office and there is a delivery risk." | Initiation + escalation discipline | Inform the next level / inform the delivery manager, with plan |
| "A colleague keeps making the same mistake." | Feedback skill | Private, factual, specific feedback, then escalate if it continues |
| "You disagree with your manager's decision." | Disagree-and-commit | Rationale + review + support the final decision |
| "Why do you want to join Infosys?" | Fit + motivation quality | Specific, mutual, evidence-based |
| "Why this stream?" | Self-awareness + informed choice | Link to skills, interest, and the stream's actual work |
| "What is your greatest weakness?" | Self-awareness + action | Real weakness + concrete corrective action, no cliché |
| "Where do you see yourself in 5 years?" | Aspiration realism | Growth inside the domain you are choosing, not title-chasing |

### C. The motivational answer — 4 components

A strong motivational answer contains:

1. **Specificity** — name the actual domain, product, client type, or technology you want to work on.
2. **Mutuality** — what you will contribute, not only what you want. ("I want to apply my Java and DBMS skills
   on enterprise delivery problems" beats "I want to learn and grow".)
3. **A credible reason for Infosys specifically** — scale and global delivery, structured learning, exposure to
   multiple domains, the technology depth of a delivery partner, working with distributed teams.
4. **Forward-looking action** — what you would do in the first year. ("I want to be strong in the full stack
   before the stream assessment.")

| Answer type | Verdict | Why |
|-------------|---------|-----|
| "Because of the salary and the brand name." | ❌ | Purely transactional; ignores the work |
| "Because I could not get a job anywhere else." | ❌ | Desperation; no fit shown |
| "Because my friend works here." | ❌ | Referral is not a reason |
| "I want to work on large-scale distributed systems, and I would contribute my Java and DBMS foundation while learning the enterprise stack." | ✅ | Specific, mutual, Infosys-relevant, forward-looking |
| "I want to be part of a global delivery team and learn domain expertise, because I enjoy solving data problems for real users." | ✅ | Good: names the work and the mutual part |

### D. The "disagree with your manager" archetype — in detail

This one appears in almost every batch, so learn it properly.

| Step | Correct behaviour |
|------|-------------------|
| 1 | Understand the decision fully — ask what constraint drove it (deadline, cost, skill availability) |
| 2 | Prepare a **written rationale** with data, not opinion |
| 3 | Ask for a **review meeting** with the evidence present, and listen to the constraint you missed |
| 4 | If the decision stands, **commit and support it** — record the risk |
| 5 | Move on. Do not repeat the objection publicly or to peers |

❌ Never: go around your manager to their peers; complain to the team; refuse to execute; escalate immediately to
the next level; argue in a meeting without preparation.

## Worked example

> **Q.** A colleague of yours has consistently missed the deadline for a shared module. You have raised it once in
> a stand-up and nothing changed. The module goes to UAT next Monday.
>
> A. Send a formal email to your project manager and the client relationship manager marking the module as high
>    risk, and CC the entire project team.
> B. Tell the team that anyone who cannot finish on time should not be assigned to shared deliverables.
> C. Speak to the colleague privately with the specific dates and impact, agree a recovery plan, and inform your
>    team lead that a recovery plan is now in place.
> D. Do nothing further. You have already raised it once, and it is not your module to manage.

**Answer: C.**

**Step-by-step:**

| Option | Archetype | Failure |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| A | Over-escalating | Bypasses the team lead, CCs everyone, and frames a team issue as a client risk before trying a fix. Escalation before conversation. |
| B | Aggressive | A public put-down with no facts, no plan, and no attempt to solve the delivery problem. It also creates a hostile team. |
| C | **Professional** | Private, specific, factual conversation → concrete recovery plan → team lead informed with a plan, not a complaint. Note it *escalates* only after the direct step. |
| D | Passive | The first escalation attempt was a public stand-up mention with no specific dates and no plan. Silence now hands the risk to the team. |

**Why C over A:** The escalation ladder says Level 2 (direct conversation) comes before Level 3 (team lead) and
Level 4 (project manager + CRM). C follows the ladder. A skips two rungs on the first try.

## Quick rules

- Circle the judgement word: **best** → keep the professional option; **least** → throw it away.
- In a "least appropriate" question, the worst option is the one that **creates a new problem**, not the one that
  does nothing.
- Motivation answers need **specific + mutual + Infosys-relevant + forward-looking**. All four.
- "Because of the salary" is always wrong; "because of the scale, the learning, and the domain" is right.
- For a disagreement, the sequence is always: **prepare evidence → request review → commit**.
- For a repeated teammate mistake: **private, specific, planned** — then escalate.
- "I would keep my manager informed" is good. "I would inform my manager and also update the whole project" is
  over-escalation.

## Common traps

- **Score-blind reading.** Reading all four options and then choosing without checking "least" flips the answer
  with near certainty.
- **Confusing "lazy" with "harmful".** The passive option is the least professional. It is rarely the least
  appropriate.
- **The enthusiasm trap.** A long, emotional motivation answer with no specifics scores worse than three
  specific sentences.
- **Disagree-and-escalate.** The professional answer to "I disagree with my manager" involves a review meeting and
  then commitment — not a complaint to HR.
- **Naming a stream you did not research.** "I want the AI stream because AI is the future" is weak. Name a task,
  not a trend.
- **The perfectionist trap.** "I would not act until I had complete information" is a passive answer wearing a
  professional costume.

## Checklist

- [ ] I circled the judgement word before reading options.
- [ ] In a "least appropriate" question, I eliminated the professional option first.
- [ ] My motivation answer names something specific about Infosys or the stream.
- [ ] My motivation answer says what **I contribute**, not only what I get.
- [ ] My disagreement answer ends in commit-and-support, not repeated objection.
