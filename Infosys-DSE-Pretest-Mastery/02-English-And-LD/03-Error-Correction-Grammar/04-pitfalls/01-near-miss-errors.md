# Near-Miss Errors

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Error Correction ·  _Estimated reading time: 7 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [Error taxonomy](../01-concepts/01-error-taxonomy.md)

## In one sentence
**The two traps in error correction are the segments that look wrong but are right, and the errors that hide inside a phrase that looks grammatically complete — and you lose marks on both.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Weak-English readers over-flag: they pick the chunk with the unfamiliar word, the longest chunk, or the chunk
  with a preposition they have never seen.
- Strong readers still lose marks by over-flagging formal-but-correct constructions.
- The countermeasure is a **distractor vocabulary**: a list of things that are correct but look wrong.

## Core concept

### Trap 1 — Correct but unfamiliar

| Segment that looks wrong | Why it is actually correct |
|--------------------------|------------------------------|
| "in accordance with the requirements" | Formal but standard |
| "with respect to the second phase" | Correct preposition |
| "in the event that the client requires" | Formal but correct |
| "the reason being that…" | Colloquial, accepted in Indian English |
| "prior to the handover" | Correct, formal |
| "on the basis of the analysis" | Correct |
| "at this point in time" | Correct, if wordy |
| "the same as" / "the same to" | Both correct in different structures |
| "each other" | Only for two; "one another" for more — but the exam rarely tests it |
| "comprised of" | Widely used and accepted in Indian English |
| "different from" / "different than" | Both acceptable in modern usage |
| "towards" vs "toward" | Both correct |
| "learnt" / "learned" | Both correct |

⚠️ None of the above is a reliable error. If your answer depends on "that sounds wrong to me", it is probably
wrong.

### Trap 2 — The error that hides inside a correct-looking phrase

This is the most common way candidates lose marks: the broken part is a **single word** inside a phrase that
otherwise works.

| Broken chunk | The hidden error | Fix |
|--------------|------------------|-----|
| "the team **were** informed" | plural verb for a unified team | "the team **was** informed" |
| "each of the modules **require**" | plural verb after "each" | "**requires**" |
| "a **hour**" | wrong article by sound | "**an** hour" |
| "discussed **about**" | extra preposition | "discussed" |
| "**more** then 5" | than/then | "**than**" |
| "**effect** the schedule" | affect/effect | "**affect**" |
| "**less** errors" | fewer/less | "**fewer** errors" |
| "**it's** own team" | its/it's | "**its** own team" |
| "**there** going to be a delay" | their/there/they're | "**they're** going to be a delay" |
| "for **since** two years" | since/for | "**since** two years" or "**for** two years" |
| "the engineer **which** did it" | who/which | "**who**" |
| "I enjoy **to work**" | gerund/infinitive | "**working**" |
| "**not only** … **but also**" | ✅ **this one is correct** — do not flag it | — |
| "**any more** questions?" (in a question) | ✅ correct | — |

### Trap 3 — Judging the chunk, not the clause

| Situation | Why it bites |
|-----------|--------------|
| A relative clause with its subject outside the chunk | "the file **that I sent to Rahul**" — the subject of "sent" is "I", not "the file" |
| A chunk containing a coordinating conjunction | ", and the client **has** been informed" — the verb belongs to the second clause |
| A chunk containing a preposition whose object is outside the chunk | "depends on **the** schedule" — the article belongs to "schedule" |
| A chunk ending mid-phrase | "of the" in "the scope **of the** work" — perfectly fine |

### Trap 4 — Forcing an error that is not there

Sometimes you scan and find nothing. Then:

1. Re-read the **options** — you have probably mis-segmented.
2. Check whether you mis-read a word ("were" vs "was", "its" vs "it's", "then" vs "than").
3. Only then, consider that the error is a rarer family (comma splice, dangling participle, redundant modifier).

**Do not** invent an error in a chunk because it reads awkwardly.

### The 20-second elimination protocol

```
1. Read the chunk once.
2. Ask: can I name the RULE it breaks?          → no rule, no error.
3. Ask: is the rule a real rule, not a style?    → style ≠ error.
4. If yes → candidate. Now check the other three chunks are clean.
```

### The "swap test"

A powerful confirmation: **swap the chunk with each of the others.** The correct chunk, when it sits in the
sentence, produces a sentence that is unambiguously correct. If a candidate chunk produces a sentence that is
still awkward or still broken, it is not the error.

## Worked example

> **Q.** Identify the part of the sentence that contains an error.
>
> "**One of the modules** **require** **a restart after every deployment**, **and the team is investigating** **the cause**."
>
> A. One of the modules
> B. require a restart after every deployment
> C. and the team is investigating
> D. the cause

**Answer: B**

**The scan:**

| Step | Finding |
|------|---------|
| Meaning | One module needs a restart after each deployment; the team is looking into why. |
| Verb | "require", "is investigating" |
| Agreement | "**One** of the modules **require**" ❌ — the subject of the clause is "**One**", a singular pronoun, so the verb must be "**requires**". The plural noun "modules" is inside a prepositional phrase ("of the modules") and does **not** control the verb. |
| Tense | Both present simple, describing current facts ✅ |
| Preposition | "a restart **after** every deployment" ✅ · "the cause" ✅ |
| Article | "a restart" (indefinite, first mention) ✅ · "the cause" (specific) ✅ |
| Modifier | "after every deployment" correctly modifies "restart" ✅ |
| Connector | ", and" joins two independent clauses ✅ |

**Fix:** "One of the modules **requires** a restart after every deployment…"

**Why not the others — and this is the near-miss:**

| Chunk | Why it looks like the error | Why it is correct |
|-------|------------------------------|-------------------|
| A | It contains the plural noun "modules" right next to a singular verb in the next chunk. It *looks* like the agreement error. | There is no verb in this chunk. The real subject is "One", and it is in the next chunk. Judging a chunk in isolation gives the wrong answer here. |
| C | It contains "the team **is**" — and "the team are" is a famous agreement trap, so "the team is investigating" looks like the Indian-vs-British usage question. | In Indian English a unified team takes a **singular** verb, so "the team **is**" is correct. Do not flag it. |
| D | Nothing wrong with a bare noun phrase. | ✅ Correct. |

**The two lessons:**
1. The error sat in a chunk that contained **no verb at all** (chunk B contains the verb, but the **subject** is in
   chunk A). You must pull the subject and the verb together before you judge.
2. Two other chunks contained textbook agreement-looking patterns ("one of the modules", "the team is") that were
   both **correct**. Over-flagging is how you pick A or C here instead of B.

## Quick rules

- Name the **rule**, not the feeling. "It sounds wrong" is not an analysis.
- Style is not an error. Formality, length, and register are never counted.
- The error is often **one word** inside a longer correct phrase.
- Check for near-identical word pairs: were/was, its/it's, then/than, affect/effect, fewer/less, since/for,
  who/which, principal/principle.
- If nothing fires, re-read the options and look for a single mis-read word.
- Never force an error you cannot name.

## Common traps

- **Flagging "in accordance with"** or "prior to" as wrong.
- **Flagging "not only … but also"** as a double negative.
- **Missing "its" vs "it's"** because both look like "it s".
- **Flagging "comprised of"** or "different than".
- **Assuming the error must be in the longest option.**

## Checklist

- [ ] For each chunk I could name a specific rule.
- [ ] I confirmed the other three chunks are clean.
- [ ] I checked the near-identical word pairs.
- [ ] I did not flag anything merely because it is formal or unusual.
- [ ] I ran the repair test on my answer.
