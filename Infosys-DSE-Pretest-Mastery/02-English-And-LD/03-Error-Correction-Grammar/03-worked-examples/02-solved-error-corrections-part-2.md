# Solved Error Corrections — Part 2

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Error Correction ·  _Estimated reading time: 10 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [The 8-step scan](../02-techniques/01-the-eight-step-scan.md)

## In one sentence
**Five more solved items from the less frequent families — comma splice, double negative, since/for, who/which/that, and redundant modifiers — so that no item in the paper can surprise you.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- The rarer families appear maybe once in three papers. Each one costs you 90 seconds and one mark if you cannot
  recognise it.
- These families also produce the most convincing distractors, because a comma splice or a double negative often
  *reads* fluently.

## Core concept

Scan order unchanged: MEANING → VERB → AGREEMENT → TENSE → PREPOSITION → ARTICLE → MODIFIER → CONNECTOR.

## Worked example

### Q1. Comma splice

> **Q.** Identify the part of the sentence that contains an error.
>
> "**The deployment failed**, **we rolled back** **the release** **and informed** **the** **client about the** **delay**."
>
> A. The deployment failed,
> B. we rolled back the release
> C. and informed the client about the
> D. delay

**Answer: A** — the comma joins two independent clauses with no coordinator: "The deployment failed" is a complete
clause, and "we rolled back…" is another. A comma alone cannot join them.

**Fixes (all valid):**
- "The deployment failed**;** we rolled back the release and informed the client about the delay."
- "The deployment failed**, and** we rolled back the release and informed the client about the delay."
- "The deployment failed**. W**e rolled back the release and informed the client about the delay."
- "**After** the deployment failed, we rolled back the release and informed the client about the delay."

**Why not the others:** B is a well-formed independent clause — on its own it is fine. C is a correct coordinated
verb phrase sharing the subject "we" ✅. D is a plain noun phrase.

### Q2. Double negative

> **Q.** Identify the part of the sentence that contains an error.
>
> "**Since** **the** **report was not approved**, **we** **did not receive** **no** **feedback** **from the** **reviewer**."
>
> A. Since the report was not approved
> B. we did not receive
> C. no feedback from the
> D. reviewer

**Answer: C**

| Step | Finding |
|------|---------|
| Meaning | The report was not approved, so we got no feedback from the reviewer. |
| Verb | "was not approved", "did not receive" |
| Agreement | "the report **was**" ✅ · "**we did**" ✅ |
| Tense | Consistent past ✅ |
| Preposition | "feedback **from** the reviewer" ✅ |
| Article | "the report", "the reviewer" ✅ |
| Modifier | "Since the report was not approved" — a subordinate clause modifying the main clause ✅ |
| Connector | **Double negative** — "did **not** receive **no** feedback" ❌ (standard formal English avoids stacking two negatives) |

**Fix:** "…we did not receive **any** feedback from the reviewer." (or "we received **no** feedback from the
reviewer.")

**Why not the others:** A is a correct subordinating clause with a passive verb. B is correct — one negative is
fine. D is a plain noun phrase.

**Exception to remember:** "not only … but also" is a legitimate correlative pair: "He is not only skilled but also
diligent."

---

### Q3. since vs for

> **Q.** Identify the part of the sentence that contains an error.
>
> "**The** **project has been running for** **the last two years**, **and during** **this period** **the team has** **delivered nine** **releases**."
>
> A. The project has been running for
> B. the last two years
> C. and during this period
> D. the team has delivered nine releases

**Answer: B** — "for the last two years" ❌. "For" takes a **duration**; "the last two years" is a **period
defined by a point** ("the last two years" = from two years ago until now). The correct forms are "**for two
years**" (plain duration) or "**since** two years ago" / "**since** 2024" (point).

**Fixes:** "The project has been running **for two years**…" or "The project has been running **since 2024**…"

**Why not the others:** A is correct — "for" with the present perfect continuous ("has been running for") ✅. C is a
correct coordinating conjunction + prepositional phrase ✅. D is a correct plural verb with "the team" as a unified
whole in Indian English, and "nine releases" is correct.

**Rule of thumb:** `since` + a **starting point** (a date, a year, "Monday", "two hours ago"); `for` + a **bare
duration** ("two hours", "three years", "a long time").

### Q4. who / which / that

> **Q.** Identify the part of the sentence that contains an error.
>
> "**The engineer which** **fixed the** **build** **is on** **leave** **this week**, **and the** **module that** **she** **delivered last** **month was** **released on time**."
>
> A. The engineer which
> B. fixed the build is on
> C. leave this week, and the
> D. module that she delivered last month was released on time

**Answer: A**

| Step | Finding |
|------|---------|
| Meaning | The engineer who fixed the build is on leave; the module she delivered last month was released on time. |
| Verb | "fixed", "is", "was released", "delivered" |
| Agreement | "the engineer **fixed**" ✅ · "**is** on leave" ✅ · "the module **was released**" ✅ |
| Tense | Past and present used appropriately in different clauses ✅ |
| Preposition | "on leave" ✅ · "on time" ✅ |
| Article | "the build", "the module" ✅ |
| Modifier | "which fixed the build" — attaches to "the engineer" ❌ (a person cannot be a "which") |
| Connector | ", and" joins two independent clauses ✅ |

**Fix:** "**The engineer who** fixed the build is on leave this week…"

**Why not the others:** B is a correct verb phrase in a subordinate clause. C is a correct comma + coordinator
joining two independent clauses. D is a correct relative clause using "that" for a thing, with "she" referring
to the engineer ✅.

**Rule:** `who` for people, `which` for things, `that` for either (restrictive only — no comma before it).

---

### Q5. Redundant modifier

> **Q.** Identify the part of the sentence that contains an error.
>
> "The team **has** **written** **a** **new** **software** **module** **for the** **new** **client** **and it is** **absolutely essential** **for the** **release**."
>
> A. has written a new software module
> B. for the new client
> C. and it is absolutely essential
> D. for the release

**Answer: C**

| Step | Finding |
|------|---------|
| Meaning | The team wrote a module for a new client, and it is essential for the release. |
| Verb | "has written", "is" |
| Agreement | "the team **has written**" ✅ (unified team, singular in Indian English) · "**it is**" ✅ |
| Tense | Present perfect + present simple, consistent ✅ |
| Preposition | "**for** the new client" ✅ · "**for** the release" ✅ |
| Article | "**a** new software module" ✅ (indefinite first mention) · "the new client" ✅ |
| Modifier | "**absolutely essential**" ❌ — "essential" already means "completely necessary". "Absolutely" adds nothing. Redundant modifiers are counted as errors in this item format. |
| Connector | "and" joining two independent clauses ✅ |

**Fix:** "…and it is **essential** for the release."

**Why not the others:** A is correct — "new" modifies "module" and is not redundant ("new" + "module" is fine;
it is "new" + "software" that would be arguable). B is correct — "the new client" is specific. D is a correct
prepositional phrase.

**Note on A:** "new software module" is acceptable, because "new" and "software" are not synonyms. A redundancy
error needs **two words that mean the same thing**: "absolutely essential", "completely finished", "final
conclusion", "free gift", "past history", "end result", "repeat again", "unexpected surprise".

## Quick rules

- A comma cannot join two independent clauses on its own.
- Two negatives = error in formal English; "not only … but also" is the exception.
- `since` + a point, `for` + a bare duration.
- `who` for people, `which` for things, `that` for either.
- Redundant modifiers come in matched pairs — memorise the list.
- In these five items, the errors were: a comma, a negative, a preposition, a relative pronoun, and one
  redundant adverb.

## Common traps

- **Missing the splice boundary.** In Q1 the wrong part is only two words and a comma — you must see the whole
  clause pair.
- **Accepting "did not receive no feedback"** because it "sounds emphatic". It is a double negative.
- **Using "which" for a person** because the rest of the sentence is about a build.
- **Flagging "the new client"** as redundant. Redundancy needs two words meaning the same thing.

## Checklist

- [ ] For every comma, I checked whether both sides are independent clauses.
- [ ] I scanned for stacked negatives.
- [ ] I checked since/for and who/which/that.
- [ ] I checked the modifier list for redundant pairs.
- [ ] I confirmed my fix repairs the sentence.
