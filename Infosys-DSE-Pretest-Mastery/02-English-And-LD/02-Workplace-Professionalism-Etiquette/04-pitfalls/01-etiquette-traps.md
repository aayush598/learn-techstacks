# Etiquette Traps

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Workplace Professionalism & Etiquette ·  _Estimated reading time: 7 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [The etiquette decision check](../02-techniques/02-the-etiquette-decision-check.md)

## In one sentence
**The etiquette traps are the options that are either *polite but empty*, *efficient but non-compliant*, or *helpful but rule-breaking* — and one of those three is the intended answer every time.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- These items rarely have an obviously wrong option. All four sound reasonable in the abstract; only one survives
  contact with the role, the rule, and the impact.
- Knowing the three trap shapes lets you eliminate without evaluating the merits of each option.

## Core concept

### Trap type 1 — Polite but empty

**Shape:** correct tone, zero mechanism. "I would thank the client and assure them we value their association."

| Element | Trap | Professional |
|---------|------|-------------|
| Thank-you | ✅ | ✅ |
| Action | ❌ "assure them we value them" | ✅ specific next step with a time |
| Record | ❌ | ✅ written summary |

**Kill it by:** asking *"what changes because of this behaviour?"* If the answer is "nothing", it is a trap.

### Trap type 2 — Efficient but non-compliant

**Shape:** saves time, breaks a rule. "Skip the mandatory session — the material repeats what I know."

| Element | Trap | Professional |
|---------|------|-------------|
| Time saved | ✅ | — |
| Rule followed | ❌ | ✅ |
| Alternative used | ❌ | ✅ reschedule / online module / prior approval |

**Kill it by:** asking *"which written rule does this break?"* If one exists, the option is out.

### Trap type 3 — Helpful but rule-breaking

**Shape:** solves the requester's problem, breaks a duty. "Send the data to my colleague for their portfolio."

**Kill it by:** asking *"whose interest does this serve, and whose interest does it cost?"* Confidentiality items
are almost always this shape.

### Trap type 4 — Right for you, wrong for the role

**Shape:** behaviour appropriate for a peer, inappropriate for a new joiner, a senior, or a client-facing person.
"Your team wears T-shirts, so you will too." / "The senior was wrong, so correct him."

**Kill it by:** check 1 of the decision check — **role**.

### Trap type 5 — Over-communicating

**Shape:** solves the communication problem by exposing everyone. "Reply-all to the whole project so someone acts."

**Kill it by:** check 4 — **timing/proportionality**. Escalation is a last resort, not a first move.

### Trap type 6 — Self-interested framing

**Shape:** the behaviour is dressed up as professional but the motive is convenience. "I will leave early since
the code compiles and works on my machine."

**Kill it by:** the real-world self-check — *"would this look good if it appeared in a screenshot in a training
deck?"*

## Worked example

> **Q.** A new joiner notices that colleagues regularly share their company laptop passwords with each other
> so that they can "quickly" log in to a shared screen for a meeting. What is the most appropriate response?
>
> A. Join the practice, since refusing would make the new joiner look difficult on day one.
> B. Use the shared login only when a meeting is starting and the laptop is far from the desk.
> C. Decline to share or use shared credentials, and if the practice creates a real access problem, raise it
>    through the access-request process or with the team lead so that a compliant solution is put in place.
> D. Report the four colleagues who share passwords to the ethics helpline immediately.

**Answer: C**

| Option | Trap type | Failure |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| A | Trap 6 | Conformity is not professionalism. Shared credentials break authentication, audit trails, and access control — and joining makes you a participant. |
| B | Trap 2 | "Only during meetings" does not fix anything; it just schedules the breach. The rule is not about timing. |
| C | ✅ RIGHT | Refuses the practice, states the compliant alternative route, and raises it as a **process** issue rather than a person issue. |
| D | Trap 5 / over-escalation | Naming four colleagues and going straight to the ethics helpline on day one is disproportionate and damages relationships. The professional route is the team lead or the access-request process. |

**Why C and not D:** C fixes the system and protects the people. D punishes individuals for a process gap that
the organisation itself created.

## Quick rules

- Ask **"what changes because of this?"** — no change means trap.
- Ask **"which rule does this break?"** — a rule means trap.
- Ask **"whose interest does this serve?"** — if it serves you at a colleague's or client's expense, trap.
- Check the **role**, not just the rule: peers and new joiners are held to different standards.
- **Never** report a colleague publicly or as a first move; use the process route first.
- **Never** adopt a local practice as a personal standard if it breaks policy.
- Escalation is **proportionate**: team lead → process owner → formal channel.

## Common traps

- **"Everyone does it"** is the single most common distractor logic in this sub-type.
- **Assuming someone else will handle it** (the minutes, the compliance training record, the follow-up mail).
- **Apologising as the whole behaviour.** An apology with no recovery plan is a trap.
- **Doing the right thing in the wrong channel** — a formal request sent in a chat group.
- **Confusing firmness with rudeness.** Direct, factual, private correction is professional; public humiliation
  is not.

## Checklist

- [ ] My chosen option changes something or records something.
- [ ] It breaks no written rule.
- [ ] It is proportionate to the role and the impact.
- [ ] It does not require another person's humiliation or exposure to fix it.
- [ ] I would be comfortable if it were screenshotted into a training deck.
