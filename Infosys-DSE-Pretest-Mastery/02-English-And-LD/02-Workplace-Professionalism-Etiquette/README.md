# Workplace Professionalism & Etiquette

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Workplace Professionalism & Etiquette ·  _Estimated reading time: 6 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [Section 2 overview](../README.md)

## In one sentence
**Etiquette questions are scored on one principle — professionalism means protecting the work and the people while keeping the process intact — and the correct option is always the one that does both without bending the rules.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Section 2 usually contains **2–4 etiquette items**: email behaviour, meeting conduct, punctuality, grooming,
  confidentiality, phone etiquette, code of conduct, training attendance, ID badge, personal work on company
  equipment.
- These items test **judgment about norms**, not about rules you memorised. The exam does not give you the
  policy manual; it gives you a situation and expects the conventional professional response.
- Many items are close calls. The tie-breaker is always the same: **does the behaviour protect the client,
  the team, and the process at the same time?**

## Learning objectives

After this folder you should be able to:

- Apply the **5-point etiquette decision check** (role, impact, rule, timing, dignity) to any conduct question.
- Choose the right behaviour for **email, meetings, calls, punctuality, grooming, and follow-up** situations.
- Recognise the **professional email structure** (subject, greeting, body, action, close) and write one in 3 lines.
- Identify the **confidentiality triggers** — what you may never share, with whom, and when.
- Apply Infosys-style **code-of-conduct rules**: training attendance, ID badge, no personal work on company
  machines, dress code, no alcohol on campus, respectful conduct.
- Use the **do/don't reference table** to resolve any "most appropriate professional behaviour" item in 30 seconds.

## Quick procedure — the 30-second etiquette check

```
1. What role does the person in the scenario hold, and what does the
   situation demand of that role? (10s)
2. Does any option break a rule (policy, confidentiality, hierarchy,
   safety)? → eliminate it. (5s)
3. Does the option protect the work AND the people? → the answer. (10s)
4. If two survive: pick the one with the more proportionate
   formality and the earlier communication. (5s)
```

## The 4 filters you apply to any conduct option

| Filter | The test | The option that fails |
|--------|---------|----------------------|
| **Rule** | Does it break a written policy or a standard of conduct? | Secret, informal, off-record |
| **Time** | Does it happen soon enough to matter? | "Raise it in the next review", "mention it later" |
| **Respect** | Does it protect the other person's standing? | Blaming, calling out, going over someone's head |
| **Record** | Is there a trace that protects both sides? | Purely verbal, only on email, nothing documented |

An option must pass all four. Most wrong options fail exactly one.

## Key takeaways

- Professional conduct = **rule-compliant, timely, respectful, and documented**. Four words, four filters.
- An etiquette item is rarely about *whether* you act — it is about **how, when, and through whom** you act.
- **Escalation through the right channel** beats acting directly. Etiquette items punish bypassing the line of
  reporting as firmly as they punish silence.
- **Punctuality is respect.** "Arrive 5–10 minutes early" is the safe answer; "arrive exactly on time" and
  "arrive late if the work is more important" are both wrong.
- **Confidentiality is absolute for client data**, and applies in both directions (you must not leak it, and you
  must not gossip about colleagues).
- **Personal work on company machines is a policy violation**, and the correct answer is to use your own
  devices and time.
- **Never** use a work channel for personal complaints, grievances, or job-search activity.
- When two options look right, choose the one that **involves communication** over the one that just avoids
  trouble.
