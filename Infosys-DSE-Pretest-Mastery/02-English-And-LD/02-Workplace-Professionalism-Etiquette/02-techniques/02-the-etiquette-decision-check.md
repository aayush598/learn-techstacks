# The Etiquette Decision Check

> Section 2 · English & L&D · Workplace Professionalism & Etiquette ·  _Estimated reading time: 7 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [Meetings, punctuality, appearance & confidentiality](../01-concepts/02-meetings-punctuality-appearance-confidentiality.md)

## In one sentence
**Every conduct question in this sub-type is answered by the same five checks — role, rule, impact, timing, dignity — and you can run all five in 30 seconds.**

## Why the pre-test tests this

- Etiquette options are usually close calls. Two of the four survive a first read, which is where candidates
  lose time and then lose marks by guessing.
- The five checks give you a **tie-breaker**, so you are never choosing on feel alone.
- Meera's Section 2 problem is usually here: etiquette items *feel* subjective, so she deliberates. The checks
  make it mechanical.

## Core concept

### The 5-point check

| # | Check | Question | Fail → eliminate |
|---|-------|----------|-----------------|
| 1 | **ROLE** | What does the person's role demand here? (new joiner, senior, project lead, client-facing) | Behaviour that contradicts the role |
| 2 | **RULE** | Does this break a written rule, policy, or code of conduct? | Any policy breach |
| 3 | **IMPACT** | Does it protect the client, the delivery, and the team? | Behaviour that harms one of them |
| 4 | **TIMING** | Is the timing proportionate — early enough to be useful, not so early it is disruptive? | Too late (no benefit) or too early/informal |
| 5 | **DIGNITY** | Are the people involved treated with respect — no public correction, no gossip, no humiliation? | Public put-downs, gossip, blame |

**Scoring:** an option that passes all five is the answer. An option that fails **two or more** is out. If two
options pass all five, use the tie-breaker: **prefer the option that involves communication and documentation**.

### The tie-breaker hierarchy

```
Most professional
  1. Acts, informs the right person, documents, follows up        ← usually the answer
  2. Acts and informs, but informally or late
  3. Informs, but takes no action
  4. Acts alone, informs nobody                                  ← passive risk
  5. Does nothing
Least professional
```

### The archetype labels for conduct questions

| Label | Shape | Example |
|-------|-------|---------|
| **RULE-BREAK** | Violates a written policy | Doing personal work on the office laptop |
| **LAZY** | Avoids the effort or the confrontation | Skipping a mandatory session because it looks repetitive |
| **DISRESPECTFUL** | Treats a person badly | Correcting a senior in front of a client; gossiping about a colleague |
| **SLOPPY** | Looks fine but has a mechanical flaw | Sending an email with no subject, an empty attachment, wrong recipients |
| **DISRUPTIVE** | Breaks the flow of the workplace | Taking calls in a meeting, being late, over-running |
| **RIGHT** | Follows the rule, communicates, respects people | Attend, inform in advance, request a reschedule through the process |

### Common conduct archetypes and their answer shape

| Scenario | Professional answer shape |
|----------|--------------------------|
| Meeting starts at 3 p.m., your task needs 3 hours | Attend the meeting; delegate or rescope the task with a handover note; if the task genuinely outranks the meeting, confirm priority with your manager in advance |
| You will be late for a client meeting | Inform the organiser immediately with a reason and a firm new ETA |
| Mandatory training conflicts with a client call | Attend the training (or use the sanctioned alternate route); inform your manager in advance; request the reschedule through the process |
| A colleague needs confidential data for a personal project | Decline, explain the confidentiality rule and the code of conduct, offer a sanitised dataset through the data owner |
| You spot a colleague's code that violates a security policy | Report through the security/policy channel; do not ignore it, do not broadcast it in the team chat |
| A senior is rude to you | Stay professional, do not retaliate, document if needed, and use the formal channel if it continues |
| You want to raise a grievance about your team lead | Use the formal HR/ethics route, not the team chat and not a colleague's personal email |

### Quick self-check for "I am doing this in real life"

- [ ] Would I be comfortable if this behaviour appeared in a screenshot in a training deck?
- [ ] Am I choosing the convenient option or the correct one?
- [ ] Does my behaviour protect the **client** even when it costs me personally?
- [ ] If my manager read only the written record of this, would it look professional?

## Worked example

> **Q.** A client sends you an urgent request at 9 p.m. on a Friday, right before your planned leave. The client
> expects a response before the weekend. What is the most appropriate behaviour?
>
> A. Ignore it until Monday, since you have already applied for leave.
> B. Reply on Saturday morning with an acknowledgement, tell the client what you can and cannot do before
>    Monday, and arrange a colleague or the on-call resource to handle the urgent part.
> C. Forward it to the whole team group on Saturday night asking who can help.
> D. Tell the client you are on leave and end the conversation there.

**Answer: B**

| Option | Check | Failure |
|--------|-------|---------|
| A | Impact, Timing | A client-visible request dies over a weekend. The professional answer never leaves a client waiting silently, even when you are on leave. |
| B | ✅ All five | Acknowledges promptly, sets honest expectations, and hands the urgent part to someone who can act. Protects client and team. |
| C | Rule, Dignity | Broadcasting into a group chat is over-escalation and it makes a client request look like an internal emergency. It also implies others should just drop their work. |
| D | Impact | Technically polite, but it abandons a client need. The correct behaviour is to arrange cover **and** tell the client. |

**The generalisation:** *leave does not remove responsibility*. The professional answer is always **acknowledge →
set expectations → arrange cover**.

## Quick rules

- Run all 5 checks before you commit: role, rule, impact, timing, dignity.
- Eliminate anything that fails 2+ checks.
- Tie-breaker: prefer the option with communication and documentation.
- **Never** treat a client request as "not my problem because I am on leave".
- **Never** respond to a policy breach with silence or with a group broadcast.
- **Never** publicly correct a senior, and never gossip about colleagues.
- Timing rule of thumb: respond to work items the **same working day**; escalate a client item **immediately**,
  by phone if needed.
- If two options both pass all five checks, pick the one that is **more specific and more actionable**.

## Common traps

- **Confusing politeness with professionalism.** D in the worked example is polite and professionally wrong.
- **Confusing efficiency with correctness.** Skipping a training session is efficient and non-compliant.
- **The "everyone does it" trap.** Common practice that breaks a written rule is still a rule break.
- **Testing on a friend.** Behaviour that would be wrong if a client saw it is wrong.
- **Forgetting dignity.** Options that are technically compliant but humiliating to a colleague are the hardest
  distractors — check check 5 every time.

## Checklist

- [ ] I ran all five checks on my chosen option.
- [ ] My option protects the client, not just my own convenience.
- [ ] My option respects the person involved, including their dignity.
- [ ] My option includes a communication step, not just an action.
- [ ] I did not pick an option that breaks a written policy.
