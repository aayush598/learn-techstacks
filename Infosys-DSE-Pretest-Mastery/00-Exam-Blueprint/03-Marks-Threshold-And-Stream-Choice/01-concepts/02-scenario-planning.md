# Scenario Planning For Target Scores

> Section 00 · Exam Blueprint · Marks Threshold And Stream Choice · _Estimated reading time: 16 min_
> **Prerequisite:** [01-threshold-arithmetic.md](01-threshold-arithmetic.md)

## In one sentence
**Plan three scenarios — weak day, average day, strong day — and decide now what you will do in each so you never improvise under pressure.**

## Why the pre-test tests this
- **Uncertainty:** your accuracy fluctuates day to day, so a single plan is fragile.
- **Pre-decided responses:** avoid in-test panic (Meera's documented weakness).
- **Margin strategy:** strong days build buffer; weak days rely on cheap marks.
- **Realistic targets:** matches the scoring benchmarks used across this resource.

## Core concept

### Three scenarios
| Scenario | Assumed accuracy | Java | English | DBMS | Aptitude | Total | Action |
|----------|------------------|------|---------|------|----------|-------|--------|
| Weak day | Low | 11 | 9 | 6 | 5 | 31 ❌ | Fix before test; more mocks |
| Average day | Medium | 15 | 11 | 8 | 6 | 40 ✅ | Hold the plan; bank cheap marks |
| Strong day | High | 19 | 13 | 9 | 8 | 49 ✅✅ | Do not relax; protect margin |

### Decision rules per scenario
| If you feel... | Do this |
|----------------|---------|
| Slow/hazy (weak day) | Lean on cheap archetypes (DBMS theory, behavioural English, DS, ranking); skip every expensive derivation |
| Normal (average day) | Follow the standard plan; use the 90-second rule strictly |
| Sharp (strong day) | Attempt expensive archetypes too, but still flag at 70s; convert flags in the final 15 min |

### Recovery math
| Gap to 39 | Fastest recovery |
|-----------|------------------|
| 1-2 marks | Two more cheap marks (DBMS theory or behavioural English) |
| 3-5 marks | Add English error correction + Aptitude data sufficiency |
| 6-8 marks | Add recognised Java output patterns |

### Why the weak-day plan matters most
The weak day is the scenario that fails. On a weak day you will not out-think the paper, so you must out-*prioritise* it: take only the cheap marks and refuse every expensive temptation.

## Worked example
**Rahul's weak-day plan.**

Rahul's English is weak, so on a weak day he starts with the 11 cheap English judgment questions (behavioural and etiquette) — they need no grammar skill. He then banks DBMS theory (ACID, normalization) for about 6 marks. That is roughly `11 + 6 = 17` near-certain marks before touching anything hard.

He then spends the rest of the test on structural Aptitude (data sufficiency, ranking, inequalities) and Java output he recognises. If his strong-suit Java delivers 15, he lands near 40 even on a bad English day.

**Numbers:** the weak-day plan swaps "attempt everything" for "secure 17 cheap marks". It trades upside for certainty, which is exactly what a weak day needs.

### Rebuilding the plan after a mock
| Step | Action |
|------|--------|
| 1 | Score the mock |
| 2 | Assign it to weak / average / strong |
| 3 | Check the gap to 39 in each scenario |
| 4 | Adjust the weak-day cheap-mark list if needed |

## Quick rules
- **Pre-decide all three scenarios now.**
- **Weak day = cheap marks only.**
- **Strong day = still flag at 70 seconds.**
- **Recover gaps from the listed order.**
- **Never change method under pressure — only the ambition.**
- **The weak-day plan is the one that saves you.**

## Common traps
- **Not planning for a weak day** at all.
- **Over-attempting expensive archetypes** on a bad day.
- **Relaxing on a strong day** and losing the margin you built.
- **Treating the plan as fixed** when the day clearly changes.

## Checklist
- [ ] You have three scenario totals written down.
- [ ] You know your weak-day cheap set.
- [ ] You know your recovery order by gap size.
- [ ] You keep the 70s rule even on strong days.
- [ ] You have tested a weak-day plan in practice.
- [ ] You never change method under pressure.
