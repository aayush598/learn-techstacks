# Finding Your Floor Rate: The Minimum Viable Freelance Rate

## Why Your Floor Rate Matters More Than Your Target Rate

Most freelancers set prices based on what they *hope* to earn or what they *think* the market will bear. This is backwards. Your floor rate — the absolute minimum you can charge without losing money or burning out — is the single most important financial number in your freelance business. Everything else builds from this foundation.

If you price below your floor rate, you are paying for the privilege of working. Every project below this threshold erodes your savings, your sanity, and your ability to deliver quality work. This guide walks through calculating your true floor rate with precision.

## Step 4: Calculate Your Billable Hours

This is where most freelancers make a catastrophic mistake. They assume they can bill 40 hours per week, 52 weeks per year. That is 2,080 hours. In reality, the number is much lower.

**Realistic Billable Hours Calculation:**

| Factor | Calculation |
|---|---|
| Total hours in a year (52 weeks x 40 hrs) | 2,080 hrs |
| Subtract: Vacation (2-4 weeks) | -80 to -160 hrs |
| Subtract: Public Holidays (10-12 days) | -80 to -96 hrs |
| Subtract: Sick Days (5-10 days) | -40 to -80 hrs |
| Subtract: Administrative Work (emails, invoicing, proposals, accounting) | -200 to -400 hrs |
| Subtract: Marketing & Sales (lead generation, networking, content) | -150 to -300 hrs |
| Subtract: Professional Development (learning, courses, reading) | -100 to -200 hrs |
| Subtract: Buffer for Scope Creep & Overruns (10-15%) | -100 to -200 hrs |
| **Realistic Billable Hours** | **~900 to 1,200 hrs/year** |

**Yes, you read that correctly.** Most successful freelancers bill between 900 and 1,200 hours per year. The rest is eaten by non-billable but essential business activities.

**If you are just starting:** Use 1,000 billable hours. This is a safe, conservative baseline. If you can hit 1,200, you are doing exceptionally well. If you try to bill 2,000 hours, you will burn out within 12 months.

## Step 5: Calculate Your Floor Hourly Rate

Now take your Total Desired Revenue and divide by your billable hours.

**Floor Rate Formula:**

```
Floor Hourly Rate = Total Desired Revenue / Billable Hours
```

**Example:**

| Item | Amount |
|---|---|
| Total Cost of Living | $50,000 |
| Total Business CODB | $15,000 |
| Desired Profit (25%) | $16,250 |
| **Total Desired Revenue** | **$81,250** |
| Billable Hours | 1,000 |
| **Floor Hourly Rate** | **$81.25/hr** |

**If your floor rate is $81.25/hr, you should never accept work below $80/hr.** Every hour you work for less is an hour that moves you backward.

**Common reactions to this number:**
- *"That seems high, nobody will pay that."* — Then you have not built enough value, or you are targeting the wrong clients.
- *"I cannot charge that yet."* — Then you need to reduce costs or increase skills. Do not lower your floor below your actual costs.
- *"I currently charge $30/hr and this says I need $85/hr."* — This tells you exactly why you are burning out. You are subsidizing your clients with your own well-being.

## Market Positioning Relative to Your Floor

Once you know your floor, you can strategically position yourself in the market:

| Tier | Hourly Equivalent | Market Position |
|---|---|---|
| Floor Rate | $81.25 | Bare minimum — only take these rates for strategic reasons (portfolio builder, long-term retainer, equity) |
| Standard Rate | $100-125/hr | Comfortable — covers costs, profit, and some buffer |
| Premium Rate | $150-200/hr | High-value — you are delivering exceptional results and ROI |
| Elite Rate | $250+/hr | Niche expert — you are solving high-stakes problems for high-budget clients |

**Your published rate should never be your floor rate.** Your published rate should be your premium rate. Your floor is for internal decision-making only.

## The "Rate Doubling" Exercise

Here is a proven exercise to test whether your floor rate is realistic:

1. Calculate your floor rate using the method above.
2. Double it.
3. Write down 5 reasons why a client would pay that doubled rate.
4. Write down 5 specific clients or industries where that rate is normal.
5. If you cannot do steps 3 and 4 honestly, you need to either increase your skills or change your target market.

**Example for a web developer at $160/hr (doubled from $80 floor):**
- Reason 1: Building an e-commerce site that will generate $500k+/year in revenue
- Reason 2: Clients in finance, legal, or medical industries expect premium rates
- Reason 3: You provide guaranteed delivery with a contract and SLA
- Reason 4: You include ongoing maintenance and support
- Reason 5: You are faster and make fewer mistakes than a junior developer

## Red Flags: Signs Your Floor Rate Is Wrong

**Too low if:**
- You are consistently overbooked and overwhelmed
- You are dreading client work
- You are not saving any money
- You cannot take a vacation without financial stress
- You are working more than 40 hours/week on billable work

**Too high if:**
- You get no responses to proposals
- You have zero booked projects for more than 60 days
- You are not in the top 10-20% of skill/experience for your niche
- You have no portfolio or social proof at that level

**Correction strategy:** If too high, do not lower your rate. Instead, change your targeting. Find clients who need your specific expertise at your rate level. Lower the scope, not the rate. Offer a smaller package at your standard rate rather than discounting your premium rate.

## Quick Start: 4-Step Rate Setting Protocol

**Step 1:** Calculate your floor rate using the spreadsheet above this week. Do not take any new projects until you know this number.

**Step 2:** Identify your last 3 projects. Calculate what they would have paid at your floor rate vs what you actually charged. If the gap is more than 30%, you have been leaving money on the table.

**Step 3:** Raise your rates for new clients immediately to your floor rate (if below) or your standard rate (if at floor). Existing clients get a 30-day notice of rate increase.

**Step 4:** Every quarter, recalculate your floor rate. As your skills and costs increase, your floor should increase too. If it stays flat for a year, you are stagnating.

## The Psychology of Pricing Resistance

If the thought of charging your calculated floor rate makes you uncomfortable, here is the reframe:

- You are not "charging more." You are charging what your service is worth.
- Clients who cannot afford your floor rate are not your clients. They are a distraction.
- Every hour you spend below your floor rate is an hour you could have spent finding a client who will pay your actual rate.
- The market does not reward "fair" pricing. It rewards value. Price for the value you deliver, not the time you spend.

The world's top freelancers all share one trait: they know their floor rate, and they never go below it. Everything else is negotiation from a position of strength.
