# Project-Based Engagements

## Overview

Project-based pricing means you charge a fixed price for a defined scope of work. The client pays for a completed outcome, not your time. This is the most common entry point for freelancers and the fastest path to $5k-$50k+ deals when structured correctly.

## When to Use Project-Based Pricing

### Ideal Scenarios
- Scope can be clearly defined and bounded
- Requirements are relatively stable
- You have done similar projects before (repeatable delivery)
- Client wants a fixed budget commitment
- You can control the timeline and delivery method

### Avoid When
- Scope is highly ambiguous (use retainer + discovery phase)
- Client expects unlimited revisions (define revision limits)
- Requirements are actively being invented (charge T&M for discovery)
- You cannot accurately estimate effort (add 40-60% buffer)

## The Three-Part Project Structure

### Phase 1: Discovery & Definition (10-15% of project fee)
- Requirements gathering document
- Technical specification / architecture document
- Wireframes or mockups (if applicable)
- Final scope sign-off

**Deliverable:** Signed SOW (Statement of Work)
**Payment trigger:** Upon signature

### Phase 2: Core Delivery (60-70% of project fee)
- Break into 2-4 milestones
- Each milestone is a working, demonstrable increment
- Client feedback windows are time-boxed (48-72 hours)

**Example Milestones for $20k Web App:**
| Milestone | Deliverable | % of Fee |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| M1 | Backend API + Database schema | 20% |
| M2 | Frontend core pages + auth | 20% |
| M3 | Feature complete + integrations | 20% |
| M4 | Polishing + deployment | 10% |

### Phase 3: Launch & Handoff (15-20% of project fee)
- Deployment to production
- Documentation
- Knowledge transfer session(s)
- Warranty period (14-30 days bug fixes)

## Payment Schedules

### Most Profitable: 50/25/25
50% upfront, 25% at midpoint, 25% on completion. Front-loads cash, reduces risk, filters bad clients.

### Standard: 33/33/33
33% start, 33% midpoint, 33% completion. More palatable to cautious clients but slower cash flow.

### Aggressive: 60/30/10
60% upfront, 30% on delivery, 10% after 30-day warranty. Use with established trust or enterprise clients.

### Minimum Acceptable: 40/30/30
Never go below 40% upfront. If a client refuses 40%+ upfront, they are likely a problem client.

## Writing Scope That Protects You

### The Scope Triangle
```
Quality
    ↑
    |   Cost
    |   ↑
    |   |   Time
    |   |   ↑
    +---+---+
```

**The rule:** If the client changes one, the other two must adjust. State this explicitly in your contract.

### Out-of-Scope Triggers
Define exactly what triggers a change order:
- New features not in the SOW
- Design revisions beyond what's specified
- Integration with systems not listed
- Content population beyond sample data
- Browser/device support beyond specified list
- Performance optimization beyond stated targets
- Security hardening beyond baseline

### Revision Limits
- X rounds of revisions per deliverable
- Revisions within scope only
- Additional rounds billed at $X/hr
- Feedback must be consolidated (no piecemeal)

## Pricing Strategies for Project-Based Work

### Bottom-Up (Cost-Plus)
1. Estimate hours: 200 hours
2. Add buffer: 260 hours (30% buffer)
3. Multiply by desired hourly: $150/hr
4. Project price: $39,000

**Warning:** This caps your income. Always try value-based first.

### Value-Based
1. Client's problem solved = $50k/year savings
2. Your solution captures 30% of that value
3. Project price: $15k-20k

**Always anchor with value, negotiate down to price.**

### Competition-Based
Research what competitors charge for similar scope. Price at premium (top 20% of market). Never compete on price — you lose.

## The Proposal Template

```
Title: [Project Name] for [Client Name]

1. UNDERSTANDING
   - Summary of their problem (shows you listened)
   - Impact of the problem (revenue lost, cost incurred)

2. SOLUTION
   - What you will build/deliver
   - How it solves their problem
   - Technology/approach summary

3. DELIVERABLES
   - Bullet list of what they get
   - Timeline (X weeks from signed SOW)

4. Milestones & Payment Schedule
   | Milestone | Amount | Due |
   |-----------|--------|-----|

5. INVESTMENT
   - Total project fee: $XX,XXX
   - What's included / not included
   - Payment terms

6. Next Steps
   - Sign below to accept
   - 50% deposit to start
   - Valid for 14 days
```

## Pros & Cons

### Pros
- Highest earning potential per project ($5k-$100k+)
- Clear boundaries reduce scope creep
- Clients prefer fixed prices (easier to budget)
- You profit from efficiency (finish early, keep same fee)
- Builds portfolio with measurable outcomes
- Easier to sell than hourly billing

### Cons
- You eat overrun costs (if you underestimate)
- Scope creep is constant threat
- Requires strong requirements gathering skills
- Payment is lumpy (not recurring)
- Client may delay sign-off (cash flow hit)
- Harder to maintain long-term relationships

## How to Avoid the #1 Mistake

The #1 mistake is UNDERESTIMATING by 2-3x. Here's the fix:

**The 3x Rule:** Whatever you think it will take, multiply by 3 for the proposal price. Then multiply by 3 again for the initial estimate. Sounds insane? Here's why it works:

- You forget setup time (environments, accounts, tooling)
- You forget communication overhead (emails, calls, status meetings)
- You forget revision time
- You forget testing time (on every device, every browser)
- You forget deployment time
- You forget documentation time
- You forget project management time
- You forget buffer for the client going silent for 2 weeks

**Real example:**
- "It's a simple CRUD app, 40 hours"
- 40 × 3 = 120 hours (internal estimate)
- 120 × $150 = $18,000
- After buffer and revisions: actually takes 140-160 hours
- Effective rate: $112-128/hr (still good)
- Without 3x rule: effective rate would be $37-43/hr (terrible)

## Change Order Process

Make this a formal, documented process:

1. Client requests something new
2. You say "Happy to add that! Here's the impact..."
3. Send a change order document with:
   - Description of change
   - Impact on timeline
   - Additional cost
   - Revised total
4. Client signs change order before work begins
5. Add to scope, adjust schedule

**Sample change order email:**
```
Subject: Change Order — [Feature Name]

Sure, we can add that! Here's what it means:

Additional work needed:
- Backend endpoints: 8 hours
- Frontend components: 12 hours
- Testing: 4 hours
- Total: 24 hours @ $150/hr = $3,600

Timeline impact: +1 week
New completion date: [Date]

Let me know if you'd like to proceed. I'll send over the change order form.
```

## Closing the Deal

### Handling "That's too expensive"

**Bad response:** "How about $X/hr instead?"
**Good response:** "I understand budget is a concern. Let me ask — what number were you hoping for? And what would need to change about the scope to hit that number?"

Then: reduce scope, not rate. Never discount without cutting scope.

### Handling "Can you do it for X?"

If they ask for a discount, offer a trade-off:
- "I can do $15k instead of $20k if we remove the analytics dashboard and deployment. Does that work?"
- "I could do $12k with a 4-week timeline instead of 2 weeks. Sound good?"

### The "Takeaway" Close
"If this isn't the right fit right now, no worries at all. I have capacity opening up in [month] if you want to revisit then."

This signals you're not desperate. Often makes them come back.

## Contract Essentials

Every project-based engagement needs:
1. **Statement of Work (SOW)** — defines scope, deliverables, timeline
2. **Payment schedule** — when and how much
3. **Change order process** — how scope changes are handled
4. **Revision limits** — rounds and timing
5. **Acceptance criteria** — what "done" means for each deliverable
6. **Warranty terms** — bug fixes post-launch (time limit + severity limit)
7. **Termination clause** — what happens if either party cancels
8. **IP transfer** — when ownership transfers (upon full payment)

## Summary Rules

1. 50% minimum upfront (no exceptions)
2. 30% minimum buffer on estimates
3. Define scope in writing before starting
4. Change orders for every addition
5. Limit revisions to 2 rounds
6. Never discount without cutting scope
7. 3x your initial time estimate
8. Front-load payment (more upfront = better)
9. Build Phase 1 (discovery) as paid separate engagement initially
10. Raise price every 3 projects minimum
