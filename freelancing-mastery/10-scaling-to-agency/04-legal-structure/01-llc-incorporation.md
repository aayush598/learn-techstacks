# Business Entity Types: LLC, S-Corp, C-Corp, and International Considerations

## Why Your Entity Structure Matters

Your business entity determines:
- How much tax you pay
- Your personal liability
- How investors and partners see you
- How much paperwork you deal with
- Your ability to scale

Choosing the wrong structure costs you thousands in taxes and legal fees. Choosing the right one saves you money and protects your assets.

## Entity Types Compared

### Sole Proprietorship

**What it is**: You are the business. No separate legal entity.

**Best for**: Solo freelancers making under $50k/year

**Pros**:
- Simplest to set up (no registration needed)
- Lowest cost ($0 to start)
- Simple taxes (Schedule C on personal return)

**Cons**:
- Unlimited personal liability (they can take your house)
- Harder to appear professional
- Cannot have partners or employees easily
- Self-employment tax on ALL income (15.3%)
- Harder to raise money

**Verdict**: Fine for starting out. Upgrade as soon as you hit $30k+/year.

### Limited Liability Company (LLC)

**What it is**: A separate legal entity that protects your personal assets.

**Best for**: Most freelancers and small agencies ($50k–$250k/year)

**Pros**:
- Personal asset protection (lawsuits cannot touch your house)
- Pass-through taxation (no double taxation)
- Flexible (can be taxed as S-Corp later)
- Professional credibility
- Easy to add members
- Relatively simple to maintain

**Cons**:
- Annual fees ($100–$800 depending on state)
- More paperwork than sole prop
- Self-employment tax on ALL income (unless you elect S-Corp taxation)

**Cost to form**: $50–$500 (state filing fees)
**Cost to maintain**: $100–$800/year (state fees + registered agent)

**Best states to form an LLC**:
- **Delaware**: Best for companies planning to raise investment or go public
- **Wyoming**: Low fees ($100/year), no state income tax, strong privacy
- **Nevada**: No state income tax, strong asset protection
- **Your home state**: For most freelancers, this is the simplest and cheapest

**Do NOT form in Delaware unless**:
- You plan to raise venture capital
- You plan to go public
- You have investors who require it
- Your home state is not business-friendly

Forming in Delaware while living elsewhere means paying for a registered agent AND filing as a foreign entity in your home state. Double fees, double paperwork.

### S Corporation (S-Corp)

**What it is**: Not a separate entity type — it is a tax election for an LLC or corporation.

**Best for**: Freelancers and agencies making over $60k–$80k/year

**Pros**:
- **Massive tax savings**: Only pay self-employment tax (15.3%) on your salary, not on all profits
- Example: $150k profit → pay yourself $80k salary → SE tax on $80k only → save ~$10k/year in taxes
- Credibility with larger clients
- Easier to raise money (more familiar to investors)

**Cons**:
- More paperwork (payroll, payroll tax filings)
- Must pay yourself a "reasonable salary"
- Additional accounting costs ($500–$2000/year more)
- Some states do not recognize S-Corp election
- More compliance requirements

**When to upgrade to S-Corp**:
- Profits > $60k/year (breakeven on additional accounting costs)
- You want to minimize self-employment tax
- You have consistent, predictable income

**The S-Corp math**:
```
Without S-Corp (LLC):
Profit: $120,000
Self-employment tax (15.3%): $18,360
Income tax (22% bracket): ~$16,000
Total tax: ~$34,360

With S-Corp:
Profit: $120,000
Salary: $60,000 (reasonable salary)
SE tax on salary: $9,180
Income tax on salary + distributions: ~$15,000
Total tax: ~$24,180

Annual savings: ~$10,000
```

**Downsides of S-Corp**:
- Must run payroll (software like Gusto: $40/mo + $6/person)
- Quarterly payroll tax filings
- Must document board meetings (even if it is just you)
- Cannot have more than 100 shareholders
- Only one class of stock

### C Corporation (C-Corp)

**What it is**: A separate tax-paying entity.

**Best for**: Companies planning to raise venture capital, go public, or have 100+ shareholders

**Pros**:
- Most investor-friendly structure
- Can issue multiple classes of stock
- Unlimited shareholders
- Tax benefits (qualified business income deduction)
- Stock options for employees

**Cons**:
- **Double taxation**: Corporation pays tax on profits, then you pay tax on dividends
- Most expensive to form and maintain
- Most paperwork
- Complex compliance

**Verdict**: Do not choose C-Corp unless you are raising venture capital or have 100+ shareholders. The double taxation kills small businesses.

## Incorporation Process: Step by Step

### Step 1: Choose Your State

For most freelancers: **Form in your home state**.

Consider another state if:
- You live in California (high fees, complex rules)
- You plan to raise venture capital (Delaware)
- Privacy is a priority (Wyoming, Nevada)

### Step 2: Choose Your Entity Type

Decision tree:
```
Revenue < $30k/year → Sole Proprietorship (free, simple)
Revenue $30k–$60k/year → LLC (basic protection)
Revenue $60k+/year → LLC with S-Corp election (tax savings)
Venture capital → C-Corp (usually Delaware)
```

### Step 3: Register Your Business Name

- Check name availability on your state's business registry
- Consider trademarking your name (optional but recommended)
- Get a domain name that matches

**Naming rules**:
- Must include "LLC" or "Limited Liability Company"
- Cannot be confusingly similar to existing businesses
- Cannot use restricted words (bank, insurance, etc.)

### Step 4: File Formation Documents

**Articles of Organization** (called different things in different states):
- Business name and address
- Registered agent information
- Purpose of business (usually "any lawful business")
- Management structure (member-managed or manager-managed)
- Organizer signature

**Filing methods**:
- Online: Fastest (usually 1-3 business days)
- Mail: Slowest (2-6 weeks)
- Expedited: Extra fee for faster processing

### Step 5: Get an EIN

**Employer Identification Number (EIN)** from the IRS:
- Free
- Can apply online at IRS.gov
- Takes 15 minutes
- Required to open bank accounts, hire employees, file taxes

### Step 6: Create an Operating Agreement

**Not required in all states, but ALWAYS have one.**

An Operating Agreement covers:
- Ownership percentages
- Profit distribution
- Management structure
- Member responsibilities
- Decision-making process
- Dispute resolution
- Buy-sell provisions
- Dissolution process

Even for single-member LLCs, an operating agreement proves you are treating your business as a separate entity (important for liability protection).

### Step 7: Open Business Bank Accounts

- **Business checking account**: Separate all business transactions
- **Business savings account**: Set aside taxes (25-30% of every payment)
- **Business credit card**: Build business credit, track expenses

**Why separate accounts are critical**:
- Piercing the corporate veil (mixing personal and business = losing liability protection)
- Clean bookkeeping
- Professional appearance
- Easier tax preparation

### Step 8: Get Business Licenses and Permits

Check requirements for:
- **City**: Business license
- **State**: Professional license (if applicable)
- **Federal**: Industry-specific licenses

### Step 9: Register for State Taxes

- State income tax withholding (if you have employees)
- Sales tax (if you sell taxable products)
- Unemployment insurance tax

### Step 10: Set Up Accounting

- QuickBooks or Freshbooks
- Connect bank accounts
- Set up invoice templates
- Create expense categories
- Schedule monthly bookkeeping time

## S-Corp Election Process

If you choose S-Corp taxation:

1. Form LLC (or corporation)
2. File Form 2553 with IRS (within 75 days of formation or by March 15 of the tax year)
3. Set up payroll (Gusto, ADP, or Paychex)
4. Determine your "reasonable salary"
5. File quarterly payroll taxes
6. File Form 1120-S annually

**Reasonable salary**:
- Must be comparable to what you would pay someone else to do your job
- Rule of thumb: 50-60% of your business profit
- Too low = IRS audits
- Too high = no tax benefit
- There is no fixed formula, use salary surveys for your role

## International Considerations

### US Resident, International Clients

- Same entity considerations apply
- Consider a US-based LLC
- Use Wise or Deel for international payments
- Check tax treaties (some payments may be exempt from US tax)

### Non-US Resident, US Clients

- You do NOT need a US entity
- Most US companies will pay you as an independent contractor
- Consider a US LLC if:
  - You work with many US clients (professional appearance)
  - You want a US bank account
  - You need to hire US-based contractors
  
**Non-resident US LLC considerations**:
- You need an EIN (apply with Form SS-4 or through a service)
- You need a US registered agent
- You need a US bank account (can be done remotely with some banks)
- You file Form 5472 annually (non-resident owned US entity)
- You may be subject to US withholding tax (30% on certain payments)
- Your home country may tax the LLC differently

### Hiring International Contractors

When you hire contractors in other countries:
- No need to set up entity in their country
- Use Deel or Remote for compliant payments
- Ensure they are classified as contractors (not employees)
- Have them sign a contractor agreement
- Collect W-8BEN or W-9 for tax reporting

## Annual Compliance Requirements

### LLC

| Requirement | Frequency | Details |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| Annual report | Yearly | Filed with state ($50–$800) |
| Registered agent | Ongoing | ~$100–$300/year |
| Tax return | Yearly | Schedule C or Form 1065 |
| Business license renewal | Yearly | Varies by city |

### S-Corp

All of the above, plus:
| Requirement | Frequency | Details |
|-------------|-----------|---------|
| Payroll tax filings | Quarterly | Form 941 |
| Payroll tax payments | Monthly or semi-weekly | Depends on amount |
| Unemployment tax | Quarterly | Form 940 |
| Income tax return | Yearly | Form 1120-S |
| W-2/W-3 | Yearly | For yourself and employees |
| Reasonable salary documentation | Ongoing | Document your salary rationale |

## Common Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Forming an LLC too early**
If you make under $10k/year, an LLC is unnecessary. The fees eat into your revenue. Wait until you have consistent income.

**Mistake 2: Forming an LLC too late**
If you have significant assets (house, savings, investments) and no liability protection, you are exposed. Form your LLC before you have assets to protect.

**Mistake 3: Forming in Delaware for no reason**
Delaware is great for investors. For most freelancers, it is expensive and unnecessary.

**Mistake 4: Not having an Operating Agreement**
Even single-member LLCs need one. It proves you are a legitimate business.

**Mistake 5: Mixing personal and business finances**
This pierces the corporate veil. Keep everything separate. Every transaction must be business-only in business accounts.

**Mistake 6: Not setting aside taxes**
Save 25-30% of every payment for taxes. Put it in a separate savings account. Do not touch it.

**Mistake 7: Avoiding S-Corp election**
If you make $80k+/year and are not an S-Corp, you are paying thousands in unnecessary taxes.

## When to Hire a Lawyer

Hire a lawyer when:
- You are forming a partnership (multiple owners)
- You are raising investment
- You have complex IP concerns
- You are hiring employees
- Your business is >$250k/year revenue
- You are forming a C-Corp

For a simple single-member LLC, you can use LegalZoom, ZenBusiness, or do it yourself through your state's website. Total cost: $100–$500.

Choose your entity based on revenue: sole prop for under $30k, LLC for $30k–$60k, LLC with S-Corp for $60k+. Form in your home state unless you have a specific reason not to. Keep business and personal finances completely separate. Set aside 25-30% for taxes. If you are making $80k+ as a sole prop or LLC, you are paying thousands in unnecessary taxes — elect S-Corp taxation. The right entity structure saves you money and protects your assets. The wrong one costs you both.
