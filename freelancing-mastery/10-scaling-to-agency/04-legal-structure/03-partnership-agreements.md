# Partnership Agreements: Co-Founder, Revenue Sharing, and Exit Clauses

## Why Partnership Agreements Matter

Partnerships fail for one reason: unclear expectations. You assume one thing, they assume another, and six months later there is resentment, conflict, and a broken business.

A good partnership agreement does not mean you do not trust each other. It means you respect each other enough to be clear about everything upfront. The best time to write a partnership agreement is when you still like each other.

## The Cost of Not Having an Agreement

- Ambiguous decision-making (who has the final say?)
- Unclear profit sharing (who gets what?)
- Uneven contribution (one person does 80% of the work)
- No exit plan (how does someone leave?)
- Broken relationships (friendships, professional relationships)
- Legal battles (costing $10k-$100k+ and months of time)

A 1-hour conversation upfront saves years of pain and tens of thousands of dollars.

## Types of Partnership Arrangements

### 50/50 Partnership (Equal Ownership)

**Best for**: True co-founders with equal contributions and equal roles

**Structure**:
- 50% ownership each
- Equal profit distribution
- Equal decision-making (or tie-breaking mechanism)
- Equal capital contributions

**Risks**:
- Deadlock (you disagree, no one can overrule)
- Uneven contributions over time
- Resentment if one person outworks the other

**Mitigation**:
- Tie-breaking mechanism (third-party mediator, rotating tie-breaker)
- Vesting schedules (earn your equity over time)
- Regular check-ins on contribution balance

### Uneven Partnership (Split Ownership)

**Best for**: Different contribution levels (one person brings capital, the other brings labor)

**Structure**:
- Example: 60/40, 70/30, 80/20
- Based on contribution value (cash, time, expertise, network)
- Can change over time with milestones

**Pros**:
- Reflects actual contribution
- No deadlock (majority vote wins)
- More flexible

**Cons**:
- Harder to agree on split percentages
- Can feel unfair to the minority partner

### Revenue Share Partnership (No Equity)

**Best for**: Collaborators who want to work together without forming a legal entity

**Structure**:
- No ownership in the business
- Each partner gets a % of revenue/profit from specific projects or clients
- Each partner maintains their own business
- No long-term commitment

**Pros**:
- Simple to set up
- No legal entity needed
- Easy to end
- No equity complications

**Cons**:
- Less commitment
- Each partner may prioritize their own clients
- Less cohesive brand

### Project-Based Partnership

**Best for**: One-time or limited-time collaborations

**Structure**:
- Joint effort on a specific project
- Shared revenue for that project only
- Defined scope and timeline
- Each partner handles their own expenses

**Pros**:
- Low commitment
- Test working together before committing
- Clear end date

**Cons**:
- Less aligned incentives
- Harder to build a joint brand

## Essential Elements of a Partnership Agreement

### 1. Ownership and Equity

- Each partner's ownership percentage
- How equity is earned (vesting schedule)
- What happens if someone leaves before fully vested
- How new equity is issued

### 2. Capital Contributions

- How much money each partner contributes
- What happens if more capital is needed
- How capital is returned (if ever)

### 3. Profit Distribution

- How profits are split (percentage)
- When profits are distributed (monthly, quarterly, annually)
- What expenses come off the top before distribution
- Reserve requirements (how much profit stays in the business)

### 4. Roles and Responsibilities

- Each partner's specific role
- Time commitment expected (full-time, part-time)
- Decision-making authority
- Who handles what

### 5. Decision-Making

- Day-to-day decisions (each partner has autonomy)
- Major decisions (requires unanimous approval)
- List of major decisions:
  - Taking on debt
  - Hiring/firing
  - Large expenses (>$X)
  - New service lines
  - Partnership changes
  - Selling the business

### 6. Compensation

- Salary (if any) vs. profit distribution
- How salaries are determined
- Benefits (health insurance, retirement)
- Expense reimbursement

### 7. Intellectual Property

- Who owns what IP created before the partnership
- Who owns IP created during the partnership (the business)
- What happens to IP if the partnership dissolves

### 8. Non-Compete and Non-Solicit

- Can partners compete with the business after leaving?
- Can partners poach clients?
- Can partners poach employees?
- Duration and geographic scope

### 9. Performance and Contribution

- What happens if a partner is not contributing
- How contribution is measured
- Remediation process (warnings, improvement plans)
- Consequences for continued underperformance

### 10. Exit and Termination

- Voluntary exit (how to leave)
- Involuntary removal (for cause, without cause)
- Buy-sell provisions (how to value and buy out a departing partner)
- Death or disability provisions

### 11. Dispute Resolution

- Step 1: Direct conversation between partners
- Step 2: Mediation (third-party mediator)
- Step 3: Arbitration (binding)
- Step 4: Litigation (last resort)

### 12. Dissolution

- How to wind down the business
- How to distribute remaining assets
- How to handle debts and obligations

## Vesting Schedules

Equity should be earned over time. Never give someone 50% equity on day one.

### Standard Vesting Schedule

- **Cliff**: 1 year (if you leave before 1 year, you get 0% equity)
- **Vesting**: 4 years total (25% after year 1, then monthly vesting)
- **Total**: 100% after 4 years

### Why Vesting Matters

- Prevents someone from leaving after 3 months with 50% of the business
- Ensures commitment
- Aligns incentives over the long term
- Attracts serious partners

### Custom Vesting for Different Contributions

- Full-time commitment: 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff
- Part-time commitment: Extended vesting (5-6 years)
- Investor partner: Different structure (convertible note, SAFE)

## Buy-Sell Agreements

A buy-sell agreement determines how to value and buy out a departing partner.

### Valuation Methods

**Book Value**: Assets minus liabilities. Simple but may not reflect true value.

**Multiple of Earnings**: 2-5x annual profit. Most common for service businesses.

**Appraised Value**: Third-party valuation. Most accurate but expensive.

**Agreed Value**: Partners agree on a value each year and update it.

**Formula-Based**: Pre-determined formula (e.g., 3x average of last 3 years profit).

### Funding the Buyout

**Cash**: Pay from business cash reserves.

**Seller Financing**: Pay over time (e.g., 3 years, with interest).

**Life Insurance**: For death or disability buyouts.

**Third-Party Loan**: Business borrows to fund the buyout.

### Triggering Events

- **Voluntary departure**: Vested equity valued at agreed method, paid over 2-4 years
- **Involuntary termination for cause**: Buyout at discount (50-70% of value)
- **Death**: Full value to estate, funded by life insurance
- **Disability**: Partial value, funded by disability insurance
- **Retirement**: Full value, paid over agreed timeline

## Revenue Sharing Agreements

For simpler partnerships without equity.

### Revenue Share Terms

- **Share percentage**: 10-50% of revenue, depending on contribution
- **Revenue definition**: Gross revenue or net revenue (after expenses?)
- **Expense allocation**: Who pays for what?
- **Calculation frequency**: Monthly, quarterly, per-project
- **Payment terms**: Net 15, Net 30
- **Duration**: Ongoing or limited

### Revenue Share Agreement Template

```
Revenue Share Agreement

Parties: [Partner A] and [Partner B]

Effective Date: [Date]

1. Revenue Definition: Revenue means [gross/net] revenue from [specific projects/clients]
2. Share: Partner A receives [X%] of Revenue, Partner B receives [Y%] of Revenue
3. Expenses: [Each party bears their own expenses / Expenses are shared proportionally]
4. Payment: Within [15/30] days of revenue receipt
5. Reporting: [Monthly/Quarterly] accounting of revenue and payments
6. Duration: [Ongoing / Until completion of project / 12 months with renewal]
7. Termination: [30 days written notice / Immediate for breach]
8. Relationship: Independent contractors, not partners or employees
```

## Common Partnership Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Equal partnership for unequal contribution**
One person works full-time, the other part-time. Both get 50%. This breeds resentment. The person working more will eventually feel taken advantage of.

**Fix**: Vesting based on contribution. Or different ownership percentages.

**Mistake 2: No vesting**
Giving someone 50% on day one. They leave in 3 months with 50% of your business. You built everything, they own half.

**Fix**: 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff. No equity until they earn it.

**Mistake 3: Avoiding hard conversations**
Not discussing what happens if someone leaves, stops contributing, or wants to sell. Avoiding the conversation now means having it when emotions are high.

**Fix**: Have the hard conversations on day one. Put everything in writing.

**Mistake 4: Verbal agreements**
"You can trust me, we do not need a contract." Famous last words. Memories differ. Emotions change.

**Fix**: Everything in writing. Signed by both parties.

**Mistake 5: No dispute resolution**
When you disagree, there is no process. The stronger personality wins. Resentment builds.

**Fix**: Agree on a dispute resolution process upfront.

**Mistake 6: No exit plan**
You cannot imagine the partnership ending. But 50%+ of partnerships do. Plan for it.

**Fix**: Define buy-sell terms, valuation method, and process.

**Mistake 7: Different expectations**
One person wants a lifestyle business. The other wants to build an empire and sell. Inevitable conflict.

**Fix**: Align on vision, goals, and exit strategy upfront.

**Mistake 8: No regular check-ins**
You assume everything is fine. It is not. Small issues grow into big problems.

**Fix**: Monthly partnership meetings. Quarterly offsites. Annual reviews.

## Partnership Meeting Structure

### Monthly Check-in (30-60 minutes)

- Revenue and profit update
- Pipeline and sales
- Active project status
- Issues and concerns
- Decisions needed

### Quarterly Review (2 hours)

- Goal progress
- Financial review
- Partner satisfaction (1-10 scale)
- Contribution assessment
- Upcoming priorities

### Annual Offsite (Half day)

- Year in review
- Strategic planning
- Partnership health check
- Agreement review and amendment
- Goal setting for next year

## The Partnership Health Check

Rate each on a scale of 1-10 every quarter:

- **Trust**: Do I trust my partner's decisions and intentions?
- **Communication**: Are we communicating openly and honestly?
- **Contribution**: Is my partner carrying their weight?
- **Alignment**: Are we aligned on goals and priorities?
- **Respect**: Do I respect my partner's skills and judgment?
- **Enjoyment**: Do I enjoy working with my partner?

If any score drops below 7, address it immediately.

## When to Lawyer Up

You do not need a lawyer for a simple revenue share agreement. You DO need a lawyer for:

- Formal partnership with equity
- Complex buy-sell provisions
- Significant IP considerations
- Large capital contributions
- International partners

Cost: $1,000-$5,000 for a good partnership agreement. Worth every penny.

## Summary

The best partnership is one built on clear agreements. Define ownership, roles, profit distribution, decision-making, and exit terms upfront. Use vesting schedules to ensure commitment. Plan for the end at the beginning. Have regular check-ins to maintain alignment. If you are in a partnership without a written agreement, stop everything and fix it today. The conversation is uncomfortable for 2 hours. The alternative is years of pain.
