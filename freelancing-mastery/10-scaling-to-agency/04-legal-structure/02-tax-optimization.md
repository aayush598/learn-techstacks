# Tax Strategies for Freelancers and Agencies

## The Tax Mindset

Tax is your biggest expense. At $100k revenue, you pay $20k-$35k in taxes. At $500k, you pay $100k-$175k. Tax optimization is not about evasion - it is about using the laws as intended to keep more of what you earn.

The goal: Pay the legal minimum. Not a dollar more.

## Understanding Your Tax Burden

### Self-Employment Tax

As a freelancer, you pay BOTH the employee and employer portions of Social Security and Medicare:

- Employee portion: 7.65%
- Employer portion: 7.65%
- Total: 15.3% on your first $160k of net earnings

This is on top of income tax. A W-2 employee only pays 7.65%. Your 15.3% means for the first $50k of profit, you pay $7,650 in SE tax alone.

### Income Tax Brackets (2025, Single Filer)

| Taxable Income | Rate |
|----------------|------|
| $0-$11,925 | 10% |
| $11,925-$48,475 | 12% |
| $48,475-$103,350 | 22% |
| $103,350-$197,300 | 24% |
| $197,300-$250,525 | 32% |
| $250,525+ | 35% |

### Self-Employment Tax + Income Tax Combined Effective Rate

| Net Profit | Effective Tax Rate (approx) |
|------------|---------------------------|
| $30,000 | ~10% |
| $50,000 | ~15% |
| $80,000 | ~20% |
| $120,000 | ~25% |
| $200,000 | ~30% |

## Strategy 1: S-Corp Election

This is the single biggest tax savings strategy for freelancers.

**How it works**: Instead of paying 15.3% SE tax on ALL your profit, you pay yourself a "reasonable salary" and take the rest as distributions. Distributions are NOT subject to self-employment tax.

### The S-Corp Math

**Without S-Corp (LLC or Sole Prop)**:
```
Profit: $120,000
Self-employment tax (15.3%): $18,360
Income tax (22% bracket, after deductions): ~$16,000
Total federal tax: ~$34,360
Effective rate: 28.6%
```

**With S-Corp**:
```
Profit: $120,000
Reasonable salary: $70,000
SE tax on salary (15.3%): $10,710
Distributions: $50,000 (no SE tax)
Income tax on salary + distributions: ~$15,000
Total federal tax: ~$25,710
Effective rate: 21.4%
```

**Annual savings: ~$8,650**

### When S-Corp Makes Sense

- Net profit > $60k/year (breakeven on additional costs)
- You want to minimize self-employment tax
- You have consistent, predictable income

### S-Corp Costs

- Payroll software: $40-80/month (Gusto, ADP)
- Additional accounting fees: $500-$1,500/year
- Payroll tax filings: quarterly
- Reasonable salary documentation

**Breakeven**: At $60k profit, you save about $2,000 in SE tax. At $80k, you save about $4,000. At $120k, you save about $8,000. The breakeven is around $60k after accounting for additional costs.

### Reasonable Salary Guidelines

The IRS requires you to pay yourself a "reasonable salary" for the work you perform. Too low = IRS audit. Too high = no tax benefit.

**How to determine reasonable salary**:
- Use salary surveys (BLS, Glassdoor, Payscale)
- Consider your role, experience, and location
- Rule of thumb: 40-60% of your business profit
- Document how you arrived at the number

**Example reasonable salaries by role**:
- Web developer: $60k-$100k
- Designer: $50k-$80k
- Writer/Content: $40k-$70k
- Consultant: $80k-$150k

**What triggers IRS scrutiny on salary**:
- Salary below $30k (regardless of profit)
- Salary less than 20% of profit
- No salary at all (distributions only)
- Wide swings in salary year to year

## Strategy 2: Maximize Business Deductions

Every dollar of deduction saves you 20-40 cents in taxes. Maximize legitimate deductions.

### Home Office Deduction

**You qualify if**: You have a dedicated space used regularly and exclusively for business.

**Two methods**:

**Simplified method**: $5 per square foot, up to 300 sq ft = $1,500 deduction. Simple, no depreciation recapture.

**Regular method**: Actual expenses (mortgage interest, utilities, insurance, repairs) × business use percentage. Requires more paperwork but often yields a larger deduction.

**Example**:
- Home is 2,000 sq ft, office is 200 sq ft
- Business use: 10%
- Total housing costs: $24,000/year
- Deduction: $2,400 (vs $1,000 simplified)

### Vehicle Deduction

**Standard mileage rate** (2025): ~$0.67/mile for business miles.

**Actual expense method**: Track gas, maintenance, insurance, depreciation, etc.

**Which is better**: Standard mileage is simpler and often better for newer cars. Actual expenses is better for older cars or high-cost vehicles.

**Tracking**: Use MileIQ, Everlance, or a logbook. The IRS requires contemporaneous records.

### Equipment and Software

**Section 179**: Deduct the FULL cost of equipment in the year you buy it (up to $1.16M).

**Bonus depreciation**: Deduct 80% (2025) of qualifying property.

**Examples**:
- Computers and laptops
- Monitors and peripherals
- Office furniture
- Software subscriptions (monthly or annual)
- Phones and tablets

**Strategy**: If you need equipment, buy it before year-end to maximize current year deductions.

### Health Insurance

If you are self-employed, you can deduct 100% of health insurance premiums for yourself, your spouse, and dependents.

**Requirements**:
- Must have net profit from self-employment
- Cannot be eligible for employer-subsidized insurance
- Deduct on Form 1040 (above-the-line deduction)

### Retirement Plans

The best tax-saving strategy for high-income freelancers.

**SEP IRA**:
- Contribute up to 25% of net earnings (max $66k in 2025)
- Simple to set up (any brokerage)
- Must contribute equal % for employees

**Solo 401(k)**:
- Contribute as employee ($23k in 2025, + $7.5k if over 50)
- PLUS up to 25% as employer (total up to $69k)
- Can do Roth contributions
- Higher contribution limits than SEP IRA

**SEP IRA vs Solo 401(k)**:

| Feature | SEP IRA | Solo 401(k) |
|---------|---------|-------------|
| Contribution limit | 25% of profit | Up to $69k |
| Employee contribution | No | Yes ($23k) |
| Roth option | No | Yes |
| Loan option | No | Yes |
| Complexity | Simple | Moderate |

**Strategy**: Use Solo 401(k) if you want to maximize contributions. Use SEP IRA for simplicity. Max out your retirement contributions before year-end.

### Travel and Meals

**Business travel**: 100% deductible (flights, hotels, rental cars, etc.)

**Meals**: 50% deductible when meeting with clients or prospects.

**Strategy**: If you travel for client work, conferences, or networking, track every expense. Consider a "work from anywhere" trip where you combine business and pleasure - the business portion is deductible.

## Strategy 3: Tax-Loss Harvesting

If you have investments in taxable accounts, sell losing positions to offset capital gains. You can deduct up to $3,000 of capital losses against ordinary income each year.

## Strategy 4: Income Shifting

If you have a spouse or family member who can legitimately work for your business:
- Pay them a reasonable salary
- They can contribute to their own retirement account
- Shifts income from your higher bracket to their lower bracket

**Requirements**: They must perform actual work. Reasonable compensation. Document the arrangement.

## Strategy 5: Pay Family Members

If your children work for your business:
- Pay them a reasonable wage for actual work
- Up to $13,850 (2025 standard deduction) tax-free for them
- You deduct the wages
- They can contribute to a Roth IRA

**Requirements**: Actual work performed. Reasonable wages. Proper documentation (time sheets, job descriptions).

## Strategy 6: Quarterly Estimated Taxes

### Why Pay Quarterly

- Avoid underpayment penalties
- Manage cash flow
- Avoid a huge April surprise

### How to Calculate

1. Estimate your annual net profit
2. Calculate total tax (income + SE)
3. Divide by 4
4. Pay by: April 15, June 15, September 15, January 15

### Safe Harbor Rule

Pay 100% of last year's tax (110% if AGI > $150k) and avoid penalties even if you owe more.

### Payment Method

- IRS Direct Pay (free, easy)
- EFTPS (Treasury's payment system)
- Credit card (2% fee - not worth it)

## Strategy 7: Hire an Accountant

### What an Accountant Saves You

- Identify deductions you miss
- Entity structure optimization
- Tax planning throughout the year
- Audit protection and representation
- Time and stress

### Cost-Benefit

If your accountant costs $2,000/year and saves you $10,000 in taxes: 5x ROI.

**When to hire an accountant**:
- Revenue > $50k/year
- You have an S-Corp or C-Corp
- You have employees
- You are confused about taxes
- You are spending more than 5 hours/month on tax compliance

### How to Find a Good Accountant

- Ask other freelancers for referrals
- Look for CPAs with self-employment experience
- Interview 3 before choosing
- Ask about their experience with similar businesses

**Questions to ask**:
- "How many self-employed clients do you work with?"
- "What is your experience with S-Corp taxation?"
- "How do you handle tax planning (not just tax filing)?"
- "What is your fee structure?"
- "Do you offer audit support?"

## Strategy 8: Entity Structure for Tax Optimization

### Tax by Entity Type

| Entity | SE Tax | Income Tax | Complexity |
|--------|--------|------------|------------|
| Sole Prop | 15.3% on all profit | Pass-through | Low |
| LLC | 15.3% on all profit | Pass-through | Low |
| S-Corp | 15.3% on salary only | Pass-through | Medium |
| C-Corp | No SE tax | Corporate + personal | High |

### Comparison at $100k Profit

| Entity | Tax (approx) | Left Over |
|--------|-------------|-----------|
| Sole Prop / LLC | $25,000 | $75,000 |
| LLC with S-Corp | $19,000 | $81,000 |
| C-Corp | $22,000 (corp + personal) | $78,000 |

### State Tax Considerations

- **No state income tax**: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, Wyoming
- **High state income tax**: California (13.3%), Hawaii (11%), New York (10.9%), Oregon (9.9%)
- **State-specific S-Corp rules**: Some states do not recognize S-Corp election (NYC, some others)

If you live in a high-tax state, consider:
- Moving (long-term)
- Maximizing deductions
- Using retirement accounts

## Strategy 9: Timing Income and Expenses

### Defer Income

If you expect to be in a lower bracket next year:
- Delay sending invoices in December
- Delay closing deals until January
- Push revenue into next year

### Accelerate Expenses

If you want to reduce this year's tax:
- Buy equipment before year-end
- Prepay subscriptions
- Make estimated tax payments
- Contribute to retirement accounts

## Strategy 10: The Solo 401(k) Mega Backdoor

If you have a Solo 401(k) that allows after-tax contributions:
- Contribute up to $69k total
- $23k employee + 25% employer + after-tax contribution
- Roll after-tax contributions to Roth
- Tax-free growth on a significant portion of your retirement savings

This strategy requires a Solo 401(k) that specifically allows it (not all do. Check with your provider.

## Common Tax Mistakes

**Mistake 1: Not setting aside taxes**
Save 25-30% of every payment. Put it in a separate account. Do not touch it.

**Mistake 2: Missing quarterly payments**
The penalty is small but unnecessary. Set up automatic quarterly payments.

**Mistake 3: Not tracking expenses**
Every deduction you miss is money you overpay. Track everything. Even small expenses add up.

**Mistake 4: Personal vs business confusion**
Commingling funds causes problems. Keep everything separate.

**Mistake 5: Not taking the home office deduction**
Worried about audit? The risk is low. Take the deduction you deserve. Use the simplified method if you are nervous.

**Mistake 6: Overpaying for tax software**
If your return is simple, Free Fillable Forms or TurboTax works. If your return is complex, hire a CPA. The middle ground (expensive software) is often the worst option.

**Mistake 7: Filing extensions unnecessarily**
Extension to file is not an extension to pay. You still owe penalties on late payments.

**Mistake 8: Not working with a tax professional**
The tax code is 70,000+ pages. You cannot master it on your own. Professional help pays for itself.

## Annual Tax Planning Timeline

### January
- Review previous year's tax return
- Set up quarterly payment schedule
- Make prior year retirement contributions (if applicable)

### March
- Gather tax documents (1099s, expenses)
- Send documents to accountant
- File if you have a refund coming

### April 15
- File tax return or extension
- Make first quarter estimated payment
- Make prior year IRA contributions

### June 15
- Second quarter estimated payment
- Review year-to-date profit
- Adjust quarterly payment amount

### September 15
- Third quarter estimated payment
- Review year-to-date profit
- Start planning for year-end tax moves

### December
- Fourth quarter planning
- Buy equipment if needed
- Make charitable contributions
- Max out retirement contributions
- Review business structure for next year

## Summary

Tax optimization is the highest-ROI activity you can do. Every dollar saved in taxes is a dollar in your pocket. The biggest wins: S-Corp election (saves 3-6% of profit), retirement contributions (saves 20-30% of contribution), and expense tracking (saves 20-40% of expenses). Hire a CPA when you hit $50k revenue. The cost of good tax advice is 5-10x less than the tax savings it generates. Pay the legal minimum - not a dollar more.
