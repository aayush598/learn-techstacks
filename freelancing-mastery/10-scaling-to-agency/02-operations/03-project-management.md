# Project Management for Agencies: Multi-Project, Resource Allocation, and Profitability

## The Agency PM Challenge

Running an agency means running 5-20 projects simultaneously, each at different stages, with different clients, budgets, and deadlines. Without a system, you will:
- Miss deadlines on profitable projects while firefighting
- Overallocate your best people, causing burnout
- Underallocate, causing idle time and lost revenue
- Lose money on projects you thought were profitable

Good agency project management is the difference between 20% and 40% profit margins.

## The Three Pillars of Agency PM

1. **Multi-Project Management**: Visibility and control across all projects
2. **Resource Allocation**: The right people on the right projects at the right time
3. **Profitability Tracking**: Knowing exactly how much money each project makes

## Pillar 1: Multi-Project Management

### The Portfolio View

You need a single dashboard that shows every project, its status, budget, and health.

**The Project Dashboard should show**:
- Project name and client
- Current status (On Track, At Risk, Behind)
- Budget remaining ($ and %)
- Timeline status (ahead, on track, behind)
- Next milestone and its date
- Assigned team members
- Client satisfaction (1-5 scale)

**Project status definitions**:
- **On Track**: Within budget, on schedule, client happy. No action needed.
- **At Risk**: Approaching budget limit, slipping timeline, or client concerns. Needs attention.
- **Behind**: Over budget, late, or client issues. Requires immediate intervention.

### Project Sprints or Phases

Break every project into phases or sprints. Each phase should be:
- **Defined scope**: Exactly what will be delivered
- **Fixed timeline**: 1-4 weeks per phase
- **Fixed budget**: How many hours or dollars allocated
- **Review gate**: Client approval before moving to next phase

**Phase structure example**:

```
Project: Client Website Redesign
├── Phase 1: Discovery (1 week)
│   ├── Stakeholder interviews
│   ├── Current site audit
│   └── Requirements document
├── Phase 2: Design (2 weeks)
│   ├── Wireframes
│   ├── Visual design
│   └── Design approval
├── Phase 3: Development (4 weeks)
│   ├── Frontend build
│   ├── Backend build
│   └── QA testing
├── Phase 4: Launch (1 week)
│   ├── Migration
│   ├── Testing
│   └── Go live
└── Phase 5: Support (2 weeks)
    ├── Bug fixes
    ├── Training
    └── Handoff
```

### Project Templates

Create project templates for your most common project types. Each template defines:
- Standard phases
- Typical hours per phase
- Required roles
- Common deliverables
- Quality checkpoints
- Client touchpoints

**Template example: Standard Website Build**

| Design | 60 | Designer, PM | 2 weeks |

| Content | 40 | Writer, PM | 2 weeks (parallel) |
| Launch | 20 | Dev, PM | 1 week |

### Project Intake Process

Every new project goes through a standardized intake process:

1. **Intake form**: Client fills out a standardized form (scope, timeline, budget, requirements)
2. **Triage**: PM reviews and assigns priority
3. **Resource check**: Are the right people available?
4. **Capacity check**: Does the team have bandwidth?
5. **Project setup**: Create project in PM tool, set up folders, assign team
6. **Kickoff**: First meeting with team and client

### Client Communication Structure

Standardized communication prevents surprises:

**Weekly status report template**:
```
Project: [Name]
Week ending: [Date]
Status: [Green/Yellow/Red]

Accomplished this week:
- [List key accomplishments]

Next week's priorities:
- [List upcoming work]

Blockers:
- [List any issues needing attention]

Budget: $[spent] of $[total] ([%] remaining)
Timeline: [on track / at risk / behind]
```

**Status report guidelines**:
- Send every Friday by 3 PM
- Keep to 5 bullet points max
- Flag issues early (at risk before behind)
- Include budget and timeline health

## Pillar 2: Resource Allocation

### The Resource Grid

A resource allocation grid shows who is working on what, how many hours they are allocated, and when they have availability.

**Resource grid structure**:
```
Team Member | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Total | Utilization
------------|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-------|-------------
John (Dev)  | P1  | P1  | P2  | P2  | P3  | 35hr  | 88%
Sarah (Dev) | P1  | P1  | P1  | P2  | -   | 32hr  | 80%
Mike (PM)   | P1  | P2  | P3  | P3  | P1  | 40hr  | 100%
```

**Target utilization rates**:
- **Developers/Designers**: 75-85% billable (rest is training, meetings, admin)
- **Project Managers**: 60-70% billable (more time on coordination)
- **Sales/Leadership**: 20-40% billable (rest is sales, strategy, management)

Above 90% utilization = burnout risk. Below 60% = financial inefficiency.

### Capacity Planning

**Weekly capacity calculation**:
1. Team member available hours: 40 hours/week
2. Subtract non-billable: meetings (5), training (3), admin (2) = 10 hours
3. Billable capacity: 30 hours/week per person
4. Team of 4: 120 billable hours/week
5. Projected workload: sum of all project hours needed this week

If projected workload > capacity, you need to:
- Push non-urgent projects
- Hire additional resources
- Renegotiate deadlines
- Decline new work

### Resource Booking

Reserve resources before you book projects. The booking system:

1. **Project estimate created**: Resources and hours estimated
2. **Preliminary booking**: Tentative hold on resources
3. **Contract signed**: Firm booking confirmed
4. **Resource locked**: Schedule finalized

**Booking rules**:
- No project can be accepted without confirmed resource availability
- Projects get booked in order of priority (revenue, strategic value, deadline)
- Override only with leadership approval

### Dealing with Resource Conflicts

When two projects need the same resource:

1. **Prioritize by revenue**: Higher revenue project gets the resource
2. **Prioritize by relationship**: Long-term client over one-off
3. **Prioritize by deadline**: Sooner deadline gets priority
4. **Negotiate with client**: Can the timeline shift?
5. **Bring in external help**: Can we subcontract?
6. **Escalate to leadership**: Make the call

### The Bench

Maintain a "bench" of available resources:
- Internal: people between projects (3-5% of team)
- External: freelancers you can call on short notice
- Strategy: use the bench for internal projects, R&D, and sales support

A bench costs money but enables agility. The cost of idle bench time is less than the cost of turning down a $50k project because you lacked resources.

## Pillar 3: Profitability Tracking

### True Cost Calculation

Most agencies track revenue but not true cost. Here is how to calculate real profitability:

**Project cost = Direct labor + Overhead allocation + Tools + Subcontractors**

**Direct labor**: Hours spent × hourly cost (not bill rate, actual cost including taxes, benefits, etc.)
**Overhead allocation**: % of rent, software, management, admin
**Tools**: Project-specific software costs
**Subcontractors**: What you pay external help

**Example**:
```
Project revenue: $20,000
Direct labor (80hr × $50/hr cost): $4,000
Overhead allocation (15%): $3,000
Tools: $200
Subcontractors: $2,000
Total cost: $9,200
Gross profit: $10,800 (54% margin)
```

### Profitability Metrics

Track these for every project:

**Gross Margin**: (Revenue - Direct Costs) / Revenue
- Target: >50%
- Warning: <35%
- Critical: <20%

**Effective Hourly Rate**: Revenue / Total Hours
- Compare to your target billable rate
- If significantly lower, scope creep or inefficiency

**Cost Variance**: (Actual Cost - Budgeted Cost) / Budgeted Cost
- Positive = over budget
- Target: <5% variance

**Timeline Variance**: (Actual Duration - Estimated Duration) / Estimated Duration
- Positive = late
- Target: <10% variance

### Profitability Dashboard

**Per-project dashboard**:
```
Project: Client Website Redesign
Revenue: $20,000
Budget: $8,000 (80 hours × $100/hr)
Actual cost: $9,200 (92 hours)
Cost variance: +15% (OVER BUDGET)
Gross margin: 54% (target: 60%)
Effective rate: $217/hr (target: $250/hr)
Status: YELLOW — over budget, adjust remaining scope
```

**Agency-wide dashboard**:
```
Monthly revenue: $85,000
Monthly costs: $48,000
Gross profit: $37,000 (44% margin)
Overhead: $12,000 (14%)
Net profit: $25,000 (29%)
Utilization rate: 82%
Active projects: 12
Projects over budget: 3 (25%)
```

### Weekly Profitability Review

Every week, review:
1. Top 3 projects by revenue
2. Top 3 projects by profit margin
3. Bottom 3 projects by profit margin
4. Projects in the red (negative margin)
5. Resource utilization rates

### Scope Creep Prevention

Scope creep is the #1 profit killer. Prevent it with:

**The Change Order Process**:
1. Client requests something outside scope
2. You document the request and estimate the cost
3. You present the change order with cost impact
4. Client approves (or you do not do it)
5. Work proceeds only after approval

**Scope creep red flags**:
- "Quick question" emails that lead to hours of work
- "While you are in there..." requests
- Additional stakeholders appearing late in the project
- Feedback that fundamentally changes the direction
- Requests that grow in scope with each revision round

**Scope management rules**:
- Every client gets a fixed number of revision rounds (usually 2)
- Additional rounds are billable
- Anything not in the SOW is a change order
- No verbal scope changes—everything in writing
- Track scope changes and their cumulative impact

### Client Profitability Segmentation

Not all clients are equally profitable. Segment your clients:

**A Clients (20% of clients, 60% of profit)**:
- High revenue, high margin
- Low maintenance (few revisions, pay on time)
- Strategic priority: nurture and retain

**B Clients (50% of clients, 30% of profit)**:
- Moderate revenue, moderate margin
- Average maintenance
- Strategic priority: optimize (reduce maintenance, increase rates)

**C Clients (30% of clients, 10% of profit)**:
- Low revenue, low or negative margin
- High maintenance (many revisions, slow payers)
- Strategic priority: fire or fix (raise rates significantly)

Fire your C clients. They cost you more than they bring in.

## Project Management Process Flow

### Weekly Cycle

**Monday AM**: 
- PM reviews all project statuses
- Resource allocation confirmed for the week
- Client status reports from Friday reviewed

**Monday standup** (15 min):
- Week's priorities
- Blockers
- Resource conflicts

**Daily**:
- Async standup via Slack
- PM checks in on at-risk projects

**Friday PM**:
- PM reviews hours logged against budget
- Prepares weekly status reports
- Updates project dashboard

### Monthly Cycle

**Week 1**: 
- Review previous month's profitability
- Send client satisfaction surveys
- Update project forecasts

**Week 2**:
- Resource planning for next month
- Capacity review

**Week 3**:
- Mid-month budget check on all projects
- Adjust forecasts

**Week 4**:
- Month-end reporting
- Client billing
- Team retrospective

### Quarterly Cycle

**Quarterly business review**:
- Client profitability analysis
- Team performance review
- Rate adjustments
- Service line profitability
- Strategic planning

## Red Flags and How to Respond

**Red flag: Project is 20%+ over budget**
- Immediate: Stop all non-essential work
- Analysis: What caused the overage?
- Action: Submit change order or adjust scope
- Prevention: Tighter scope definition on next project

**Red flag: Resource utilization >90% for 2+ weeks**
- Immediate: Review all projects for priority
- Action: Push non-critical deadlines, hire support
- Prevention: Better capacity planning, build bench

**Red flag: Client satisfaction declining**
- Immediate: Schedule client call to address concerns
- Action: Adjust communication, assign dedicated contact
- Prevention: Regular satisfaction surveys, proactive communication

**Red flag: Profit margin below 20% on multiple projects**
- Immediate: Review all current estimates
- Action: Increase rates, improve scope management
- Prevention: Better initial scoping, tighter change control

## Scaling PM as You Grow

**1-3 people**: Simple task list. You manage everything directly. Weekly check-ins.

**4-10 people**: Dedicated PM tool. Weekly standups. Basic resource allocation. Spreadsheet profitability.

**11-25 people**: Full-time PM. Structured processes. Resource allocation tool. Regular profitability reviews.

**25-100 people**: PM department. Multiple PMs. Specialized roles (scheduler, analyst). Formal methodologies.

## Summary

Good project management is the difference between 20% and 40% profit margins. Track every project's budget, timeline, and profitability. Allocate resources intentionally. Prevent scope creep with change orders. Review profitability weekly. Fire unprofitable clients. Build systems that scale with your team. The best agencies are not the ones with the most talent—they are the ones that manage their talent and projects most effectively.
