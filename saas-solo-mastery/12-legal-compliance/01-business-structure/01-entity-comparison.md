# Business Entity Comparison for Solo SaaS Founders

## Why Your Entity Choice Matters

Your business entity determines:
- **Personal liability** — Can customers sue you personally?
- **Tax treatment** — How much you pay in self-employment, income, and corporate taxes
- **Fundraising ability** — Can you issue stock to investors?
- **Administrative burden** — How much paperwork and compliance
- **Credibility** — How you appear to customers, partners, and banks

The right choice depends on your revenue stage, funding plans, and risk tolerance. There is no single "best" entity — only the best for your specific situation.

## Entity Types Overview

| Entity | Liability Protection | Tax Treatment | Best For |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|----------|
| **Sole Proprietorship** | None (personally liable) | Pass-through (personal taxes) | Pre-revenue, testing ideas |
| **Single-Member LLC** | Personal liability shield | Pass-through (default) or S-Corp election | Most solo founders starting out |
| **Multi-Member LLC** | Personal liability shield | Pass-through (default) or S-Corp election | Small teams, co-founders |
| **S-Corporation** | Personal liability shield | Pass-through (salary + dividends) | Profitable solo founders ($60k+ income) |
| **C-Corporation** | Personal liability shield | Corporate tax (21%) + personal tax on dividends | Fundraising, going public |
| **B-Corporation** | Same as C-Corp | Same as C-Corp | Mission-driven, public benefit |

## Deep Dive: Each Entity

### 1. Sole Proprietorship

**What it is:** You and your business are legally the same entity. No registration required (beyond business licenses).

**Liability:** You are personally liable for everything. If you get sued, your personal assets (house, car, savings) are at risk.

**Tax treatment:** All income is reported on your personal tax return (Schedule C). You pay self-employment tax (15.3%) on all net income.

**Pros:**
- Simplest to set up ($0-50)
- Least paperwork
- No separate tax return
- Easy to change entities later

**Cons:**
- Unlimited personal liability
- Harder to look professional (clients may not take you seriously)
- Cannot raise outside investment
- Higher self-employment tax (compared to S-Corp)

**When to use:**
- Pre-revenue, testing an idea
- <$10k/year in revenue
- No significant liability risk (no contracts with large companies)
- Temporary status before forming an LLC

**When to switch:** As soon as you have paying customers (to protect personal assets)

### 2. Single-Member LLC

**What it is:** A Limited Liability Company with one owner. The most common choice for solo founders.

**Liability:** The LLC is a separate legal entity. Your personal assets are generally protected from business debts and lawsuits.

**Tax treatment (default):** You are a "disregarded entity" for tax purposes. All income passes through to your personal tax return (Schedule C). Same tax treatment as a sole proprietorship.

**Tax treatment (S-Corp election):** You can elect to be taxed as an S-Corporation (Form 2553). This allows you to pay yourself a "reasonable salary" and take the rest as distributions, reducing self-employment tax.

**Pros:**
- Personal liability protection
- Simple to set up and maintain
- Pass-through taxation (no double taxation)
- Flexible: can elect S-Corp taxation later
- Professional credibility
- Can have multiple members (add co-founder later)

**Cons:**
- Annual state fees ($50-800 depending on state)
- More paperwork than sole proprietorship
- Self-employment tax on all income (unless S-Corp election)
- Varies by state (some states tax LLCs heavily)

**Annual maintenance:**
- Annual report/franchise tax ($0-800 depending on state)
- Separate bank account and credit card
- Operating agreement (recommended)
- Business licenses as required

**When to use:**
- You have paying customers (any amount)
- You want personal liability protection
- You're a solo founder not seeking VC funding
- Revenue < $60k/year (before S-Corp election makes sense)

**When to switch to S-Corp:** When your net income exceeds ~$60k/year and you want to reduce self-employment tax.

### 3. Multi-Member LLC

**What it is:** Same as LLC but with two or more owners (co-founders).

**Key difference from Single-Member LLC:**
- You need an Operating Agreement (partnership agreement)
- You file a partnership tax return (Form 1065)
- Each member gets a Schedule K-1 reporting their share of income
- More complex accounting and compliance

**Operating Agreement should include:**
- Ownership percentages
- Profit/loss distribution
- Management structure (member-managed vs manager-managed)
- Voting rights and decision-making
- Transfer restrictions (can't sell your share without approval)
- Buy-sell provisions (what happens when someone leaves)
- Dispute resolution process

**Tax treatment:** Defaults to partnership taxation. Can elect S-Corp or C-Corp taxation.

**When to use:** You have a co-founder and want pass-through taxation.

### 4. S-Corporation (S-Corp)

**What it is:** A corporation that elects special tax status (Subchapter S). Not a separate entity type — it's an LLC or C-Corp that elects S-Corp taxation.

**What people mean when they say "S-Corp":**
- Usually an LLC that elected S-Corp taxation (Form 2553)
- Under the hood, it's still an LLC — it just pays taxes differently

**Tax treatment:** Pass-through (no corporate tax). You pay yourself:
1. **Reasonable salary** (W-2 wages) — subject to payroll taxes
2. **Distributions** (remaining profits) — NOT subject to self-employment tax

**Self-employment tax savings:**

| Scenario | LLC (Default Tax) | S-Corp Election | Savings |
|----------|------------------|----------------|---------|
| $50k income | $7,650 SE tax | $7,650 (all salary) | $0 |
| $80k income | $12,240 SE tax | $7,650 on $50k salary + $0 on $30k distribution | $4,590 |
| $120k income | $16,560 SE tax | $7,650 on $50k salary + $0 on $70k distribution | $8,910 |
| $200k income | $21,218 SE tax (capped) | $7,650 on $50k salary + $0 on $150k distribution | $13,568 |

**Note:** The IRS requires you to pay yourself a "reasonable salary" — you can't pay $0 salary and take everything as distributions. The salary must be reasonable for the work you do.

**Pros:**
- Significant self-employment tax savings at higher incomes
- Still pass-through taxation (no double taxation)
- Professional credibility

**Cons:**
- More administrative complexity (payroll, quarterly filings)
- Must run payroll (pay yourself a W-2 salary)
- Must file unemployment insurance
- Must file corporate tax return (Form 1120S) annually
- Must have board meetings and minutes
- Salary must be "reasonable" per IRS guidelines
- Cannot have different classes of stock (complicates fundraising)

**When to elect S-Corp:**
- Your net income exceeds $60-80k/year consistently
- You're willing to handle additional compliance (or pay a CPA)
- You don't plan to raise VC funding (VCs prefer C-Corps)

**When NOT to elect S-Corp:**
- Income under $60k (savings don't justify complexity)
- You plan to raise venture capital
- You're outside the US (S-Corp is US-specific)
- You can't handle additional paperwork

### 5. C-Corporation (C-Corp)

**What it is:** A traditional corporation. A separate tax-paying entity.

**Tax treatment:** Double taxation:
1. Corporation pays 21% federal tax on profits (plus state)
2. You pay personal income tax on dividends/salary

**However, early-stage C-Corps typically don't pay corporate tax because:**
- They reinvest all profits into growth
- They often operate at a loss (intentionally)
- Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS) can provide tax benefits

**Pros:**
- Preferred by venture capitalists (familiar structure)
- Can issue multiple classes of stock (preferred vs common)
- Stock options are cleaner and more attractive to employees
- Perpetual existence (continues even if you leave)
- Maximum liability protection

**Cons:**
- Double taxation (if you have profits)
- Most administrative burden (board meetings, minutes, annual reports)
- More expensive to set up ($500-1500) and maintain ($1000+/year)
- Complex tax returns
- Cannot use S-Corp tax savings
- Less flexibility than LLC

**When to use:**
- You plan to raise venture capital
- You intend to go public
- You have multiple investors
- You want to issue stock options to attract talent

**When NOT to use:**
- You're bootstrapping (stick with LLC/S-Corp)
- You have no plans to raise VC
- You want simplicity and low cost

**Delaware C-Corp vs State C-Corp:**

| Factor | Delaware C-Corp | Your State C-Corp |
|--------|----------------|-------------------|
| Legal precedent | Extensive (Court of Chancery) | Varies by state |
| Investor familiarity | Very high | Lower (may need conversion) |
| Franchise tax | $175-200k/year (for large corps) or minimum $400/year | Varies |
| Annual report | $50-175/year | Varies |
| Registered agent | $50-300/year | May not be needed |
| Double taxation | Same | Same |

**When to incorporate in Delaware:**
- You're raising VC (investors prefer Delaware)
- You're in a state with high corporate taxes (California, New York)
- You plan to go public

**When to incorporate in your state:**
- You're bootstrapping and want to minimize costs
- Your state has lower fees than Delaware + foreign qualification
- You have no plans to raise VC

### 6. B-Corporation

**What it is:** A C-Corp that has committed to certain social and environmental standards (certified by B Lab).

**Key points:**
- Same legal structure as C-Corp
- Must consider stakeholder impact (workers, community, environment)
- Must meet B Lab certification standards every 3 years
- Can be beneficial for mission-driven businesses

**Pros:**
- Differentiates your brand (purpose-driven)
- Attracts mission-aligned talent and customers
- Legal protection to consider non-financial stakeholders

**Cons:**
- Same complexity as C-Corp
- Additional certification cost ($500-2000+)
- Annual reporting requirements
- Very early for most solo SaaS founders

**When to consider:**
- Social/environmental mission is core to your brand
- You're already forming a C-Corp for fundraising
- You want to attract impact investors

**When to skip:** Most solo SaaS founders. Wait until you have revenue or funding.

## Decision Framework for Solo Founders

### If You're Pre-Revenue / Testing

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Sole Proprietorship or Informal            │
│  → $0 to set up                             │
│  → No liability protection (but limited risk)│
│  → Easy to convert later                    │
│  → Only while testing an idea (<$1k revenue)│
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### If You Have Paying Customers (No VC Plans)

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Single-Member LLC                          │
│  → $100-500 to set up                       │
│  → Personal liability protection            │
│  → Minimal compliance                       │
│  → Can elect S-Corp later when profitable   │
│  → Best for most solo founders              │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### If You're Profitable ($60k+ Net Income, No VC)

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  LLC with S-Corp Election                   │
│  → Self-employment tax savings              │
│  → More admin (payroll, quarterly returns)  │
│  → Must pay yourself "reasonable salary"    │
│  → Saves $4-10k/year in taxes               │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### If You're Raising Venture Capital

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Delaware C-Corporation                     │
│  → $500-1500 to set up                      │
│  → Investor expectations                    │
│  → Can issue preferred stock                │
│  → More admin and compliance                │
│  → Consider: Stripe Atlas or Clerky         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### If You Have a Co-Founder

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  Multi-Member LLC (or C-Corp if raising)    │
│  → Operating Agreement is critical          │
│  → Clearly define ownership and vesting     │
│  → Consider S-Corp for tax savings          │
│  → Or Delaware C-Corp if fundraising        │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

| Wyoming | $100 | $62 (or 0.02% of assets, whichever is less) | Tax-friendly, privacy |
| Nevada | $425 | $350-500 | Tax-friendly, no corporate income tax |
| New Mexico | $50 | $0 | Cheapest for registration |

| Any "Your State" | $50-200 | $0-800 | Varies widely |

### Foreign Qualification

If you incorporate in one state but operate in another (e.g., Delaware LLC living in California), you need to "foreign qualify" in your home state.

**This means:**
- You pay formation fees in Delaware ($90 + $300/year)
- AND you pay foreign qualification fees in your home state (varies)
- AND you file annual reports in both states
- AND you register for tax in both states

**When it's worth it:**
- You're in a state with high LLC costs (DE vs CA — you save on CA franchise tax)
- You plan to raise VC (investors want Delaware)
- You want Delaware's legal system

**When it's NOT worth it:**
- You're in a state with low LLC costs
- You're a solo founder with no investors
- You just want liability protection (your home state LLC is fine)

### LLC in Your Home State vs Delaware

| Scenario | Recommendation |
|----------|---------------|
| Live in Wyoming, Nevada, Colorado | Form in your state (cheap/free) |
| Live in California ($800/year LLC fee) | Form in Delaware, foreign qualify in CA OR form LLC in CA if you'll convert to C-Corp anyway |
| Live in New York (expensive + publication) | Form in Delaware, foreign qualify in NY |
| Live in Texas, Florida, or low-cost state | Form in your state (simple, cheap) |
| Live outside US | Form in Wyoming or Delaware (no state income tax) |
| Raising VC | Delaware C-Corp |

## How to Form Your Entity

### DIY Formation ($50-200)

Use a service like:

| Service | Cost | Best For |
|---------|------|----------|
| **LegalZoom** | $79 + state fees | Simple LLC formation |
| **ZenBusiness** | $0 + state fees | Budget-friendly LLC |
| **IncFile** | $0 + state fees | LLC with registered agent free for 1 year |
| **Northwest Registered Agent** | $225 + state fees | Privacy + good customer service |
| **Stripe Atlas** | $500 | Delaware C-Corp for fundraising |
| **Clerky** | $299 | Delaware C-Corp with proper docs |

**DIY steps for an LLC:**
1. Choose your state of formation
2. Choose a business name (check availability)
3. File Articles of Organization with the state
4. Pay formation fee
5. Create an Operating Agreement (even for single-member)
6. Get an EIN from the IRS (free)
7. Register for state taxes (if needed)
8. Open a business bank account

### Using a Lawyer ($500-2000)

**When to use a lawyer:**
- You have a co-founder (Operating Agreement is critical)
- You're raising venture capital
- You want a C-Corp with proper stock issuance
- You're concerned about specific liability issues
- Your business is in a regulated industry

**How to find a startup lawyer:**
- Ask other founders for referrals
- Check your network for startup experience
- Use [Lawyer for Startups](https://lawyerforstartups.com/)
- Look for firms that offer "startup packages" (flat fee)

## Entity Conversion Paths

You can change your entity type as your business grows:

```
Sole Proprietorship → LLC: Easy (just form the LLC)
LLC → S-Corp: File Form 2553 with IRS (tax election only)
LLC → C-Corp: File Form 8832 with IRS (or convert legally)
S-Corp → C-Corp: Revoke S-Corp election (with IRS consent)
C-Corp → LLC: Difficult (taxable event, not recommended)
```

**Note:** Moving from C-Corp to any pass-through entity is a taxable event. This is why you should only form a C-Corp if you're sure you need one.

## Tax Implications Summary

| Entity | Self-Employment Tax | Corporate Tax | Personal Income Tax | Admin Burden |
|--------|-------------------|---------------|-------------------|-------------|
| Sole Proprietorship | 15.3% on all net income | None | Schedule C + personal return | Low |
| LLC (default) | 15.3% on all net income | None | Schedule C + personal return | Low |
| LLC (S-Corp election) | 15.3% on salary only | None | W-2 + K-1 + personal return | Medium |
| S-Corp | 15.3% on salary only | None | W-2 + 1120S + personal return | Medium-High |
| C-Corp | None (as employee) | 21% federal + state | W-2 + dividends + personal return | High |

**Self-employment tax cap (2024):**
- Social Security: 12.4% on first $168,600 of net income
- Medicare: 2.9% on all net income
- So the max self-employment tax is approximately $21,218/year

## Solo Founder Entity Recommendations Summary

```
Stage: Pre-revenue (<$1k)
  → Entity: Sole Proprietorship
  → Reason: No liability risk, no cost, easy to change
  → Action: Focus on building product, not paperwork

Stage: Early Revenue ($1-60k/year)
  → Entity: Single-Member LLC (in your home state)
  → Reason: Liability protection, simple, low cost
  → Action: Form LLC, get EIN, open business bank account

Stage: Profitable ($60k-120k/year)
  → Entity: LLC with S-Corp election
  → Reason: Self-employment tax savings ($4-10k/year)
  → Action: File Form 2553, set up payroll

Stage: High Revenue ($120k+/year, no VC)
  → Entity: LLC with S-Corp election (or C-Corp)
  → Reason: Tax savings continue; C-Corp if you might raise later
  → Action: Keep S-Corp or consult CPA about C-Corp

Stage: Fundraising (any revenue)
  → Entity: Delaware C-Corp
  → Reason: Required for VC investment
  → Action: Use Stripe Atlas or Clerky, hire a startup lawyer
```
