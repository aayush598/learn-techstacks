# When to Raise Money

A decision framework for solo founders evaluating whether to fundraise or bootstrap — signals you're ready, types of investors, and how to time your raise for maximum leverage.

---

## Part 1: The Bootstrap vs. Fundraise Decision

### The Question Every Solo Founder Faces

At some point, you'll ask: "Should I raise money?"

The answer depends on three things:
1. **Do you need it?** (Do you have a capital-intensive growth opportunity?)
2. **Can you get it?** (Do you have the metrics and traction?)
3. **Should you take it?** (Is the trade-off worth it?)

### The Decision Matrix

```yaml
You should FUNDRAISE if:
  - You have proven product-market fit (15%+ MoM growth, < 5% churn)
  - You have a capital-efficient growth channel that needs scaling (paid ads)
  - You need to hire a team to accelerate (not to build the MVP)
  - Your market has a time-sensitive opportunity (first-mover advantage)
  - You want to take risk off the table personally

You should BOOTSTRAP if:
  - You haven't found product-market fit yet
  - You're pre-revenue or below $5K MRR
  - You can grow through organic channels (SEO, content, referrals)
  - You value control and don't want investor pressure
  - Your SaaS can be built and operated by a solo founder
  - You have enough savings to reach ramen profitable
```

### The Solo Founder's Honest Assessment

```yaml
Question                          | Bootstrap     | Fundraise
──────────────────────────────────|───────────────|────────────────
Do I have clear PMF?             | Maybe         | Must have
Can I grow without paid ads?     | Yes → Bootstrap | No → Consider raise
Is my CAC payback < 6 months?    | Bootstrap well| Fundraise also works
Do I have 12+ months of runway?  | Bootstrap well| Consider if growth is fast
Am I willing to give up 20-40%?  | Bootstrap     | Fundraise
Do I need $100K+ to execute?     | Bootstrap slow | Fundraise
Is there a time-sensitive market?| Can miss window | Fundraise
Do I have co-founder?            | Works either   | Helps for fundraising
```

## Part 2: Signals You're Ready to Fundraise

### The Traction Threshold

There's no hard rule, but here are common thresholds used by investors:

```yaml
Pre-Seed:
  MRR: $0 - $5K
  Growth: Any traction
  Team: Solo or 2 co-founders
  Product: MVP with early users
  Typical raise: $100K - $500K
  Note: Very hard as a solo founder without network

Seed:
  MRR: $5K - $20K
  Growth: 15-20% MoM
  Retention: < 5% monthly churn
  Team: 2-3 people (or solo + contractors)
  Revenue: Some revenue, growing
  Typical raise: $500K - $2M
  Note: This is the sweet spot for many SaaS founders

Series A:
  MRR: $50K - $150K
  Growth: 10-15% MoM (or 3x YoY)
  NRR: > 100%
  Team: 5-15 people
  Revenue: Clear path to $10M ARR
  Typical raise: $3M - $10M
  Note: Very hard to reach as solo founder without prior raises
```

### The 6 Signals Investors Look For

```yaml
Signal 1: Strong Retention
  - Monthly churn < 5% (ideally < 3%)
  - Cohort retention is stable or improving
  - NRR > 100% (existing customers are growing)
  - Proof: Cohort retention table, NRR calculation

Signal 2: Repeatable Acquisition
  - At least one channel producing customers at consistent CAC
  - CAC payback < 12 months (ideally < 6)
  - Channel isn't saturated (room to spend more)
  - Proof: Channel breakdown, CAC by channel

Signal 3: Growing Revenue
  - 15%+ MoM MRR growth for 3+ consecutive months
  - Growth is accelerating, not decelerating
  - Revenue is diversified (no single customer > 10% of MRR)
  - Proof: MRR chart over 12+ months

Signal 4: Strong Unit Economics
  - LTV/CAC > 3x (ideally > 5x)
  - Gross margin > 70% (ideally > 80%)
  - Path to profitability is clear
  - Proof: Unit economics spreadsheet

Signal 5: Large Market
  - TAM > $1B (for VC) or $100M+ (for angels)
  - You can articulate why now is the right time
  - Competition exists but you have a differentiated angle
  - Proof: Market sizing, competitive landscape

Signal 6: Founder-Market Fit
  - You have domain expertise
  - You understand the customer deeply
  - You can articulate the vision compellingly
  - Proof: Your background, customer conversations
```

### The "Sandwich" Signal

```yaml
SuperAngel investor Jason Calacanis' "Sandwich" rule:

Top Slice: Traction
  - Strong growth numbers
  - Good retention
  - Revenue

Middle: Story
  - Clear problem → solution narrative
  - Why now? Why you?
  - Vision for 5x growth

Bottom Slice: Team
  - Founder-market fit
  - Ability to execute
  - Coachability

If you have all three, you're ready to raise.
If you're missing any, focus on that first.
```

## Part 3: Types of Investors

### The Investor Spectrum

```yaml
Type               | Check Size | Stage      | Value Add        | Expectations
───────────────────|────────────|────────────|──────────────────|─────────────
Family & Friends   | $5K-$50K   | Pre-seed   | Low              | Low (love you)
Angel Investors    | $25K-$100K | Pre-seed   | Network, advice  | 3-5x return
Angel Syndicates   | $100K-$500K| Seed       | Collective wisdom| 5-10x return
Micro VCs          | $250K-$1M  | Seed       | Specialized      | 10x+ return
Traditional VCs    | $1M-$5M    | Series A+  | Institutional    | 10-30x return
Strategic / Corp    | $500K-$5M | Any        | Partnership      | Strategic alignment
Revenue-Based VCs   | $50K-$2M   | Revenue    | Revenue-focused  | % of revenue
```

### Family & Friends Round

```yaml
Best for: Pre-revenue, < $50K needed
Check size: $5K - $50K
Documents: Promissory note or simple agreement
Terms: Usually convertible note with low/no interest

Pros:
  - Fastest money (trust already exists)
  - Flexible terms
  - No board, no control
  - They're rooting for you personally

Cons:
  - Strains relationships if business fails
  - Limited check sizes
  - Usually no strategic value
  - Awkward if they want updates constantly

How to approach:
  - Have a clear ask: "I need $25K to launch"
  - Show your plan: "Here's how I'll use it..."
  - Set expectations: "This is high risk, may go to zero"
  - Use standard SAFE or convertible note (don't make it personal loan)
```

### Angel Investors

```yaml
Best for: Some traction, $25K - $100K needed
Check size: $25K - $100K per angel
Documents: SAFE or priced round
Terms: 15-25% discount on future round, $5-20M cap

Pros:
  - Industry experience and network
  - Can help with future fundraising
  - Usually founder-friendly terms
  - May have specific domain expertise

Cons:
  - Managing multiple angels (10+ can be noisy)
  - Some want too much involvement
  - Not all angels are helpful
  - Finding the right ones takes time

How to find:
  - AngelList syndicates
  - Local angel groups (Keiretsu, Tech Coast)
  - Warm intros from other founders
  - Industry events and meetups
  - Twitter/LinkedIn (many angels are active)
```

### Micro VCs

```yaml
Best for: Strong traction, $500K - $1M needed
Check size: $250K - $1M
Documents: SAFE or Series A documents
Terms: 15-25% dilution, board seat (observer), standard terms

Pros:
  - Bigger checks, fewer investors
  - Specialized SaaS knowledge
  - Portfolio network (other founders)
  - Help with Series A

Cons:
  - More process (diligence, legal)
  - Standard VC terms
  - Portfolio pressure (they have 20+ companies)
  - Board meetings (even if observer only)

Top Micro VCs for SaaS:
  - SaaS Capital
  - Indie.vc
  - TinySeed
  - Foreword.vc
  - Weekend Fund
  - Hyphen Capital
```

### Traditional VCs

```yaml
Best for: $1M+ ARR, proven growth
Check size: $2M - $10M+
Documents: Series A docs (100+ pages)
Terms: 15-25% dilution, board seat, control provisions

Pros:
  - Large checks (scale faster)
  - Brand credibility
  - Network for future rounds and exits
  - Operational support (recruiting, BD)

Cons:
  - Significant control transfer
  - Pressure to grow fast (or die trying)
  - 10x+ return expectation
  - May push for early exit or replacement

VC red flags for solo founders:
  - "We think you need a CEO who has done this before"
  - "We want a board seat with veto power"
  - "We'd like to bring in our CRO"
  
  If you hear these, they may try to replace you.
```

### Revenue-Based Financing

```yaml
Best for: $5K+ MRR, strong unit economics
Firms: Pipe, Capchase, Lighter Capital, SaaS Capital
Amount: 1-5x monthly revenue
Repayment: % of monthly revenue (3-12% typical)
Term: Until paid back (usually 12-36 months)

Pros:
  - No dilution
  - No board seat
  - Fast (days, not months)
  - Aligned with revenue (pay more when you earn more)

Cons:
  - Expensive (effective 20-40% APR)
  - Requires consistent MRR growth
  - Can be cash flow negative during repayment
  - Don't usually get strategic value

Best use cases:
  - Investing in paid acquisition (proven ROI)
  - Hiring for growth role
  - Annual prepayment incentive (discount for annual)
  - Bridge to next equity round
```

## Part 4: The Solo Founder Fundraising Strategy

### When to Actually Fundraise

```yaml
The optimal window for solo founders:

NOT YET (wait):
  - Pre-revenue or < $3K MRR
  - Churn > 8% monthly
  - No repeatable acquisition channel
  - Growth < 10% MoM
  - You haven't proven anything yet
  → Investors will own you with harsh terms

SWEET SPOT (raise now):
  - $5K - $20K MRR
  - 15%+ MoM growth for 3+ months
  - < 5% monthly churn
  - 1+ repeatable acquisition channel
  - Positive unit economics
  → You have leverage for good terms

MAYBE TOO LATE (bootstrapped too long?):
  - $50K+ MRR, growing fast
  - You don't NEED the money
  - You're already profitable
  → You have maximum leverage — but do you even want investors now?
```

### The Solo Founder Pitch

```yaml
The solo founder has a different pitch than a founding team.

Your strengths:
  - Faster decision-making
  - Lower burn rate
  - Deeper customer relationships
  - Full ownership of vision
  - "Built by one person" is a compelling story

Your weaknesses (address directly):
  - Key person risk (what if you get hit by a bus?)
  - Can you scale without a team?
  - Have you hit a ceiling?
  - Do you have a succession plan?

The solo founder pitch structure:

1. Problem & Solution (2 min)
   "X problem affects Y people. I built Z to solve it."

2. Traction (3 min)
   "I went from $0 to $10K MRR in 8 months. Here's the growth curve."
   Show: MRR chart, retention table, channel performance

3. Magic (2 min)
   "What makes this work that nobody else has: [your insight]."

4. Why Now? (1 min)
   "Market timing: [trend, regulation, technology shift]."

5. Ask (1 min)
   "I'm raising $500K. $200K to scale ads, $150K to hire, $150K for runway."
   "This gets us to 24 months of runway."
   "Target: $50K MRR in 18 months."
```

### Investor Update Template (Pre-Raise)

```yaml
Start sending updates 3-6 months before you plan to raise.

Subject: SaaS Updates — [Month] [Year] — [Company]

Hi [Investor Name],

Here's what happened this month:

METRICS:
  MRR: $8,420 (+15% MoM)
  Customers: 142 (+18)
  Churn: 3.8% (improving from 4.2% last month)
  ARPU: $59
  LTV/CAC: 5.2x

HIGHLIGHTS:
  - Launched new integration with Slack (15% of new users connected it)
  - Published case study with [customer] — 2x conversion on that page
  - Hired first part-time support person (freed up 15 hrs/week)

CHALLENGES:
  - Google Ads CPC increased 20% (adjusting targeting)
  - Enterprise pilot delayed 2 weeks

ASK:
  - Warm intro to [target company]?
  - Feedback on pricing page redesign?
  - Know any great content writers?

Thanks,
[Your Name]
```

### The Raise Timeline

```yaml
3-6 months before: Build relationships
  - Start sending investor updates
  - Follow investors on Twitter
  - Meet at events/conferences
  - Get intros from existing network

1-2 months before: Prepare materials
  - Pitch deck (10-12 slides)
  - Financial model (3-5 year projections)
  - Data room (metrics, legal, team)
  - Customer references list

Weeks 1-3: Outreach
  - 50-100 investor meetings
  - Warm intros preferred (conversion: 3x cold)
  - Pitch → follow up → next meeting

Weeks 3-6: Diligence
  - Top 3-5 investors get deeper access
  - Customer calls, data review, product demos
  - Term sheet negotiations

Weeks 6-8: Close
  - Legal docs (SAFE or priced round)
  - Wire transfer
  - Press release / announcement
```

## Part 5: Fundraising Readiness Checklist

### The Pre-Raise Audit

```yaml
METRICS (must have):
  ☐ MRR history (12+ months ideally)
  ☐ MRR growth rate (3+ months of 15%+)  
  ☐ Customer count and growth
  ☐ Churn rate (monthly and annual)
  ☐ LTV/CAC with payback period
  ☐ Gross margin
  ☐ Cohort retention table
  ☐ ARPU by plan tier

DOCUMENTS:
  ☐ 10-slide pitch deck
  ☐ 3-year financial projection
  ☐ Data room (Notion, Google Drive, or dedicated tool)
  ☐ Product demo (recorded, 5 minutes)
  ☐ Customer references (3-5 happy customers)
  ☐ Competitive landscape analysis
  ☐ Market sizing (TAM, SAM, SOM)

LEGAL:
  ☐ Proper entity (C-Corp preferred for fundraising)
  ☐ Cap table (if any previous investment)
  ☐ IP assignment (you own all IP)
  ☐ Founder agreement (if any co-founders)
  ☐ Standard contracts (terms of service, privacy policy)

PERSONAL:
  ☐ Clear narrative (problem → solution → traction → vision)
  ☐ Story practice (10+ times)
  ☐ Investor targets list (50+ names)
  ☐ Network warming (start 3 months before)
  ☐ Can handle rejection (you'll hear "no" 50-100 times)
```

### The "Don't Raise Until" List

```yaml
Don't raise until you can honestly say:

☐ "I have product-market fit" (users would be disappointed without your product)
☐ "I know my unit economics" (LTV, CAC, payback — by channel)
☐ "I have a repeatable acquisition channel" (you can spend $1 to reliably get $3+)
☐ "I can articulate why I need capital" (specific use, not "growth")
☐ "I'm ready to give up some control" (board, reporting, investor input)
☐ "I understand the terms" (valuation, dilution, liquidation preference)
☐ "I can handle the pressure" (investors expect hockey-stick growth)
☐ "I have a backup plan" (what if the raise fails?)

If you can't say all 8, you're not ready.
```

## Part 6: Alternatives to Traditional Fundraising

### When Fundraising Doesn't Make Sense

Sometimes the right answer is: don't raise at all (yet — or ever).

Alternative paths:

```yaml
Path 1: Bootstrap to Profitability
  Pros: Full control, no dilution, no pressure
  Cons: Slower growth, may miss market window
  Best for: Founders who value independence over scale

Path 2: Revenue-Based Financing
  Pros: No dilution, fast, aligned with revenue
  Cons: Expensive, requires consistent MRR growth
  Best for: Proven growth with strong unit economics

Path 3: Grants and Competitions
  Sources: SBIR (US govt), local economic dev, startup competitions
  Amounts: $25K - $250K
  Pros: Non-dilutive, validation
  Cons: Time-consuming to apply, restrictive uses

Path 4: Customer Prepayments
  - Offer 20-30% discount for 2-year prepaid
  - Build enterprise contracts with upfront payment
  Pros: Revenue, customer lock-in, no dilution
  Cons: Limits some growth (discount)

Path 5: Consulting / Services
  - 1-2 days/week consulting (pays the bills)
  - Build SaaS on the side
  Pros: Income, extended runway, market insights
  Cons: Slows SaaS progress, double workload
```

## Part 7: The Decision — Final Framework

### The One-Page Decision Matrix

```yaml
                    | BOOTSTRAP                      | FUNDRAISE
────────────────────┼────────────────────────────────┼──────────────────────
Early stage (<$5K)  | Only option (no one invests)   | Not ready — bootstrap
Growing ($5K-20K)   | Viable — many succeed here    | Best time to raise
Scaling ($20K+ MRR) | Possible — slower growth       | Most efficient option
Profitable ($50K+)  | You've already won — why raise?| Only if you want 10x+

Factors pushing to BOOTSTRAP:
  - You can grow through organic channels
  - You value control above fast growth
  - Your SaaS works well as a solo business
  - You have 12+ months of personal runway

Factors pushing to FUNDRAISE:
  - You have a paid acquisition channel that works
  - You need to hire to accelerate
  - Market timing is critical (first-mover advantage)
  - You want to reduce personal financial risk
```

### The Honest Question

```yaml
Don't ask yourself "Should I raise money?"
Ask: "Am I building a company that needs outside capital?"

If the answer is YES (you need $500K+ to execute):
  → Fundraising is necessary, not optional
  → Start preparing 6 months before you need the money
  → Build relationships before you need them

If the answer is NO (you can grow organically):
  → Fundraising is optional, not necessary
  → Only raise if the terms are favorable
  → Don't raise just because "that's what startups do"

If the answer is I DON'T KNOW:
  → You're not ready — build more traction first
  → Get to $5K MRR with clear unit economics
  → Then decide: is this a lifestyle business or a VC-backable company?
```

The best time to raise money is when you don't need it. The best founders raise when they have leverage — strong metrics, multiple offers, and a clear plan. If you're raising because you're desperate, the terms will reflect that.
