# Value-Based Pricing: Pricing Based on What Your Product Is Worth

## The Pricing Philosophy Shift

Most founders price their product based on costs (cost-plus pricing) or competitors (competition-based pricing). Both are wrong. The right way to price is based on the VALUE your product delivers to customers.

**Cost-plus:** "It costs us $10/month to run, so we charge $30/month."
**Competition-based:** "Our competitor charges $50/month, so we charge $45/month."
**Value-based:** "Our product saves customers $1,000/month, so we charge $200/month."

Value-based pricing captures a portion of the value you create. It's the most profitable, most defensible, and most customer-aligned pricing strategy.

## The Economics of Value-Based Pricing

### The Value Calculation

```
Value delivered to customer: $X/month
Your price: $Y/month
Customer's ROI: ($X - $Y) / $Y = ROI

Example 1: Save Time
- Product saves customer 20 hours/month
- Customer's time value: $50/hour
- Value delivered: $1,000/month
- Your price: $200/month
- Customer ROI: 400%

Example 2: Save Money
- Product reduces customer's software costs by $500/month
- Value delivered: $500/month
- Your price: $150/month
- Customer ROI: 233%

Example 3: Make Money
- Product increases customer's revenue by $2,000/month
- Value delivered: $2,000/month
- Your price: $499/month
- Customer ROI: 300%
```

### The Value Capture Ratio

The percentage of value you capture in your price.

```
Value capture ratio = Your price / Value delivered

Standard SaaS ratios:
- 10-20% capture = Fair price (customer keeps 80-90% of value)
- 20-30% capture = Premium price (customer keeps 70-80%)
- 30%+ capture = High price (needs exceptional value perception)
- <10% capture = You're leaving money on the table

Example:
- Value delivered: $1,000/month
- Price at 15% capture: $150/month (standard)
- Price at 25% capture: $250/month (premium)
- Price at 5% capture: $50/month (leaving money)
```

### The Willingness to Pay (WTP) Curve

Customers have a range of willingness to pay:

```
Maximum WTP: "This is worth $500/month to me"
Fair WTP: "This is fairly priced at $200/month"
Minimum WTP: "I'd try it at $50/month"
Below minimum: "It's not worth anything to me"

Your job: Price within the range that captures value
while keeping the customer happy they bought.

The sweet spot: Price at 50-70% of maximum WTP
- Above 70%: Customers may hesitate or resent the price
- Below 50%: You're leaving value on the table
```

## Part 1: Quantifying Your Product's Value

### Method 1: Time Savings

Calculate how much time your product saves per user/team per month.

```
Step 1: Time saved per use
"How long does [task] take without our product?"
"With our product, how long?"
"Difference = time saved per use"

Step 2: Usage frequency
"How many times per week/month do users do this task?"

Step 3: Value calculation
Time saved per use × Frequency × Time value = Monthly value

Example: Automated invoice processing
- Manual invoice processing: 30 minutes per invoice
- Automated processing: 5 minutes per invoice
- Time saved: 25 minutes per invoice
- Invoices per month: 40
- Time saved per month: 1,000 minutes (16.7 hours)
- Time value (accounting clerk): $25/hour
- Value delivered: $417/month
```

**Time value by role:**

| Role | Hourly Value (Fully Loaded) |
|------|---------------------------|
| Admin/Support | $25-40/hour |
| Accountant/Functions | $35-60/hour |
| Sales/Account Manager | $50-80/hour |
| Engineer/Developer | $75-120/hour |
| Manager/Director | $80-150/hour |
| Executive/Founder | $150-300/hour |

### Method 2: Cost Reduction

Calculate how much money your product saves the customer directly.

```
Step 1: Current costs
"How much does the customer currently spend to solve this problem?"
- Software costs
- Labor costs (FTE or contractor)
- Outsourcing costs
- Error/penalty costs

Step 2: Future costs with your product
"With our product, what will they spend?"

Step 3: Value calculation
Current costs - Future costs = Direct savings

Example: Compliance automation
- Current: External compliance consultant at $500/month
- Compliance manager: 10 hours/month at $75/hour = $750/month
- Regulatory fine risk: $5,000/year average
- Total current cost: $500 + $750 + $417 = $1,667/month
- With product: $200/month subscription + 2 hours/month internal
- Value: $1,667 - $350 = $1,317/month savings
```

### Method 3: Revenue Generation

Calculate how much additional revenue your product helps customers earn.

```
Step 1: Baseline revenue
"How much does the customer currently earn without our product?"

Step 2: Revenue with product
"What additional revenue can they generate with our product?"

Step 3: Value calculation
(Differential revenue × Probability) = Value

Example: Lead enrichment tool
- Baseline: Sales team closes 20 deals/month at $2,000 average = $40,000/month
- With enrichment: Close rate improves by 15% → 23 deals/month = $46,000/month
- Additional revenue: $6,000/month
- Probability of achieving this: 70%
- Value: $4,200/month
```

### Method 4: Risk Reduction

Calculate how much risk (financial, compliance, operational) your product mitigates.

```
Step 1: Identify risks
"What could go wrong if the customer doesn't solve this problem?"
- Compliance violations (fines, legal costs)
- Data breaches (remediation, lawsuits, reputation)
- Operational failures (downtime, errors)
- Competitive disadvantage (lost market share)

Step 2: Quantify risk
"If this risk materializes, what's the cost?"
- Probability: How likely is it to happen?
- Expected value: Cost × Probability

Step 3: Value calculation
Risk reduction = Value of mitigated risk

Example: Security compliance tool
- Risk: Data breach costing $100K (including fines, remediation, lost business)
- Probability without tool: 15% annually
- Expected annual cost: $15,000 ($1,250/month)
- Probability with tool: 3%
- Expected annual cost: $3,000 ($250/month)
- Value of risk reduction: $1,000/month
```

### The Value Stack

Most products deliver value through multiple channels:

```
Your product's total value = 
Time savings    + Cost reduction    + Revenue generation      + Risk reduction
║                    ║                     ║                       ║
║ Value: $417/mo     ║ Value: $500/mo      ║ Value: $2,000/mo     ║ Value: $1,000/mo
║                                        Total Value: $3,917/mo
║
║ Price (20% capture): $783/month
║
║ Price (conservative, focus on time + cost): $917/month
║
║ Price (minimal): $417/month (time savings only)
```

## Part 2: Willingness to Pay Research

### Method 1: The Van Westendorp Price Sensitivity Meter

Ask customers four questions:

```
1. At what price would you consider [product] to be so expensive
   that you would never consider buying it? (Too expensive)

2. At what price would you consider [product] to be expensive
   but still worth considering? (Expensive, but acceptable)

3. At what price would you consider [product] to be priced
   so low that you would question its quality? (Too cheap)

4. At what price would you consider [product] to be a bargain? (Cheap)
```

**Analyzing Van Westendorp results:**

```
Plot four cumulative distribution curves.

Key points:
- IDP (Indifference Price Point): Where "expensive but worth it" 
  and "cheap" curves cross = Most comfortable price
- OPP (Optimal Price Point): Where "too expensive" and "too cheap"
  cross = Optimal price for maximum willingness to buy
- PMC (Point of Marginal Cheapness): Where "too cheap" and "cheap" cross
- PME (Point of Marginal Expensiveness): Where "expensive" and
  "too expensive" cross

Your target price: Between OPP and IDP
```

**Example Van Westendorp results:**

```
Average responses:
- Too cheap: $10
- Cheap/bargain: $25
- Expensive but acceptable: $75
- Too expensive: $150

OPP (optimal price): ~$40-50
IDP (indifference price): ~$55-65

Recommended price: $49/month
```

### Method 2: Direct Questioning

```
"At $X/month, would you purchase this product?"
- Test different price points with different groups
- Track "yes" rate at each price

Better than "how much would you pay?" because it's specific.

Price   Yes Rate
$19     85%
$29     72%
$49     58%
$79     35%
$99     22%
$149    12%

The price where 50-60% say yes is usually a good starting point.
Above 60%: Too cheap
Below 40%: Too expensive
```

### Method 3: Competitor Price Anchoring

```
"If our product is similar to [competitor A at $50/month] and
better than [competitor B at $99/month], where should we price?"

Customers naturally anchor on competitor pricing.
Use this to position:
- Above competitor price = Premium positioning
- Same price = Parity positioning
- Below competitor price = Value positioning
```

### Method 4: The "Magic Wand" Question

```
"If you could wave a magic wand and solve [problem], what would
that be worth to your business per month?"

This measures the PAIN more than the PRICE.
But it gives you an upper bound for pricing.

Follow-up:
"So if we could solve that for $X/month, would it be worth it?"
- If they say yes at the upper bound, you have room
- If they hesitate, you're near their limit
```

## Part 3: Pricing Research Methods

### Pre-Launch (No Product Yet)

```
Method 1: Landing page pricing test
- Create 3 versions of your landing page with different prices
- Split traffic (Google Optimize, VWO)
- Measure: Click-through on pricing, signup rate
- Note: People say they'd pay, but actions differ

Method 2: Interview-based pricing
- In customer interviews, ask about value
- "What would this problem cost you if unsolved?"
- "What have you spent trying to solve it?"
- "What would a perfect solution be worth?"

Method 3: Competitor price analysis
- What do competitors charge?
- How is your product different?
- Are you worth more or less?
- Price within the range, but lean higher
```

### Post-Launch (With Customers)

```
Method 1: A/B test pricing
- Show different prices to different segments
- Measure conversion rate at each price
- Ensure statistical significance (100+ conversions per variation)

Method 2: Survey existing customers
- "If [product] was priced at $X, would you continue using it?"
- "What is [product] worth to you per month?"
- "How much does [product] save you per month?"

Method 3: Analyze churn by price
- Do customers who pay more churn less? (They value it more)
- Do customers who pay less churn more? (They value it less)
- If high-churn segment exists, maybe price is wrong
```

### The Pricing Survey

Send to your email list or existing customers:

```
[PRODUCT NAME] PRICING SURVEY

1. How much does [product] save you in time per month?
   ___ hours

2. How much does [product] save/increase your revenue per month?
   $___

3. If [product] cost $X/month, would you still use it?
   Yes / No / Not sure

4. If [product] was free, what feature would you be willing
   to pay for?
   [Open text]

5. What is the maximum you would pay for [product] per month?
   $___

6. What is a fair price for [product] per month?
   $___

7. How would you feel paying $X/month?
   (Too cheap / Good deal / Fair / Expensive / Too expensive)
   - Test 3-4 price points across respondents
```

## Part 4: Setting Your Price

### The Value-Based Price Formula

```
Research phase gives you:

1. Quantified value delivered: $X/month
   (time savings + cost reduction + revenue generation + risk reduction)

2. Willingness to pay range: $Y1 - $Y2
   (from Van Westendorp, surveys, A/B tests)

3. Competitor pricing: $Z1 - $Z2
   (market range)

Your price = function of (Value, WTP, Competition)

Simple formula:
Base price = (Quantified value × Capture ratio)
Adjust = Function of (WTP range, Competitive position)

Recommended capture ratio:
- Clear, measurable value: 20-30%
- Moderate value: 15-20%
- Hard-to-measure value: 10-15%
```

### The Pricing Decision Matrix

| Competitor Price | Our Value | Our Price Strategy | Example |
|-----------------|-----------|-------------------|---------|
| Lower | Higher | Premium above competitor | $199 vs. $149 |
| Lower | Same | Parity + differentiation | $149 vs. $149 |
| Lower | Lower | Below competitor (value) | $99 vs. $149 |
| Higher | Higher | Premium market leader | $499 vs. $399 |
| Higher | Same | Parity (capture their overpriced customers) | $399 vs. $399 |
| Higher | Lower | Disruptor | $199 vs. $399 |
| None | Unique | Value-based (set the market) | Whatever value dictates |

### Price Testing Framework

```
Phase 1: Initial price (educated guess)
- Based on value calculation + competitor analysis
- Set price at 50% of your calculated value
- Leave room to increase

Phase 2: Test (±30%)
- Run 3 price points: P-30%, P (current), P+30%
- Track conversion rate and revenue per visitor
- Statistical significance: 100+ conversions per variation
- Time needed: 2-4 weeks

Phase 3: Optimize
- Choose the price that maximizes LTV, not just conversions
- Consider: churn rate at each price, support cost, upsell potential
- Factor: long-term value, not just initial conversion

Phase 4: Repeat (quarterly)
- As product improves, value increases → price can increase
- As competition changes, price may need adjustment
- Customer mix changes → pricing tiers may need adjustment
```

## Part 5: Tiered Pricing Based on Value

### Value-Based Tier Design

Don't create tiers based on features. Create tiers based on VALUE delivered.

```
Bad: Basic ($29), Pro ($79), Enterprise ($199)
     [Arbitrary feature split]

Good: Starter ($29), Growth ($79), Scale ($199)
      [Tiers tied to customer size/value]

Better:
Solo ($29/month)
- For freelancers, value = $200/month
- Capture rate: 15%

Team ($99/month)
- For small teams, value = $600/month
- Capture rate: 17%

Business ($299/month)
- For growing companies, value = $2,000/month
- Capture rate: 15%

Enterprise (Custom)
- For large orgs, value = $5K+/month
- Capture rate: varies
```

### Value Metrics

Choose a metric that scales with value:

```
User-based value metric:
- More users = more value = higher price
- Example: Slack, Asana, Linear
- Value alignment: High (more team members = more communication value)

Usage-based value metric:
- More usage = more value = higher price
- Example: Twilio, SendGrid, AWS
- Value alignment: Very high (pay for what you use)

Feature-based value metric:
- More features = more value = higher price
- Example: Most tiered SaaS
- Value alignment: Medium (features don't always correlate with value)

Outcome-based value metric:
- More outcomes = more value = higher price
- Example: Stripe (percentage of transaction), Shopify
- Value alignment: Perfect (you win when customer wins)
```

**Selecting your value metric:**

```
Best value metrics for SaaS:
1. Number of active users
2. Volume of data processed/stored
3. Number of transactions/actions
4. Number of projects/customers/contacts
5. Revenue processed/earned through platform
6. Time saved (hard to meter but powerful)
7. Percentage of cost reduced (consulting-like model)

Worst value metrics for SaaS:
1. Number of features (doesn't scale with value)
2. Number of logins/users (if low correlation to value)
3. Flat price (leaves money on the table)

Choose a metric that:
- Scales with value the customer receives
- Is easy to understand and track
- Feels fair (customer pays more when they get more)
- Is in the customer's control
```

## Part 6: Communicating Value-Based Price

### The Value Proposition by Price

```
At $X/month, here's what you get:

The problem you're solving costs you $Y/month in:
- Time: ___ hours at $___/hour = $___
- Money: $___ in direct costs/revenue
- Risk: $___ in potential losses

Our product eliminates/captures $Y/month in value.
At $X/month, you keep $Y-X/month.

Your ROI: (Y-X)/X = ___%

This is a no-brainer investment.
```

### Pricing Page Value Communication

```
Instead of features, lead with value:

Free Tier:
"Get started. No commitment. See the value yourself."

Pro Tier ($49/month):
"Save 20 hours/month on [task]."
"Most customers see ROI in the first week."

Business Tier ($199/month):
"Save your entire team 100+ hours/month."
"Enterprise-grade [feature] for growing businesses."

Enterprise (Custom):
"Custom solution for your specific needs."
"[Industry] teams using [product] see 3x ROI."
```

### Handling Price Objections

```
Objection: "That's expensive."
Response: "Compared to what? The problem it solves
costs you $X/month. At $Y/month, you save $Z/month."

Objection: "I can do it myself for free."
Response: "You can. But the 10 hours/month it takes
could be worth more than the $49/month subscription."

Objection: "Your competitor charges less."
Response: "They solve 60% of the problem. We solve 100%.
If 60% is enough for you, their product is a good fit."

Objection: "I don't have budget."
Response: "What would it take to justify the investment?
Let's calculate your expected ROI together."
```

## Pricing Psychology

### Anchoring

The first price a customer sees sets an anchor that influences how they evaluate other prices.

```
How to anchor:
1. Show the highest price tier first (or near the top)
2. Show original price before discount
3. Compare to "before" state (manual process cost)
4. Show competitor pricing comparison

Example:
Enterprise: $499/month (anchor — makes Pro look reasonable)
Pro: $99/month (looks cheap compared to $499)
Starter: $29/month (looks like a steal compared to $499)
```

### The Decoy Effect

Add an asymmetrically unattractive option to make another option look better.

```
Digital subscription pricing:
Web only: $59/year
Print only: $129/year (DECOY — nobody wants this)
Web + Print: $129/year (LOOKS AMAZING compared to Print only)

Magazine subscription pricing (classic example from The Economist):
Web only: $59
Print only: $125 (DECOY)
Web + Print: $125 (THE CHOICE)
```

### Charm Pricing

Prices ending in 9, 99, or 95:

```
$49 vs. $50: $49 feels significantly cheaper (and converts better)
$99 vs. $100: $99 is "the $90s" vs. "over $100"

Test: $49 vs $50 can show 20%+ conversion difference

But: For B2B, round numbers ($50, $100, $500) feel more professional.
Test both approaches.
```

### Framing

Present price relative to value, not cost.

```
Bad: "$49/month"
Good: "Less than $1.65/day — less than your morning coffee"
Better: "For $49/month, you save 20 hours — that's $2.45/hour
        for your time. How much do you bill per hour?"

Bad: "$500/month"
Good: "$500/month to eliminate a $2,000/month problem"
Better: "At $500/month, you're capturing 75% of the value
        we deliver. You keep $1,500/month."
```

## Value-Based Pricing Case Studies

### Case Study: Basecamp

**The pricing:** $99/month flat. No per-user pricing.

**The value:** Basecamp saves project managers from the complexity of alternatives. The value of simplicity, clarity, and reduced overhead is hard to quantify but real.

**Why it works:**
- Customers spend $99/month for unlimited users
- Compare to Asana: $50/month for 5 users → as team grows, Asana costs more
- Basecamp's flat pricing captures more value from larger teams
- For a team of 20, Basecamp at $99 is cheaper than Asana at $240

**Lesson:** Pricing can capture value from different segments differently. Flat pricing can be a bargain for large customers and premium for small ones.

### Case Study: CRM Pricing

```
CRM Example:

HubSpot: $50-5,000/month (tiered)
Salesforce: $25-300+/user/month
Pipedrive: $15-99/user/month
Close: $25-100/user/month

Value varies dramatically:
- A 10-person sales team generating $2M/month in pipeline
  → A CRM enabling 10% more conversions = $200K/month value
  → Even $5K/month CRM = 2.5% capture

Value-based pricing for this segment:
- Price as percentage of customer's sales revenue
- Sales team tracking $100K/month in deals: $100-500/month CRM
- Sales team tracking $1M/month in deals: $1,000-5,000/month CRM
```

## The Solo Founder's Value-Based Pricing Playbook

### Month 1: Research
```
Week 1: Calculate your product's value (time, cost, revenue, risk)
Week 2: Conduct pricing interviews (Van Westendorp, WTP)
Week 3: Analyze competitor pricing
Week 4: Set initial price (based on value + research)
```

### Month 2-3: Test
```
Test 3 prices: P-30%, P, P+30%
Each with 100+ conversions for significance
Track: conversion rate, revenue per visitor, churn
Select price that maximizes LTV
```

### Quarter 4+: Optimize
```
Quarterly reviews:
- Has value increased? (new features)
- Has market changed? (new competitors, economy)
- Is churn correlated with price?
- Are there segments that would pay more?

Adjust accordingly — but grandfather existing customers.
```

## Final Thoughts

Value-based pricing is not a one-time exercise. As your product improves, the value it delivers grows. You should be increasing prices over time.

The best price is the one that:
- The customer happily pays (they feel they got a deal)
- You feel good about (you're fairly compensated for value)
- Leaves room for both parties to win (not zero-sum)

If your customers don't think they got a bargain, your price is too high. If you don't think you're fairly compensated, your price is too low.

**Price on value, not cost. Price for the customer, not for yourself. Price for the long term, not the quick sale.**
