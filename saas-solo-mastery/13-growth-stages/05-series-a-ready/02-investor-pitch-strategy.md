# Pitching to Investors: Finding the Right Investors, Warm Intros, Pitch Deck, Due Diligence

## Why Fundraising Is Different for Solo Founders

As a solo founder, you face unique challenges in fundraising:

- **Team risk:** Investors worry about single points of failure
- **Bandwidth:** You're building the product AND fundraising — something will slip
- **Inexperience:** Most solo founders haven't raised before
- **Network:** Solo founders often have smaller networks than founding teams

But you also have unique advantages:
- **Capital efficiency:** Bootstrapped or lean solo founders spend money wisely
- **Decision speed:** No co-founder disagreements; you can close investors quickly
- **Founder-market fit:** Solo founders are often deep experts in their domain
- **Authenticity:** Your personal story is compelling and unique

This guide covers the complete fundraising process for solo founders, from preparation to closing.

## Phase 1: Fundraising Readiness Check (Before You Start)

### The Self-Assessment

Before reaching out to any investor, answer these:

```
1. Do you need to raise?
   - Can you reach your goals bootstrapped?
   - What does capital unlock that organic growth cannot?
   - Is your business capital-efficient enough that funding accelerates, not sustains?

2. Are investors the right source?
   - Have you considered revenue-based financing, grants, or debt?
   - Do you want the strings attached to VC (board, pressure, dilution)?

3. Is your business ready?
   - $30K+ MRR with 15%+ MoM growth (for B2B SaaS)
   - Strong retention (NRR > 100%)
   - Clear path to $10M+ ARR
   - Growing market with defensible position

4. Are YOU ready?
   - Can you handle 50+ rejection conversations?
   - Do you have 10-20 hours/week for fundraising?
   - Can you continue running the business while fundraising?
```

### The Solo Founder's Fundraising Readiness Scorecard

Score yourself 1-5 on each:

```
___ Product-market fit (Sean Ellis 40%+, retention flattening)
___ Revenue momentum ($30K+ MRR, 15%+ MoM for 6+ months)
___ Unit economics (LTV:CAC > 3:1, payback < 12 months)
___ Market size (TAM > $1B or clear path to it)
___ Founder-market fit (deep expertise, unique insight)
___ Story clarity (can explain the business in 60 seconds)
___ Warm network (10+ VC connections or path to intros)
___ Personal runway (12+ months of savings/ramen)
___ Emotional readiness (handle rejection, maintain momentum)

Total: ___ / 45

Score interpretation:
40+ = Ready to fundraise now
30-40 = Need to strengthen weak areas first
20-30 = Focus on business before fundraising
< 20 = Build more traction; too early
```

## Phase 2: Finding the Right Investors

### Investor Fit Matrix

Not all money is good money. Find investors who fit:

```
Criteria for Investor Fit:

Stage fit
  - Seed: $500K-2M checks, pre-PMF or early PMF
  - Series A: $2-10M checks, proven PMF, scaling

Sector fit
  - Do they invest in your category?
  - Do they understand your business model?
  - Example: Consumer VCs won't understand enterprise SaaS

Check size
  - Can they write your target check?
  - Do they lead rounds?
  - Do they reserve for follow-on?

Value add
  - Domain expertise in your space
  - Network for customers and partners
  - Operational support (hiring, strategy)
  - Solo founder friendly (understand your situation)

Personality
  - Alignment on vision and strategy
  - Communication style match
  - Respect for founder autonomy
  - References from other founders (call them!)
```

### Investor Target List

Build a list of 50-100 investors:

```
Tier 1: Ideal (10-20 investors)
- Invest at your stage and sector
- Lead rounds
- Strong reputation
- Add value in your specific domain
- Warm intro possible

Tier 2: Good (20-30 investors)
- Invest at your stage
- May or may not lead
- Decent reputation
- Generalist but solid firm

Tier 3: Fallback (20-50 investors)
- Might be interested
- May need to be educated on your space
- Smaller checks or less relevant
- Cold outreach acceptable
```

**Sources for investor names:**

```
1. Crunchbase: Search for investments in your category + stage
2. AngelList: Search by sector, check size, stage
3. PitchBook (paid): Most comprehensive
4. Twitter: Follow VC partners, see who they invest in
5. Your investors' portfolio pages: Who else is in their portfolio?
6. Competitor announcements: Who invested in similar companies?
7. Meetups/events: Meet investors in person
8. Warm referrals: Ask other founders who they recommend

For each investor, document:
- Name, title, firm
- Email, Twitter, LinkedIn
- Recent investments (3-5)
- Why they're a fit (specific reason)
- Who can make a warm intro
- Status (Contacted, Meeting, Passed, Term Sheet)
```

## Phase 3: Warm Intros (The Only Way to Start)

### Why Cold Outreach Doesn't Work

Investors receive hundreds of cold emails per week. They ignore 99% of them.

```
Warm intro conversion: 30-50% → meeting
Cold email conversion: 1-5% → meeting

A warm intro is 10x more likely to get a meeting.
```

### The 7 Warm Intro Sources

```
1. Other founders in their portfolio
   "Hey [Founder], I'm building [company]. You're in [VC]'s 
   portfolio. Would you be open to an intro?"

2. Angel investors who've co-invested with them
   "Hey [Angel], I know you've co-invested with [VC]. I'm raising my 
   round and would love an intro if you think it's a fit."

3. Advisors or board members
   "Hey [Advisor], I'm looking to meet [VC Partner]. Do you know them?"

4. Lawyers, accountants, or bankers who work with them
   "I'm working with [Law Firm] who's connected to several VCs. 
   Can they make an intro?"

5. Event or conference connections
   "We met at [Conference]. I've made progress since then and 
   would love to reconnect."

6. Twitter or LinkedIn engagement (lowest warm level)
   "I've been following your content on [topic]. We're building 
   in this space and I'd value your perspective."

7. Mutual connections on LinkedIn
   "We have [X] mutual connections. [Name] suggested I reach out."
```

### The Intro Request

When asking for an intro, make it easy for the introducer:

```
Bad intro request:
"Can you introduce me to [VC]?"

Good intro request:
"Would you be open to introducing me to [VC Partner] at [Firm]? 
Here's a blurb you can forward:

'I've been following [Founder Name]'s journey with [Company Name]. 
They're building [one-line value prop] and have hit [key metric] 
with [traction detail]. I think this could be relevant for your 
focus on [sector/theme]. Would you be open to a conversation?'

Happy to provide more context if helpful."
```

### The Warm Intro Email Template

When the VC receives the intro, your email should be:

```
Subject: [Name] / [Company] — intro from [Mutual Connection]

Hi [VC Name],

[Mutual Connection] suggested I reach out.

I'm building [Company Name]. We help [target audience] [solve 
problem] by [unique approach].

Key traction:
- $XXK MRR
- XX% MoM growth (XX months)
- XX% NRR
- XX customers including [notable logos]

We're raising a [Seed/Series A] round of $X to [specific use of 
funds].

Would you be open to a 30-minute conversation?

Relevant context:
- I'm a solo founder. [Brief reason why this is an advantage]
- [Market stat] → TAM of $XB
- [1-2 sentence competitive differentiation]

Here's our deck and metrics: [link]

Best,
[Name]
[Link to LinkedIn/Twitter]
```

## Phase 4: The Pitch Deck

### The Solo Founder's 10-Slide Deck

Keep it to 10-12 slides. Investors spend 2-3 minutes on the first pass.

```
Slide 1: Title
- Company name + logo
- One-line description
- Your name + title
- "Solo Founder" (if advantageous to mention)

Slide 2: Problem
- Single, specific problem
- Who experiences it and how it feels
- Current alternatives (how they solve it today)
- "Before the product" story (make it visceral)

Slide 3: Solution
- Your product in one sentence
- Screenshot or GIF of the product (not a logo)
- How it solves the problem differently

Slide 4: Why Now? (Market Timing)
- What changed that makes this idea possible?
- Trends, technology shifts, regulation changes
- "Why wasn't this built 5 years ago?"

Slide 5: Product
- 3-5 key features with user-facing benefits
- Magic moment: "Users experience [value] in [time]"
- Product roadmap (what you'll build with funding)

Slide 6: Traction
- Revenue chart (MRR over time — hockey stick preferred)
- Key metrics: MRR, growth rate, NRR, gross margin, LTV:CAC
- Customer logos (if notable)
- "We hit [milestone] in [timeframe]"

Slide 7: Business Model
- How you make money (pricing tiers, usage-based, etc.)
- Average revenue per customer
- Unit economics (CAC, LTV, payback period)
- Path to $10M+ ARR

Slide 8: Market Size
- TAM: $XB (endpoint)
- SAM: $XM (reachable now)
- Expansion path: "We start here, expand here, end here"
- Analyst data or bottom-up calculation

Slide 9: Competitive Landscape
- 2x2 matrix (features vs. market) or table
- Your position: unique intersection
- Competitive moats: network effects, data, brand, distribution
- Why you'll win (not just "better product")

Slide 10: Team / Founder
- Your background (relevant experience)
- "Why I'm the right person to build this"
- Key hires planned with funding
- Advisors or notable supporters

Slide 11: Financials + Ask
- Revenue projection (3 years)
- Key assumptions (growth rate, churn, pricing)
- Funding ask: $X for Y% equity
- Use of funds: [Product: 40%, Marketing: 30%, Team: 20%, Ops: 10%]

Slide 12: Vision + Closing
- Where the company will be in 5 years
- "Help us [transform/make/change] [industry/problem]"
- Contact info
- "We'd love to have you on this journey"
```

### Deck Design Principles

```
1. One idea per slide
   - Don't cram multiple concepts
   - Each slide should have ONE key takeaway

2. Visuals over text
   - Use screenshots, charts, diagrams
   - No more than 10-15 words per slide (except traction)

3. Big font
   - 30pt minimum for body text
   - 60pt+ for headlines
   - Investors may view on phone

4. Revenue chart goes up and to the right
   - Monthly MRR chart with clear trajectory
   - Log scale if growth is exponential
   - Annotate key events (launch, pivot, feature release)

5. Be honest about weaknesses
   - If you have gaps, address them proactively
   - "We know [limitation] and plan to address by [action]"
   - Honesty builds trust more than perfect answers
```

### The "Solo Founder" Slide Considerations

Some investors see solo founder as risk. Address it:

```
On your team slide or verbally:

"I'm a solo founder by choice, not by accident. [Reason]: I built 
this myself to validate demand before building a team. Now that 
we have PMF, I'm using this round to hire [roles].

I have [X] years of experience in [domain]. I've built [other 
accomplishments]. I'm not a first-time founder looking for a 
co-founder — I'm a proven builder hiring a team."

Alternatively, have a "shadow advisor" or part-time CXO who 
fills gaps:
- Technical co-founder or CTO (if you're non-technical)
- Sales advisor (if you're product-focused)
- Industry expert (if you're new to the space)
```

## Phase 5: The Fundraising Process

### The Solo Founder's Fundraising Timeline

```
Week 1-2: Preparation
- Finalize pitch deck
- Prepare data room
- Build investor target list (50+)
- Secure 5-10 warm intros

Week 3-6: First Meetings
- 3-5 investor meetings per week
- Iterate on pitch based on feedback
- Send follow-ups within 24 hours
- Track all interactions in CRM

Week 4-8: Partner Meetings
- First meetings lead to partner meetings
- More detailed due diligence
- Begin competitive process (create FOMO)

Week 6-10: Due Diligence
- Financial data review
- Customer calls
- Legal review
- Reference calls

Week 8-12: Term Sheet + Close
- Review term sheet (get legal counsel)
- Negotiate terms
- Legal closing
- Announce round
```

### Meeting Structure

The initial 30-minute VC meeting:

```
5 min: Your pitch (elevator pitch + key highlights)
    - Problem, solution, traction, ask
    - Don't read slides — tell a story

15 min: VC asks questions
    - Be prepared, be honest
    - "I don't know" is better than making up an answer

5 min: VC describes their firm
    - What they invest in, how they add value
    - Take notes — this helps you decide if they're right

5 min: Your questions
    - "Why are you interested in our space?"
    - "How do you support solo founders?"
    - "Who are the best founders you've worked with?"

5 min: Next steps
    - "What's the process from here?"
    - "When should I expect to hear back?"
    - "Is there anything else I can provide?"
```

### Common Investor Questions (And How to Answer)

```
Q: "Why are you a solo founder?"
A: "I intentionally stayed solo to validate demand before building 
a team. Now I'm ready to hire. I have [advisor/network] covering 
gaps."

Q: "What if you can't scale?"
A: "I've built systems for [support, onboarding, marketing]. My 
first hires are planned for [specific time]."

Q: "What's your moat?"
A: "[Specific moat: data network effects, brand, regulatory, 
proprietary tech]. We're building [another moat] with [specific 
strategy]."

Q: "How will you use the funding?"
A: "[Product development] to accelerate [roadmap item]. [Growth 
marketing] to scale [winning channel]. [Hiring] for [key roles]."

Q: "What are your biggest risks?"
A: "[Risk 1] — we're addressing it by [action]. [Risk 2] — we're 
addressing it by [action]. [Risk 3] — we're monitoring."

Q: "Why now?"
A: "[Technology/market change] has made this possible. [Trend] 
creates urgency. [Timing] is optimal because [reason]."

Q: "What traction milestone would make this a no-brainer?"
A: "We believe [specific metric/event] will be a clear signal. 
We're on track to hit that in [time]."

Q: "What's your ideal outcome?"
A: "We're building a [$100M+/$1B+] company. [Specific vision for 
industry change]."

Q: "Why us / this firm?"
A: "We researched [firm]'s focus on [sector/stage]. [Partner]'s 
background in [domain] is relevant. Your portfolio company [Name] 
validates your approach."
```

### Managing the Fundraising Pipeline

Use a CRM to track every interaction:

```
Investor CRM Fields:

Name: [First, Last]
Firm: [Name]
Partner: [Yes/No]
Stage: [Target/Contacted/Meeting 1/Meeting 2/Partner Meeting/Due Diligence/Term Sheet/Closed/Pass]
Warm Intro: [Name of introducer]
Notes: [Meeting summary, key points, objections]
Follow-up: [Date + Action]
Relationship: [Cold/Lukewarm/Warm]
Priority: [High/Medium/Low]
```

**Pipeline targets:**

```
50 initial targets → 20 first meetings → 10 partner meetings → 
5 term sheets → 2-3 offers → 1-2 close
```

**Pipeline management tips:**
- Batch meetings (Tue/Wed/Thu — avoid Mondays and Fridays)
- Have parallel conversations (creates FOMO)
- Set deadlines (soft or hard closes)
- Don't stop meeting until you have a signed term sheet

## Phase 6: Due Diligence

### What VCs Check During Due Diligence

```
Financial:
  - Bank statements (verify revenue)
  - Stripe or billing records
  - P&L statements (last 12-24 months)
  - Tax returns (last 2 years)
  - Cap table and ownership
  - Expense breakdown
  - ARR calculation methodology

Customer:
  - Customer list (top 10-20 by revenue)
  - Customer calls (VC will call 3-5 customers)
  - Churn analysis
  - NPS or satisfaction data
  - Contract terms (annual/monthly, commitments)

Legal:
  - Incorporation documents
  - IP assignment (do you own your code?)
  - Trademarks and patents
  - Contracts with partners, employees, contractors
  - Privacy policy and terms of service
  - Compliance (GDPR, SOC2, etc.)

Product:
  - Codebase review (sometimes)
  - Architecture overview
  - Security practices
  - Roadmap and priorities
  - Technical debt assessment

Team:
  - Background checks
  - References
  - Key person risk assessment
  - Hiring plan
```

### Customer Reference Calls

VCs will want to talk to your customers. Prepare them:

```
Prep your references:

"Hi [Customer Name], an investor interested in [Company Name] 
may reach out to learn about your experience. Here's what to 
expect:

1. They'll ask: 'How did you find [Product]? What problem does 
it solve? How much value does it provide? Would you be 
disappointed without it?'

2. What would be helpful: Be honest and specific. If you've 
saved time or money, mention the numbers. If you'd recommend 
us, say so.

3. What to avoid: Don't oversell. Just share your genuine 
experience.

Thank you — your feedback helps us build a better company."
```

## Phase 7: Term Sheet and Closing

### Key Term Sheet Terms for Solo Founders

```
Valuation:
  Pre-money: Value of company before investment
  Post-money: Pre-money + investment amount
  Dilution: What % you're giving up

Liquidation preference:
  Standard: 1x non-participating (VC gets their money back, 
  or converts to common)
  Aggressive: 2x+ participating (VC gets multiple AND shares 
  remaining)

Board composition:
  Standard: 2 founders + 1 VC + 1 independent
  Solo founder: Consider 1 you + 1 VC + 1 independent
  Protect yourself: Don't give VC board control

Vesting:
  Standard: 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff
  Accelerated vesting: Negotiate for termination without cause

Pro-rata rights:
  Right to participate in future rounds
  Important for maintaining ownership

Information rights:
  Monthly/quarterly reporting requirements
  Standard for VC deals

No-shop clause:
  30-60 days where you can't talk to other investors
  Keep it as short as possible

Legal fees:
  VC pays their own legal fees (standard)
  Or split (less common)
```

### The Solo Founder's Term Sheet Checklist

```
Before signing:

- [ ] Have a lawyer review the term sheet (spend $1-3K, it's worth it)
- [ ] Understand liquidation preference
- [ ] Board composition is founder-friendly (no VC control)
- [ ] Vesting is standard (4yr/1yr cliff)
- [ ] No personal guarantees
- [ ] No unusual debt covenants
- [ ] Pro-rata rights are reasonable
- [ ] Information rights are standard
- [ ] No-shop clause is reasonable (30 days max)
- [ ] Legal fees are covered by VC or shared
- [ ] Advisor or founder-friendly VC partner is on board
```

### Closing

The legal process takes 2-4 weeks.

```
Closing timeline:

Week 1: Term sheet signed
  - Lawyer reviews, you negotiate key terms
  - Both parties agree and sign term sheet

Week 2-3: Legal documentation
  - Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA)
  - Investors' Rights Agreement
  - Right of First Refusal / Co-Sale
  - Voting Agreement
  - Your lawyer reviews AND the VC's lawyer drafts

Week 3-4: Closing
  - All documents signed
  - Money wired to company account
  - Press release (if desired)
  - Thank your investors
  - Get back to building
```

## Phase 8: Life After Fundraising

### What Changes When You Take VC Money

```
Immediate changes:
- You have a board (reporting, meetings, governance)
- You have investors to update (monthly/quarterly)
- You have a clock (VCs expect a return in 7-10 years)
- You have expectations (growth targets, milestones)

Pressure changes:
- Growth expectations increase (from 15% MoM to 20%+ MoM)
- Hiring pressure (grow team fast)
- Spend pressure (invest the capital)
- Exit pressure (IPO or acquisition eventually)

What doesn't change:
- You still need to build a great product
- You still need happy customers
- You still need to manage cash
- You still wake up every day and choose what to work on
```

### Managing Investor Relationships

```
Don't:
- Hide bad news (it always comes out)
- Miss deadlines without communicating
- Take meetings with every investor request
- Forget they're your partners, not your bosses
- Over-promise and under-deliver

Do:
- Send updates consistently (even if there's no news)
- Ask for help specifically ("I need an intro to X")
- Celebrate wins with them
- Be transparent about challenges early
- Set realistic expectations

Remember:
- VCs work for you (you're their investment)
- But they can also fire you (if things go badly)
- Good relationships make everything easier
- Bad relationships are worse than no investment
```

## Common Fundraising Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Raising Too Early
- Raised $2M with $5K MRR → Spent $1.5M finding PMF → Need another round at bad terms
- Solution: Wait until you have clear PMF and strong growth

### Mistake 2: Raising Too Late
- Bootstrapped to $100K MRR → Want to grow faster → Takes 6 months to raise → Missed market window
- Solution: Start fundraising process 6 months before you need the money

### Mistake 3: Not Building Relationships Before Fundraising
- Cold emailing VCs → Low response rate → Takes longer → Creates desperation
- Solution: Start building VC relationships 12 months before you raise

### Mistake 4: Not Having a Pipeline
- Talking to 1-2 VCs at a time → No leverage → Bad terms
- Solution: Run 20+ parallel conversations → Create competition → Better terms

### Mistake 5: Lying or Exaggerating
- Fudging metrics → Caught in due diligence → Trust broken → Deal dies
- Solution: Be accurate. If you're not sure, say "approximately" or "I'll confirm."

### Mistake 6: Taking the Wrong Money
- First offer → Accept → Wrong VC partner → 7 years of difficult board meetings
- Solution: Vet investors as much as they vet you. Call references.

### Mistake 7: Stopping Fundraising After Getting a Term Sheet
- Get one term sheet → Stop talking to others → Negotiation leverage disappears
- Solution: Keep conversations going until the money is in the bank

## Final Thoughts

- **Fundraising is a full-time job for 2-3 months.** You cannot build product effectively while raising. Plan accordingly.

- **Rejection is not personal.** VCs pass for a thousand reasons: fund focus, check size, partner bandwidth, market timing. Most have nothing to do with you.

- **Your solo founder status can be an asset.** Capital efficiency, resourcefulness, and deep customer understanding are hard to replicate. Some VCs specifically seek solo founders.

- **The best fundraise is one you don't need.** The strongest negotiation position is "I don't need this money." Grow your business to the point where you're choosing investors, not begging them.

- **The relationship after the check matters more than the check.** Choose investors who will be good partners for the long journey ahead.

- **You built a business from nothing as a solo founder.** Fundraising is just another challenge. You're more than capable of handling it.

Good luck. The money is out there. Go find the right partners for your journey.
