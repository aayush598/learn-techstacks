# Making Your First Hire

## The Most Important Decision You'll Make

Hiring your first employee is the scariest and most important decision in a solo founder's journey. It's the moment you transition from solo operator to team builder. Get it right, and your business accelerates. Get it wrong, and you lose time, money, and momentum — and the emotional toll is enormous.

**The hard truth:** Your first hire will likely either be your best hire ever or your worst mistake. There is very little middle ground.

## When to Hire Your First Employee

### The Readiness Checklist

Before hiring, honestly assess:

```
Revenue Readiness:
  ☐ MRR is stable (not fluctuating wildly month to month)
  ☐ MRR is at least 3-5x the employee's monthly salary
  ☐ You have 6+ months of payroll in the bank
  ☐ You have a clear growth trajectory (not flat)

Operational Readiness:
  ☐ You have documented processes for the role
  ☐ You have the tools and systems needed
  ☐ You can spend 10+ hours/week on management
  ☐ Your product is stable enough to have a new person work on it

Personal Readiness:
  ☐ You've hit a ceiling (can't grow without help)
  ☐ You're spending >20 hours/week on tasks you'd offload
  ☐ You've tried outsourcing and contractors first
  ☐ You're ready to give up control
  ☐ You know exactly what you need (not "someone to help")
```

**Minimum thresholds (US/international full-time):**

| Metric | US-based Hire | Remote (Global) Hire |
|--------|--------------|---------------------|
| Minimum MRR | $15,000-20,000 | $8,000-12,000 |
| Months of payroll saved | 6+ | 6+ |
| Hours you spend on delegable tasks | 30+/week | 20+/week |
| Time until hire pays for itself | 3-6 months | 1-3 months |

### Signs You're Ready

**You SHOULD hire when:**

1. **You have more revenue opportunities than you can pursue** — Leads are going cold, customers are waiting, and you're the bottleneck.

2. **You're neglecting core parts of the business** — Customer support is suffering, product quality is declining, or marketing has stopped entirely because you can't do everything.

3. **You're working 60+ hours/week consistently** — And you've already automated and streamlined everything you can.

4. **You have a clear, high-value role to fill** — Not just "someone to help" but a specific set of responsibilities.

5. **You've tested the role with a contractor** — You know the work can be done by someone else and you know what good looks like.

**You should NOT hire when:**

1. **You haven't maximized your own efficiency** — Have you automated everything possible? Have you tried contractors and VAs first?

2. **You're hiring out of desperation or loneliness** — An employee won't fix burnout or loneliness. In fact, managing someone may make it worse.

3. **The role is vague** — "Need someone to help with the business" is not a job description.

4. **Revenue is unpredictable** — If you have one or two big customers making up most of your revenue, losing them would mean you can't make payroll.

5. **You're not ready to manage** — Hiring means meetings, reviews, feedback, performance management, and possibly firing. If that sounds worse than doing the work yourself, you're not ready.

### The Try-Before-You-Hire Path

The lowest-risk path to your first employee:

```
Step 1: Hire a contractor on Upwork (1-3 months)
  → Test the role, the working relationship, and your ability to manage
  → Cost: Hourly, no commitment
  → Risk: Low

Step 2: Increase hours to part-time (3-6 months)
  → 20+ hours per week, more responsibility
  → They learn your business deeply
  → You learn what it's like to depend on someone
  → Cost: Part-time salary
  → Risk: Medium

Step 3: Offer full-time employment (6+ months)
  → If they've proven themselves and you can afford it
  → Convert from contractor to employee
  → Cost: Full salary + benefits
  → Risk: High (but mitigated by trial period)
```

## Who to Hire First

### The Four First-Hire Archetypes

For most solo SaaS founders, your first hire should be one of these:

**1. Customer Support / Success ($35-65k/yr)**

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Frees up the most time (you spend hours on support) | Can feel disconnected from "building" |
| Direct impact on customer happiness | May not reduce dev workload |
| Learnable (don't need deep technical skills) | Onboarding can take 2-3 months |
| Customer insights improve product | |

**When to hire for support:**
- You're spending 15+ hours/week on support
- Support backlog is growing faster than you can respond
- Customer satisfaction is declining (response time)
- You have 200+ active users

**Where to find:**
- Former power users of your product
- Customer-facing roles at other SaaS companies
- Remote talent from Upwork (conversion to full-time)
- Philippines, India, Eastern Europe (lower cost, good English)

**2. Developer / Engineer ($80-150k/yr)**

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Directly accelerates product development | Most expensive hire |
| You can focus on strategy and architecture | Hard to vet technical skill |
| Frees you from coding | Risk: you lose your own coding edge |
| Easier to find remotely | |

**When to hire a developer:**
- You have 6+ months of features in the backlog
- You're spending 30+ hours/week coding
- Technical debt is accumulating faster than you can fix it
- You have paying customers depending on reliability
- You've been successful with freelance developers

**Where to find:**
- Your network (referrals from other developers/CTOs)
- GitHub contributors in relevant open-source projects
- Technical communities (Hacker News, Stack Overflow)
- Remote-first (global talent pool)

**3. Marketing / Growth ($50-90k/yr)**

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Drives revenue growth | Harder to measure impact directly |
| Can be content, social, SEO, or growth | Requires clear strategy (you must guide) |
| Often pays for itself quickly | Many applicants, few great ones |

**When to hire a marketer:**
- You have a clear marketing channel that works
- You know what needs to be done but don't have time
- Content creation has stalled
- You have the budget for ad spend
- You have a product that's ready for scale

**Where to find:**
- Indie Hackers, MicroConf community
- Content marketers in your industry
- Referrals from other SaaS founders
- Part-time → full-time conversion from a contractor

**4. Operations / Business ($40-70k/yr)**

| Pros | Cons |
|------|------|
| Handles everything else | This is a catch-all role |
| Frees you completely | Hard to define clear responsibilities |
| Can grow into COO/Chief of Staff | May not have deep domain expertise |

**When to hire an operator:**
- You're spending time on admin, finance, vendor management
- You need someone to manage other hires
- Your business has grown complex (multiple revenue streams, channels)
- You want to focus entirely on product/strategy

**Where to find:**
- Generalist operators from startups
- Former founders (great understanding of your challenges)
- Project managers looking for more ownership

### Total Cost of Employment (US)

For a US employee, the total cost is not just the salary:

```
Base Salary: 1.00x
  → Payroll taxes (Social Security, Medicare): +7.65%
  → Workers' comp insurance: +0.5-2%
  → Health insurance (individual): +$5-8k/year
  → Health insurance (family): +$15-25k/year
  → 401k matching (optional but expected): +3-4%
  → Equipment (laptop, monitor, etc.): +$2-4k initial
  → Software licenses: +$1-3k/year
  → Training/development: +$1-2k/year
  → Office (if co-working or remote stipend): +$2-6k/year

Total multiplier: 1.25x - 1.4x

So a $80,000 salary actually costs $100,000-$112,000/year
```

## Equity vs Salary

### The Solo Founder's Equity Problem

As a solo founder, you own 100% of your company. Giving away equity is painful. But for your first employee, equity can:
- Offset lower salary (you can't pay market rates)
- Align incentives (they care about success)
- Build loyalty (they're part of something)
- Attract talent you can't afford otherwise

### Equity Grant Guidelines for First Employee

**How much to give:**

| Role | Early Stage (Pre-revenue) | Growth Stage ($5-20k MRR) | Scale Stage ($20k+ MRR) |
|------|-------------------------|--------------------------|------------------------|
| First developer | 2-8% | 1-3% | 0.5-1% |
| First support | 1-3% | 0.5-1% | 0.25-0.5% |
| First marketer | 2-5% | 1-2% | 0.5-1% |
| First ops/biz | 2-5% | 1-2% | 0.5-1% |
| Co-founder (not first hire) | 30-50% | N/A | N/A |

**Vesting Schedule (Standard):**

```
Standard: 4-year vesting with 1-year cliff
  → Cliff: No equity vests in year 1
  → If they leave before 1 year: they get nothing
  → After 1 year: 25% vests (1/4 of total)
  → Monthly vesting after: Remaining 75% vests over next 36 months

Example: 4% equity grant
  → Month 0-12: No vesting (cliff)
  → Month 12: 1% vests (25% of 4%)
  → Month 13: 1.0833% vests
  → ... (1/48th per month)
  → Month 48: Fully vested at 4%
```

**Why the cliff matters:**
- Prevents someone from joining, getting equity, and leaving quickly
- Filters out people who aren't committed (those who won't stay a year)
- Standard across the industry — no negotiation needed

**Acceleration clauses:**
- **Single trigger:** Equity accelerates if you fire them without cause (rare for first hire)
- **Double trigger:** Equity accelerates if they're fired AND the company is acquired (standard)
- For first hire: Keep it simple. Standard vesting, no acceleration.

### The Salary + Equity Tradeoff

```
Example: Hiring a developer for your $10k MRR SaaS

Option A: Higher salary, no equity
  → $100k salary + benefits = $125k total
  → Cost: $10,417/month (your entire MRR)
  → Not feasible

Option B: Lower salary + equity
  → $70k salary + benefits = $88k total
  → Cost: $7,333/month (73% of MRR)
  → 2% equity (staggering over 4 years)
  → Feasible? Tight, but possible

Option C: Modeest salary + meaningful equity
  → $50k salary + benefits = $63k total
  → Cost: $5,250/month (52% of MRR)
  → 3% equity
  → More affordable

Option D: Part-time / remote international
  → $30-40k salary (contractor, Eastern Europe)
  → No benefits cost
  → 0.5-1% equity
  → Cost: $2,500-3,333/month
  → Very feasible
```

**Rule of thumb:** Your first hire should not cost more than 30-50% of your MRR. If it does, you can't afford a full-time US employee yet — consider part-time, international, or equity-heavy compensation.

### The "Will Work for Equity" Risk

Be wary of candidates who want mostly equity and little salary. This often means:
- They're not confident in their ability to get a job (red flag)
- They're overvaluing your equity (which is likely worth $0 today)
- They may leave quickly when they need money

**Better signal:** Someone who takes a reasonable salary and is excited about equity as a bonus, not a substitute.

## The Hiring Process

### Step 1: Write the Job Description

**The "Solo Founder" job description template:**

```
[Position]: First [Role] at [Company Name]

About us:
[Company Name] is a [brief description] serving [target customers].
We're [revenue amount / stage / growth rate]. I'm a solo founder looking
for [Company Name]'s first employee.

The problem you'll solve:
[What specific problem this role addresses]
E.g., "I'm spending 20 hours/week on customer support instead of building
product. You'll take over support so I can focus on the product roadmap."

What you'll do:
  → [Responsibility 1]
  → [Responsibility 2]
  → [Responsibility 3]
  → (...list 4-6 specific responsibilities)

Who you are:
  → [Quality 1: e.g., "You're excited about working at an early-stage company"]
  → [Quality 2: e.g., "You're comfortable with ambiguity"]
  → [Quality 3: e.g., "You take ownership and work independently"]

What we offer:
  → [Salary range]
  → [Equity range]
  → [Remote/office policy]
  → [Benefits: health insurance? equipment? vacation?]

How to apply:
  → [Send email to X with subject Y]
  → [Include: resume, brief note about why this role interests you, and answer:
    "What would you do in your first 30 days to make an impact?"]

Note about being first employee:
  → This is not a corporate job. You'll have autonomy, direct impact,
    and the opportunity to shape this role as the company grows.
  → It's also not for everyone. You need to be self-directed, comfortable
    with imperfect information, and excited about building from here.
```

### Step 2: Source Candidates

For your first hire, do NOT rely on job boards alone. Use multiple channels:

```
Highest quality:
  → Your personal network — Ask "Who's the best [role] you've ever worked with?"
  → Founder communities (Indie Hackers, MicroConf, SaaS communities)
  → Former colleagues and classmates
  → Referrals from your customers (power users who know your space)

Good quality:
  → LinkedIn (search + outreach)
  → GitHub (developers — look at their code quality, contributions)
  → Specific communities (Dribbble for designers, Stack Overflow for devs)
  → Remote job boards (We Work Remotely, Remote OK, Dynamite Jobs)

Lower quality (use with vetting):
  → Indeed, Monster, traditional job boards
  → Craigslist (very mixed)
  → General postings (lots of noise)
```

### Step 3: Interview Process

**Process structure for first hire:**

```
Stage 1: Application Review
  → Look for: specific, relevant experience; enthusiasm for early-stage
  → Filter out: generic applications, wrong location/timezone, mismatched skills

Stage 2: Screening Call (20-30 min)
  → Your goal: Basic fit assessment
  → Topics: Their experience, expectations, salary, availability
  → Your pitch: Why this role is exciting, honest about challenges

Stage 3: Deep Interview (45-60 min)
  → Their experience deep dive (STAR method)
  → Technical/skills assessment (relevant to the role)
  → Cultural fit / values alignment

Stage 4: Practical Assessment (paid)
  → For support: Handle real support tickets for a day (you review)
  → For developer: Paid test project (4-8 hours, real feature)
  → For marketing: Write a blog post or create a campaign brief
  → For operations: Complete a real ops project

Stage 5: Final Call (30 min)
  → You: "I want you to join. Here's the offer."
  → Them: "Yes, but can we discuss [X,Y,Z]?"

Stage 6: Reference Check
  → Talk to 2-3 former managers or clients
  → Ask: "What's the best thing about working with them?"
         "What's something they struggled with?"
         "Would you work with them again?"

Stage 7: Offer
  → Written offer letter
  → One week to consider
  → Available to answer questions
```

**Interview questions for first hire:**

```markdown
For any role:
  → "Why do you want to join an early-stage company vs an established one?"
  → "What's your experience working with limited resources?"
  → "How do you handle ambiguity?"
  → "Tell me about a time you took initiative without being asked."
  → "What's something you've built from scratch?"
  → "How do you make decisions when there's no precedent?"
  → "What's the hardest feedback you've ever given or received?"
  → "What do you need from me to do your best work?"

For a developer:
  → "Walk me through a project you're proud of — from conception to delivery."
  → "How do you approach debugging a complex issue?"
  → "What's your testing philosophy?"
  → "How do you balance shipping fast vs shipping quality?"

For support:
  → "Tell me about a time you turned around an unhappy customer."
  → "How do you handle being the face of a company?"
  → "What's your system for staying organized under high volume?"

For marketing:
  → "What's a growth experiment you ran that surprised you?"
  → "How do you prioritize marketing channels?"
  → "How do you measure the impact of your work?"
```

## Remote-First Culture — Even With One Employee

### Why Remote-First From Day One

Even if you work from home and your first hire works from home, you need a remote-first culture. Because:

1. Your next hire may be anywhere in the world
2. It builds good habits (documentation, async communication)
3. It scales (you don't have to change when you have 3, 5, or 10 people)
4. It's a competitive advantage (access to global talent)

### Remote-First Principles for a Team of Two

```markdown
1. Write everything down
  → Every decision, every process, every meeting note
  → Async-first: document before (or instead of) meeting
  → If it's not documented, it didn't happen

2. Default to async
  → Use Loom for complex explanations (better than meetings)
  → Use Slack/Linear for day-to-day
  → Batch meetings to one day (or eliminate them entirely)

3. Daily standup (async)
  → In Slack or Linear:
    "What I did yesterday: [X]
     What I'm doing today: [Y]
     Blockers: [Z]"
  → 2 minutes, not 30

4. Weekly 1:1 (30 min, video)
  → Non-negotiable
  → Topics: Priorities, blockers, feedback, growth, well-being
  → This is THEIR time — let them set the agenda

5. Overcommunicate context
  → Share metrics, customer feedback, strategic decisions
  → Don't assume they know what you're thinking
  → A team of two fails when information is siloed

6. Be intentional about connection
  → First 5 minutes of every call: personal check-in
  → Virtual coffee (15 min, no agenda) once/week
  → Celebrate wins publicly

7. Invest in good tools
  → Slack (communication)
  → Linear (task management)
  → Notion (documentation)
  → Zoom/Google Meet (video)
  → GitHub (code collaboration)
```

### The First 90 Days Plan

```
Week 1: Onboarding
  → Set up tools and accounts
  → Read all documentation
  → Shadow your work (watch you do the job)
  → 1:1 daily check-ins
  → Deliverable: "I understand the basics"

Week 2-3: Ramp up
  → Start doing the work with supervision
  → You review everything
  → 1:1 daily → 3x/week
  → Deliverable: "I can do the job with guidance"

Week 4-6: Independence
  → Work independently (you review periodically)
  → 1:1 2x/week
  → Start suggesting improvements
  → Deliverable: "I own the role"

Week 7-12: Ownership
  → Full ownership of their area
  → You provide strategic guidance, not supervision
  → 1:1 weekly
  → Start contributing to planning and strategy
  → Deliverable: "I'm improving the role, not just filling it"
```

## The Emotional Transition

### What It Feels Like

Going from solo to "team" is emotionally complex. You'll feel:

- **Relief** — "Finally, I can focus on what matters"
- **Fear** — "They're going to break something"
- **Impatience** — "It's faster to do it myself"
- **Guilt** — "I'm paying them more than I pay myself"
- **Loss of control** — "Things are being done differently now"
- **Pride** — "I built something that supports another person"

All of these are normal. Acknowledge them without letting them drive decisions.

### Common Mistakes First-Time Managers Make

| Mistake | Why It Happens | How to Avoid |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| **Not letting go** | You're used to doing everything yourself | Set boundaries: "You own this, I won't touch it for 2 weeks" |
| **Too much oversight** | Fear of mistakes | Trust the process you built. Mistakes are learning. |
| **Too little support** | You're busy with your own work | Block 1:1 time. Be available for questions. |
| **Unclear expectations** | You assume they know what to do | Over-communicate. Write it down. Check understanding. |
| **No feedback culture** | Avoiding hard conversations | Give feedback early and often. Don't let issues fester. |
| **Doing their job** | "It's faster if I just do it" | No. Stop. Let them do it. It's your job to build capacity. |
| **Not celebrating** | You're focused on what's next | Pause. Celebrate wins. Build momentum. |

### Managing Up (For You)

As the founder, you report to your own standards. But with an employee, you have new responsibilities:

```
Your new job description as a manager:

1. Remove blockers — Make sure your employee can do their best work
2. Provide context — Connect their work to the company mission
3. Give feedback — Regular, honest, constructive
4. Set direction — Clear priorities and expectations
5. Create safety — Psychological safety to make mistakes and speak up
6. Develop them — Help them grow their skills and career
7. Hold them accountable — For results, not activity
8. Celebrate wins — Recognize good work publicly
```

## The "First Employee" Reality Check

Your first employee will not be a clone of you. They will:
- Work differently than you
- Make mistakes you wouldn't make
- Need more guidance than you expect
- Ask questions you haven't thought about
- See things from a perspective you don't have

**This is the point.** If you wanted a clone, you'd never need to hire. The value of another person is their difference.

### If It Doesn't Work Out

Sometimes your first hire is wrong. Signs:

```
First 30 days:
  → They need more hand-holding than expected
  → Communication is challenging
  → Quality is below expectations
  → → Coach harder. Give specific feedback.

30-60 days:
  → Same issues persist despite feedback
  → They're not improving
  → Team culture is suffering
  → → Have a "clear the air" conversation. Set a 2-week improvement plan.

60-90 days:
  → No improvement
  → You dread interacting with them
  → They're causing more work than they save
  → → It's time to let them go.
```

**How to let your first employee go:**

```
1. Rip the band-aid (don't drag it out)
2. Be direct and honest: "This isn't working out. Here's why."
3. Be generous with severance (2-4 weeks)
4. Have the conversation in the morning (not Friday at 4 PM)
5. Prepare for the conversation: what you'll say, what they'll get
6. Have a witness (if possible)
7. Collect company property
8. Revoke access immediately
9. Send the final paycheck promptly
10. Write a reference letter (that's honest but not harmful)
```

**Learning from a bad hire:**
- What went wrong in the interview?
- What did you overlook?
- What was unclear in the job description?
- How can you improve your hiring process?

## Your First Year With an Employee

```
Month 0-1:
  → Onboarding, tool setup, ramp up
  → Daily 1:1s
  → You do more hands-on teaching
  → Expect lower productivity initially

Month 2-3:
  → Independence on routine tasks
  → 2-3x/week 1:1s
  → You review, they execute
  → Productivity reaches 50-70%

Month 4-6:
  → Full ownership of their area
  → Weekly 1:1s
  → They suggest improvements
  → Productivity reaches 100%
  → First performance review

Month 7-12:
  → Strategic contributions
  → They're training YOU on their area
  → Monthly strategic 1:1s
  → Productivity exceeds 100% (better than you'd have done)
  → Annual review and compensation adjustment
```
