# Finding and Managing Contractors

## The Solo Founder's Contractor Strategy

Contractors are the engine that lets a solo founder operate like a team. For a fraction of the cost of employees, you can access specialized talent on demand — without payroll taxes, benefits, office space, or long-term commitment.

The key difference between successful and failed contractor relationships: **Successful ones treat contractors like partners, not commodities.**

### Finding Freelancers Outside Platforms

**Referral-Based (Highest Success Rate):**
1. Ask other founders in your network: "Who do you use for [specific task]?"
2. Post in founder communities: "Looking for a [role], budget $X/hr, recommendations?"
3. Check LinkedIn: Search for freelancers with relevant skills and experience
4. Twitter/X: Search for "freelance [skill] looking for work" or "open to work"

**Community-Based:**
1. Indie Hackers — Post in the "Looking for Freelancers" section
2. r/forHire or r/freelance — Reddit communities
3. Hacker News "Who's Hiring" and "Seeking Freelancer" threads (monthly)
4. Dev.to or relevant tech community job boards

**Direct Outreach:**
1. Find people writing great content in your space — they likely freelance
2. Look at who's speaking at industry conferences
3. Check GitHub contributors in relevant open-source projects
4. Search Dribbble/Behance for designers whose style matches your brand

### Platform Strategy: Using Upwork Effectively

Upwork is the largest freelance marketplace but can be hit-or-miss without a strategy.

**Posting a job that attracts quality candidates:**

```
Title: Experienced React Developer for SaaS Dashboard (Ongoing)

Description:

I'm building [Product Name], a [brief description] SaaS serving
[mid-market/enterprise] customers. I'm looking for a senior React developer
for an ongoing (10-20 hours/week) collaboration.

What you'll work on:
  - Build new features for our dashboard (React + TypeScript)
  - Integrate third-party APIs (Stripe, SendGrid, PostHog)
  - Write tests (Jest, React Testing Library)
  - Code review and pair programming

Requirements:
  - 5+ years of professional React experience
  - TypeScript expert (no `any` types in our codebase)
  - Experience with GraphQL (Apollo) and Next.js
  - Excellent written English communication
  - Available 10-20 hours/week US timezone overlap

Interview process:
  1. Submit proposal with relevant portfolio
  2. 30-min video call (cultural fit)
  3. Paid test project ($200, ~4 hours)
  4. Ongoing if test goes well

Budget: $50-80/hour (depending on experience)

To show you read this, start your proposal with "I read about your SaaS"
and mention one feature you think it should have.
```

**Screening proposals:**

| Red Flags | Green Flags |
|-----------|-------------|
| Generic copy-paste proposal | References specific parts of your job post |
| No portfolio or examples | Relevant portfolio with similar projects |
| Can't do a video call | Proactive about scheduling a call |
| Over-promises (perfect score, instant delivery) | Realistic about timelines and uncertainty |
| Doesn't ask questions about the project | Asks clarifying questions about the scope |
| Price is significantly below market | Price is in market range ($50-80/hr for senior dev) |
| Profile has unrelated skills | Focused specialization in what you need |

**Upwork's fee structure:**

```
For you (client): No fee to post or hire
For freelancer: 10% fee on earnings (reduces to 5% after $10k with client)

So when you pay a freelancer $50/hr:
  → Freelancer receives $45/hr (Upwork takes $5)
  → This is normal — don't try to negotiate down to account for the fee
```

### Vet-Then-Hire Platforms (Toptal, Gun.io)

These platforms do the vetting for you. The trade-off: higher hourly rates but lower risk.

**Toptal process:**
1. You fill out a brief (skills, timeline, budget)
2. They screen candidates and send 2-3 matches within 48h
3. You interview the candidates
4. If none work, they send more (no-risk trial period)

**When to use Toptal/Gun.io:**
- You need someone to start immediately (no time to vet)
- The work is critical to your business (high risk tolerance needed?)
- You've had bad experiences on Upwork
- You need specialized skills that are hard to find

**When NOT to use Toptal:**
- You have a limited budget (you pay a premium for vetting)
- You enjoy vetting and have the time
- The work is simple (you're overpaying)

## Vetting Contractors

### The Vetting Process

**Stage 1: Portfolio & Resume Review (30 min)**

Look for:
- **Relevant experience** — Have they done similar work for similar companies?
- **Project outcomes** — What results did they achieve (not just "built X")
- **Work quality** — GitHub code quality, writing samples, design portfolio
- **Communication style** — Clear, professional, responsive
- **Red flags** — Gaps, exaggerations, generic descriptions

**Stage 2: Video Call (30-45 min)**

Questions to ask:

```
About their work:
  → "Tell me about a project similar to mine that you worked on"
  → "What was the hardest technical challenge you solved?"
  → "How do you handle scope creep or unclear requirements?"

About collaboration:
  → "How do you prefer to communicate (async/sync)?
  → "What time zone are you in and what are your working hours?"
  → "How do you handle feedback and revisions?"

About reliability:
  → "What happens if you get sick or need time off?"
  → "How do you track and report your time?"
  → "What percentage of your work is ongoing vs one-off projects?"

Hypothetical:
  → "If you joined my project today, what questions would you ask?"
  → "What would your first week look like?"
  → "How would you approach [specific challenge in your project]?"
```

**Stage 3: Paid Test Project (4-8 hours)**

This is the most important step. Pay for a small, representative piece of work.

**Test project guidelines:**
- Should be real work (not make-work)
- Should be completable in 4-8 hours
- Should test the skills you'll need most
- Should include the full workflow (spec → execution → delivery → revision)
- **Always pay for test projects** — if you don't, you attract low-quality candidates

**Test project examples:**

```
For a developer:
  "Implement this small feature: [link to GitHub issue with spec].
  Please include tests and documentation. Budget: 6 hours at your rate."

For a writer:
  "Write a 500-word blog post on [topic relevant to your SaaS].
  Include [specific angle]. Budget: $50."

For a designer:
  "Design the user profile settings page based on this brief: [brief].
  Include desktop and mobile. Budget: $100."

For a VA:
  "Triage these 20 emails and respond to the ones you can.
  For the rest, categorize and draft responses. Budget: 2 hours at your rate."
```

**Evaluation rubric:**

| Criterion | Weight | 1 (Poor) | 3 (Good) | 5 (Excellent) |
|-----------|--------|---------|---------|--------------|
| Quality of output | 30% | Many errors, below expectations | Minor issues, met expectations | Exceeded expectations, no errors |
| Communication | 25% | Poor, slow, confusing | Clear, timely, professional | Proactive, asks good questions |
| Process adherence | 20% | Ignored instructions | Followed instructions | Added value beyond instructions |
| Timeliness | 15% | Late without notice | On time | Delivered early |
| Revisions | 10% | Defensive, can't incorporate feedback | Accepts feedback well | Incorporates feedback quickly |

**Threshold:** Score of 3.5+ → Hire. Score below → Pass.

## Managing Contractors

### Communication

**Asynchronous First, Synchronous Second**

Default to async communication (Slack, email, Loom) to minimize meeting overhead.

```
Daily:
  → Loom video update (2-3 min) for blockers or questions
  → Slack for quick questions

Weekly:
  → 15-30 min video check-in
  → Review progress, blockers, next week's priorities
  → Screen share for complex discussions

Monthly:
  → 30 min retrospective
  → What's going well? What could improve?
```

**Communication guidelines to establish upfront:**

```
1. Response time expectations:
   → My response time during work hours: within 2 hours
   → My response time outside work hours: next business day
   → Your response time: within 4 hours during agreed hours

2. Communication channels:
   → Urgent/blockers: Slack DM (label with [URGENT])
   → Daily updates: Loom video or written summary
   → Questions: GitHub/Asana comments on relevant tasks
   → Deep discussions: Scheduled video call

3. Documentation:
   → All decisions documented in GitHub issues
   → All specs written before work begins
   → All changes logged in project management tool
```

**Loom video guidelines:**

Loom is the single best tool for async contractor communication.

```
When to use Loom:
  → Explaining a complex spec (easier than writing paragraphs)
  → Reviewing their work (point at things on screen)
  → Giving feedback (tone is clearer than text)
  → Pair debugging (walk through an issue together)

Loom structure:
  1. Context (10s): "This video is about [X]"
  2. The message (2-5 min): Show, explain, demonstrate
  3. Action items (30s): "So what I need from you is [Y] by [date]"
```

### Project Management

**Tools for contractor collaboration:**

| Tool | Best For | Why |
|------|----------|-----|
| **GitHub** | Code, issues, PRs | Built-in code review, CI/CD |
| **Linear** | Task management | Fast, keyboard-first, ideal for sprints |
| **Notion** | Specs, docs, knowledge base | Flexible, collaborative |
| **Figma** | Design collaboration | Comments, version history, prototypes |
| **Slack** | Daily communication | Channels, threads, integrations |
| **Google Drive** | Documents, spreadsheets | Familiar, collaborative |

**Setting up access:**

```
Access levels for contractors:
  → GitHub: Write access to specific repos (not admin)
  → Linear: Member role (not admin)
  → Sentry: Limited access to their project's errors
  → AWS: IAM role with limited permissions (not full access)
  → Stripe: Restricted API key (test mode only initially)

Always:
  → Create separate accounts with limited permissions
  → Use Bitwarden shared folders for shared credentials
  → Never give admin access to critical systems
  → Revoke access immediately when engagement ends
```

**Workflow for code contributions (GitHub):**

```markdown
1. Task created in Linear with spec and acceptance criteria
2. Developer creates branch from main: `feature/description`
3. Developer implements feature with tests
4. Developer opens Pull Request with description of changes
5. CI runs (tests, linting, type checking)
6. You review the PR:
   √ Code quality and standards
   √ Test coverage
   √ Security considerations
   √ No sensitive data or credentials
7. Address feedback
8. Merge to main
9. Deploy (automated or manual)
```

**Workflow for content contributions:**

```markdown
1. Task created in Linear with brief and keywords
2. Writer submits first draft in Google Doc (with comments enabled)
3. You review and leave comments
4. Writer incorporates feedback (round 1)
5. Optional: second round of feedback
6. You approve and publish
7. Writer submits invoice (Net-15 payment terms)
```

### Quality Control

**The 80/20 Review Rule:**

Review the first 80% of a contractor's work thoroughly (every line of code, every word). As trust builds over time, reduce to 20% spot-checking.

```
Phase 1 (First 2 weeks): 80% review
  → Review everything
  → Provide detailed feedback
  → Establish quality standards

Phase 2 (Week 3-8): 50% review
  → Review critical paths and suspicious code
  → Less detailed on boilerplate

Phase 3 (Month 3+): 20% review
  → Spot-check
  → Focus on security, edge cases, performance
  → Trust but verify
```

**Code review checklist:**

```markdown
Security:
  ☐ No hardcoded credentials, tokens, or secrets
  ☐ Input validation and sanitization
  ☐ Authentication checks on all protected routes
  ☐ SQL injection prevention (parameterized queries)
  ☐ XSS prevention (escaping output)
  ☐ Rate limiting on sensitive endpoints

Correctness:
  ☐ Meets acceptance criteria
  ☐ Handles edge cases (empty states, errors, race conditions)
  ☐ Error handling (try/catch, proper error messages)
  ☐ Backward compatible (or migration plan)
  ☐ No hardcoded values that should be configuration

Code Quality:
  ☐ Follows project conventions (naming, file structure)
  ☐ Proper TypeScript types (no `any`)
  ☐ Tests pass and cover the new code
  ☐ No dead code or commented-out code
  ☐ Reasonable function complexity (not too long)

Documentation:
  ☐ README or inline docs for complex logic
  ☐ API documentation updated if endpoints changed
  ☐ Environment variables documented if new ones added
```

### Payment & Contracts

**Payment structures:**

| Structure | Best For | Pros | Cons |
|-----------|----------|------|------|
| **Hourly** | Ongoing, scope uncertain | Flexible, fair for both | Hard to budget, no cost certainty |
| **Fixed price** | Well-defined project | Cost certainty, clear deliverables | Scope creep risk, change orders |
| **Milestone** | Multi-phase project | Pay for results, staged delivery | Admin overhead |
| **Monthly retainer** | Ongoing commitment (10+ hrs/week) | Steady income for them, priority for you | Minimum commitment |

**Payment terms:**

```
Standard terms for contractors:
  → Net-15 (pay within 15 days of invoice)
  → Net-30 acceptable for established relationships
  → No net-60 (this is disrespectful to freelancers)

How to pay:
  → Upwork: Through platform (automatic)
  → Direct: Wise, PayPal, or direct bank transfer
  → Always pay on time — this is how you build loyalty
```

**Contracts for contractors:**

Keep it simple. A basic Independent Contractor Agreement should cover:

```markdown
Independent Contractor Agreement

Parties:
  → [Your Company Name] (Client)
  → [Contractor Name] (Contractor)

Services:
  → Description of work to be performed
  → Delivery format and acceptance criteria
  → Timeline and milestones

Payment:
  → Rate ($X/hour or fixed price)
  → Payment terms (Net-15)
  → Invoicing procedure

Intellectual Property:
  → All work product is "work made for hire"
  → Full IP assignment to Client
  → Contractor warrants their work is original

Confidentiality:
  → Contractor will not disclose proprietary information
  → Duration: during + 2 years after engagement

Independent Contractor Status:
  → Contractor is not an employee
  → Contractor handles their own taxes and benefits
  → No exclusivity (contractor can work for others)

Termination:
  → Either party may terminate with 7 days notice
  → Payment for work completed up to termination

[Signatures]
```

**Note on IP assignment:** This is critical. Without explicit IP assignment, the contractor may retain ownership of the code/design they create for you. Every contract must include a work-for-hire and IP assignment clause.

### Time Tracking

For hourly contractors, use a time tracking tool:

| Tool | Best For | Features | Cost |
|------|----------|----------|------|
| **Toggl** | Simple time tracking | Web, desktop, mobile, reports | Free (5 users) |
| **Harvest** | Time + invoicing | Web, mobile, invoicing, reports | $12/mo |
| **Clockify** | Free time tracking | Unlimited users, free | Free |
| **Upwork Tracker** | Upwork hourly contracts | Screenshots, activity monitoring | Built-in |

**How to handle time tracking:**

```markdown
For Upwork hourly contracts:
  → Use Upwork's built-in tracker (takes screenshots)
  → This protects both of you (payment protection for them, proof for you)

For direct contracts:
  → Ask for a weekly time log in Toggl/Clockify
  → Review hours against deliverables
  → Don't micromanage hours — focus on output
  → If someone consistently reports more hours than expected for the output,
    have a conversation about efficiency or scope comprehension
```

## Building Long-Term Relationships

### Retaining Great Contractors

Finding a great contractor is hard. Keeping them is easier but requires effort.

**What contractors value (in order):**
1. **Consistent work** — They don't want to wonder if they'll have hours next week
2. **Paid on time** — Every time, without reminders
3. **Clear communication** — Good specs, timely feedback, no ambiguity
4. **Respect for their time** — No last-minute changes, respect boundaries
5. **Fair rates** — Not necessarily the highest, but fair for the work
6. **Growth opportunities** — Interesting work that builds their portfolio
7. **Positive relationship** — They enjoy working with you

**Practices to retain talent:**

```
Weekly:
  → Clear priorities for the week
  → Quick check-in (15 min video or async)
  → No surprises — communicate changes early

Monthly:
  → On-time payment (always)
  → "Is there anything you need from me to do your best work?"
  → Check-in on workload (too much, too little, just right?)

Quarterly:
  → Rate review (consider increasing if value has grown)
  → Portfolio items (document the project for their portfolio)
  → "How are things going overall? Anything you'd like to change?"

Annually:
  → Rate increase (cost of living + performance-based)
  → Thank-you gift ($100-200, or a nice bottle of wine)
  → Written testimonial for their profile
```

### When to Let a Contractor Go

Not every relationship works out. Signs it's time to move on:

```
Performance issues:
  → Consistent quality below expectations
  → Missed deadlines without communication
  → Doesn't incorporate feedback
  → Hours don't align with output

Communication issues:
  → Unresponsive for extended periods
  → Defensive or dismissive of feedback
  → Doesn't ask questions when confused
  → Misses scheduled check-ins

Reliability issues:
  → Frequent last-minute cancellations
  → Unreliable availability
  → Subcontracts without telling you
  → Works for competitors without disclosure
```

**How to end a contractor relationship professionally:**

```
1. Have a direct conversation (not email):
   "I appreciate the work you've done, but unfortunately I don't think
   this is working out. The [specific issue] has been a challenge.
   I think it's best to part ways. I'm happy to pay for this week's
   work and provide a reference for what went well."

2. Pay for all work completed (including this week)

3. Provide a reference (if genuine):
   "I can confirm they worked on [project] from [date] to [date]."
   Only say positive things that are true.

4. Revoke access:
   → GitHub, Linear, AWS, etc.
   → Do this immediately after the conversation
   → Change any shared credentials

5. Learn from it:
   → What went wrong in your vetting?
   → What could you have done differently in onboarding?
   → Update your processes accordingly
```

## Legal & Tax Considerations

### Contractor vs Employee Misclassification

This is a critical legal issue. Misclassifying an employee as a contractor can result in back taxes, penalties, and lawsuits.

**The IRS test (simplified):**

| Factor | Contractor | Employee |
|--------|-----------|---------|
| Control over schedule | Contractor sets their hours | You set their hours |
| Control over methods | Contractor chooses how to work | You direct how to work |
| Financial investment | Contractor uses their own tools | You provide all tools |
| Profit/loss opportunity | Contractor can work for others | Exclusive to you |
| Relationship duration | Project-based | Ongoing, indefinite |
| Benefits | Contractor pays own benefits | You provide benefits |
| Integration | Work is separate | Work is core to your business |

**US-specific safe practices:**
- Contractor can work for other clients
- Contractor uses their own equipment
- Contractor sets their own hours (within agreed availability)
- Contractor is not on your payroll or benefits
- Contractor invoices you (you don't time-track them like employees)
- You have a written contractor agreement
- Contractor handles their own taxes

**International contractors:**
- Generally lower risk of misclassification claims
- They report income in their own country
- You typically don't need to worry about their local tax laws (but get advice)
- Use platforms like Deel or Remote.com to handle compliance

### Tax Documentation

**US Contractors:**
- They send you a W-9 (you don't send them anything)
- If you pay them > $600/year, you must issue a 1099-NEC
- You (or your accountant) file 1099s with the IRS by January 31

**Non-US Contractors:**
- They are responsible for their own taxes
- You typically don't need to issue any tax forms
- Some countries require withholding (rare for independent contractors)
- Check: do you have a "permanent establishment" risk? (Probably not for occasional contractors)

|--------------|----------------------------|-------------|
| US East | Philippines, India, Eastern Europe | 6-9 AM, 6-9 PM |
| US West | Philippines, India | 6-9 AM (their evening) |

**Best practice:** Try to find contractors with at least 4 hours of overlap with your working day.

## The Contractor Lifecycle Summary

```
FIND → VET → ONBOARD → MANAGE → RETAIN → (GROW OR EXIT)

FIND
  → Platforms (Upwork, Toptal, etc.)
  → Referrals (best source)
  → Communities (Indie Hackers, etc.)

VET
  → Portfolio review
  → Video call interview
  → Paid test project (4-8 hours)
  → Reference check

ONBOARD
  → Contractor agreement signed
  → Access provisioned
  → Tools set up
  → First week: daily check-ins
  → SOPs shared

MANAGE
  → Async-first communication
  → Weekly check-ins
  → Clear specs and acceptance criteria
  → Pay on time (Net-15)
  → Quality reviews (80/20 rule)

RETAIN
  → Consistent work
  → Fair rates
  → Appreciation and feedback
  → Growth opportunities
  → Quarterly reviews

If successful → Grow the relationship (more hours, more responsibility)
If not → Part ways professionally, learn from the experience
```
