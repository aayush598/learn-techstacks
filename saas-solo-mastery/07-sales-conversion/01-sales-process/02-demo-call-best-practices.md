# Demo Call Best Practices

## The Solo Founder's Demo Advantage

As a solo founder, your demo calls are your highest-leverage sales activity. Each call is an opportunity to convert a prospect into a customer without needing a sales team. The good news: you have advantages that no sales rep can match.

**Your advantages:**
- You built the product — you know it better than any salesperson could
- You control the roadmap — you can say "I'll build that" and mean it
- Founder authority — prospects trust you as the creator
- Technical depth — you can answer any technical question immediately
- Decision speed — you can adjust pricing, offer discounts, promise features in real-time

**Your disadvantages:**
- Limited time for sales calls
- Emotional attachment to the product
- Tendency to over-explain features
- Difficulty switching from builder mode to seller mode

This guide will help you maximize every demo call minute.

## Before the Demo: Preparation

### The 15-Minute Prep Ritual

Never take a demo call cold. Spend 15 minutes preparing:

**First 5 minutes — Research:**
- Review their signup data (when they joined, what they've used, what they've explored)
- Look at their LinkedIn profile (role, background, mutual connections)
- Check their company website and Crunchbase
- Identify their likely use case based on their profile
- Note their company size, industry, and tech stack if visible

**Next 5 minutes — Agenda setting:**
- Write down 3 specific problems they likely face
- Choose 3-5 features to demo that map to those problems
- Prepare 1-2 discovery questions about their current workflow
- Identify your goal for the call (demo → next step? demo → close?)

**Final 5 minutes — Environment check:**
- Close all other applications
- Pre-open the relevant parts of your product
- Set up screen sharing (clean your desktop, close tabs)
- Test audio and video
- Have a backup plan (phone number if video fails)
- Prepare a glass of water

### The Pre-Demo Email

Send this 24 hours before the call:

```
Subject: Confirming our call — [Company Name]

Hi [Name],

Looking forward to our call on [day] at [time].

To make the most of our 30 minutes together, it would help me to understand:

1. What's the primary challenge you're trying to solve?
2. What have you tried so far?
3. What would a successful outcome look like for you?

Also, feel free to invite anyone else from your team who should be part of this conversation.

Best,
[Your Name]
```

This serves two purposes: it qualifies the lead (if they don't respond, they're less serious) and it gives you context to tailor the demo.

## The Demo Call Structure

### The Perfect 30-Minute Demo

**0:00-3:00 — Connection and Agenda**
- Thank them for their time
- Quick personal connection (mutual contact, their recent LinkedIn post, their company's latest news)
- State the agenda: "I'd like to spend the first 10 minutes understanding your situation, then 15 minutes showing how we help, and 5 minutes discussing next steps. Does that work?"
- Confirm recording permission: "Would you mind if I record this so I can share it with you afterward?"

**3:00-10:00 — Discovery**
This is the most important part. Do not skip it.

Ask questions in this order:

**Problem questions:**
- "What prompted you to look for a solution now?" (Find the trigger event)
- "Walk me through how you're currently handling [problem]."
- "What's working well with your current approach? What's not?"
- "What happens if this problem doesn't get solved?" (Find the pain magnitude)

**Decision process questions:**
- "Who else is involved in making this decision?"
- "Have you evaluated any other solutions?"
- "What criteria will you use to make your final decision?"
- "What's your timeline for making a decision?"

**Success criteria questions:**
- "If we were having this conversation 3 months from now and everything went perfectly, what would have changed?"
- "How will you measure the success of this investment?"
- "What would need to be true for you to feel confident moving forward?"

**Take notes during this phase.** Type what they say. It shows you're listening and gives you material for later.

**10:00-25:00 — The Demo**
Now you show the product. But not randomly.

**The solo founder's demo method:**

1. **Paint the before picture:** "You mentioned that [problem they described] is taking your team [X hours]. Let me show you how our product addresses that."

2. **Show the feature, not the interface:** Don't click through menus. Show the outcome. Use keyboard shortcuts, pre-prepared states, or even a recording of the ideal flow if live demo is risky.

3. **Bridge every feature to their pain:** "You said [specific pain]. This feature solves that by [mechanism]."

4. **Pause and check:** After each major point, ask "Does that make sense?" or "How would that work for your team?"

5. **Let them drive (if possible):** "Would you like to try this yourself? I can give you control of the screen." (Use tools like Whereby or Zoom's remote control)

6. **Show the workflow, not the feature list:** Demonstrate how their specific process flows through your product from start to finish.

7. **Handle questions gracefully:** If they ask about a feature you don't have, say "That's not something we have today, but here's how our customers are solving that..." Don't promise features you haven't built.

8. **Bring energy:** Your product is amazing. Your enthusiasm should be contagious. If you sound bored, they'll feel bored.

**The demo flow example:**
- "You mentioned onboarding takes 2 weeks. Let me show you how we reduce that to 2 days." (3 min)
- "You said reporting is a pain point. Here's our analytics dashboard." (2 min)
- "You mentioned you need to collaborate with your team. Let me show you how sharing works." (2 min)
- "And finally, you asked about security. Here's our audit log and permission settings." (2 min)
- Total: ~9 minutes of demo, then open for questions

**Warning signs during demo:**
- They go silent → pause and ask "What are you thinking?"
- They check their phone/watch → you're losing them, speed up or pivot
- They ask about competitor features → address head-on, acknowledge strengths
- They ask "can it do X?" repeatedly → they may not be a good fit

**25:00-30:00 — Next Steps**
The most commonly blown segment. Don't end with "let me know if you have questions."

**The Close:**
- Summarize: "Based on what you've shared, it sounds like [problem 1], [problem 2], and [problem 3] are your priorities. Our solution addresses each of these through [feature 1], [feature 2], and [feature 3]. Is that accurate?"
- Ask for commitment: "What would need to happen for us to move forward together?"
- Present the next step specifically:
  - If they're ready: "Great, I'll send over a proposal. Can I send that to you by [time]?"
  - If they need internal buy-in: "Who else needs to see this? Can we schedule a call with them?"
  - If they need time: "What timeline are you looking at? I'll follow up on [date]."
- Offer social proof: "Before we go, let me share a quick story about [similar company] who was in your exact situation..."

**If they're not ready:**
- "What's holding you back from moving forward?"
- Listen to the objection (covered in the next file)
- Set a specific follow-up date: "I'll circle back on [date]. In the meantime, I'll send over that case study we discussed."

## Discovery Questions by Category

### Technical Discovery (for technical buyers)
- "What does your current tech stack look like?"
- "How are you handling [technical challenge] today?"
- "What integration requirements do you have?"
- "What are your security and compliance requirements?"
- "How do you handle authentication and access control currently?"
- "What's your deployment environment like?"

### Business Discovery (for business buyers)
- "What's the business impact of [problem]?"
- "How are you measuring [relevant metric] today?"
- "What's the cost of the current solution (time, money, missed opportunities)?"
- "What ROI would justify this investment?"
- "How would success with our solution impact your team's goals?"

### Personal Discovery (for understanding motivation)
- "What made you personally interested in solving this problem?"
- "What keeps you up at night about [problem area]?"
- "Have you tried solving this before? What happened?"
- "If money weren't a concern, what would the ideal solution look like?"
- "What would your boss say if you told them about our solution?"

## Handling the Technical Demo

As a solo founder, you can (and should) go deep technically. This is a superpower.

### When Demo-ing to Technical Audiences:
- Show the API documentation
- Demonstrate webhook integrations
- Show code samples
- Talk about architecture decisions you made
- Be transparent about limitations
- Discuss your data model and how it maps to their domain
- Offer to do a technical POC

### Example Technical Demo Flow:
"Let me show you how our API handles this. I'll create a quick integration in our sandbox environment. You'll see how the authentication works, how webhooks fire, and how you can extend this with your custom logic."

Keep code samples clean and pre-prepared. Don't type code live unless you're 100% confident.

## Demo Call Objections (Quick Responses)

These deserve a full file (see 01-sales-process/03-objection-handling-saas.md), but here are quick responses:

**"It's too expensive."**
"I understand. What budget did you have in mind? Let me see what we can do. Also, consider that [current solution] costs [X], and our customers typically save [Y] by switching."

**"We're not ready yet."**
"What specifically needs to happen before you're ready? Is it budget, timeline, or convincing others?"

**"We're happy with [competitor]."**
"That's great. What do you like about them? And what's one thing you wish they did better?" (Pro tip: every unhappy customer of a competitor starts as a happy customer)

**"We need to think about it."**
"I completely understand. To help you think about it, what questions are still on your mind? And when should I follow up with you?"

**"Can you add [feature]?"**
"That's an interesting request. Right now our focus is on [core value prop]. If that's a deal-breaker, I understand, but here's how some of our customers work around that..."

## Post-Demo Follow-Up

### Immediate (within 2 hours):
- Send the recording of the call
- Thank-you note with key takeaways
- Link to relevant documentation
- Case study or social proof relevant to their use case

### Within 24 hours:
- Answer any questions that came up
- Send the proposal if discussed
- Share a relevant blog post or resource
- Connect on LinkedIn (if appropriate)

### Within 1 week:
- Check in: "Any questions on the proposal?"
- Offer an extended trial or POC
- Invite to a webinar or event
- Share customer success story

## Common Solo Demo Mistakes

### Mistake 1: Demo-ing Before Discovering
You show features before understanding their problem. Result: they don't see the relevance.

Fix: Spend the first third of the call in discovery mode only. No product. Just questions.

### Mistake 2: The Feature Dump
You try to show every feature. Result: information overload, no retention.

Fix: Show only the 3-5 features that directly address their stated needs. Leave them wanting more.

### Mistake 3: Talking Too Much
You're excited about your product. You talk for 20 minutes straight.

Fix: After every 2-3 minutes of demo, ask a question. "How does that compare to your current process?" "Would that help your team?"

### Mistake 4: Ignoring the Silent Participant
If multiple people are on the call, one may say nothing.

Fix: "Jane, I've heard from Tom about the reporting needs. From your perspective as [role], what would success look like?"

### Mistake 5: Ending Without Next Steps
The call ends with "I'll send you more info" and nothing concrete.

Fix: Always end with a specific next step and a date. "I'll send the proposal by Thursday. Can we connect again next Tuesday to discuss?"

### Mistake 6: Over-Promising
You promise features that don't exist to close the deal.

Fix: Be honest about limitations. "That's not something we have yet, but here's what our customers do today." Under-promise and over-deliver.

### Mistake 7: Not Handling the Evaluation Team
Enterprise prospects may have 5+ stakeholders.

Fix: Offer to do a group demo. Provide a self-service evaluation guide. Make it easy for them to share with their team.

## Remote Demo Best Practices

### Video and Audio Quality
- Use a good microphone (USB condenser mic, not built-in laptop mic)
- Have good lighting (window in front of you, not behind you)
- Look at the camera, not the screen
- Use a neutral background or virtual background
- Mute notifications

### Screen Sharing
- Close all tabs except what you need
- Use a clean browser with bookmarks for quick navigation
- Zoom in on important elements (many viewers are on mobile/tablet)
- Use cursor emphasis features
- Pre-type any URLs they'll need

### Engagement Techniques
- Use their name frequently
- Ask questions every 3-5 minutes
- Use "Let's look at what [Company Name]'s experience would look like"
- Share customer stories as analogies
- Use the chat feature to drop links as you talk
- Use reactions and gestures

## Scaling Demos as a Solo Founder

### When You Have Too Many Demo Requests

**Triage:**
- High-value prospects: Personal live demo (you)
- Mid-value prospects: Pre-recorded personalized demo (Loom)
- Low-value prospects: Automated self-service demo (product tour tool)

**Create a demo library:**
- 3-minute product overview
- 5-minute deep dive on each major feature
- 10-minute "complete workflow" for each use case
- Customer-specific demos can be assembled from library segments

**Use demo automation tools:**
- Navattic or Walnut for interactive product demos
- Loom for personalized video demos
- Storylane for guided product tours

**Build a self-serve demo experience:**
- Create a sandbox environment with sample data
- Build guided walkthroughs that prospects can navigate independently
- Add a "Book a demo with founder" CTA for those who need human help

## Conclusion

The demo call is where your solo founder advantage shines. You can answer any question, promise (realistic) features, and close on the spot. But only if you prepare, discover before you demo, and always — always — ask for the next step.

Your demo call is not about showing buttons and menus. It's about showing a prospect their future state where their problem is solved. Make them feel that future. Then help them buy it.
